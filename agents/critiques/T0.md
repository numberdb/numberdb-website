# T0 critique: "Zero"

Read 2026-09-21 as a reader meeting the page, not as its author. T0 holds one
entry, the exact integer `0`, with no parameters; type `Z`, rigour `exact`,
`complete: yes`. The whole table is 857 bytes as `/bundle/T0` serves it.

**The table reads well. I would change one thing, and it is a phrase in
`Similar tables`.** Everything else I found is either already settled, not the
table's to fix, or noted because I noticed it and not because it is worth an
edit.

**This table was edited an hour before I read it.** `T0/README.txt` in the
bundle says "as of 2026-09-21 22:03, by zeta3", and `agents/critiques/T0-growth.md`
plus its `-repaired` note record what that edit was: `complete: yes` with the

`additive identity` added to `Keywords`. I read the repaired table. Those

## 1. `Similar tables` says "integers — contained in", which is written from the wrong side

What a reader sees, in full, under `Similar tables`:

    integers —   contained in

The em dash is the site's own punctuation between a table and the relation
glossing it; the words after it are the author's. Everywhere else in the
corpus that gloss has the **linked table** as its subject, so it completes the
sentence "this table is …" or "that table …":

    its derivative
    the same knots
    the factor $B_{2m}$, and $\zeta(1-2m)=-B_{2m}/2m$
    holds $\kappa_{-4}=\pi/4$ as its entry $a=1/4$
    the nodes are the roots of $P_n$

Here the subject is *this* table's number instead, and the linked table is the
object: a reader has to supply "[the number here is] contained in [the]
integers", and the sentence as printed asserts the reverse — that the integers
are contained in something not named. It is recoverable, because
$0\in\mathbb Z$ is not in doubt. It still makes the reader do the work, and it
is the only shape in the corpus that asks for it: over the 195 tables the walk
reached, exactly three relations are written this way, and they are T0, T1 and
T42 — the three hand-made single-integer tables, which share the phrase
because they share an author and a morning.

Smallest change, one field, no other edit:

    Similar tables:
    - relation: holds every integer, this one as its entry $n=0$
      table: HREF{Integers}[integers]

That is true of T2 as it stands (it runs $-10\leq n\leq10$ and its $n=0$ row
is the number here), it puts the linked table in the subject where the rest of
the corpus puts it, and it tells a reader what they will find when they follow
the link. **Worth doing.** If it is done, T1 and T42 want the same sentence
with "multiplicative identity" and 42 in it, and doing T0 alone leaves the
phrase in the corpus twice.

## 2. The audit's one finding: right in form, and I would not act on it

    Definition links to another table; a cross-reference belongs in
    Similar tables or a comment

The rule is a flat "no `HREF{` in `Definition`". The definition here is

> The neutral element of addition in the ring of **integers**.

with the last word linking T2. I agree with the growth review's verdict and
would leave it, and I can now put a number on why the audit fires: of 100
tables sampled across T0–T59 and T200–T239, **three** have an `HREF{` in the
definition, and they are T0, T1 and T30. So the audit is describing real
corpus practice, not inventing a rule — but the practice it describes grew up
after these tables, and the skill's own instruction cuts the other way: "Link
the first mention of a thing to what explains it. A table if the corpus holds
one." This is the first and only mention of an object the corpus holds, the
sentence means the whole ring rather than one row, and the link now points at
the table and not at `Integers#0` — which is the part that *was* wrong and was
fixed at 22:03.

The honest summary is that a reader is served identically either way: remove
the link and "integers" is still one line below in `Similar tables`. The
smallest change that would make the audit clean is to drop the `HREF{}`
wrapper and keep the words, which changes the rendered page by one colour. I
would spend the edit on §1 instead.

## 3. A reader who types the table's own name gets it fourteenth — and this is not the table's to fix

`numberdb.search_text('zero')` returns 20 tables. "Zero" is number **14**,
behind thirteen tables called "Zeros of the …":

     1  Zeros of the Dedekind zeta functions of cubic fields
     …
    13  Zeros of Bessel functions of the second kind $Y_\alpha$
    14  Zero

I measured why, because it decides whether the table can do anything about it.
The ranking is `SearchRank(F('search_vector'), query)` with no normalisation
(`numberdb_app/search.py:679`), which is Postgres `ts_rank` at normalisation
0: the score rises with the **number of weighted occurrences** of the query's
lexemes and is never divided by the length of the document. Counting
occurrences of the stem in each returned table's title and keywords (weight A),
tags (B), definition (C) and comments (D), and weighting them 1, 0.4, 0.2,
0.1, reproduces the order the site returned almost exactly — 7.0, 5.9, 4.2,
3.4, 3.1, 2.5, 2.1, 1.8, 1.8, 1.7, 1.6, 1.6, 1.6, then T0 at **1.0**, one
occurrence, its one-word title. A table whose whole name is the word is beaten
by every table that says the word in a title, a tag, a definition and nine
comments.

Nothing in the table fixes that. `Keywords` is weight A, so a keyword would
help only by repeating a word the title already carries, which is padding the
index rather than serving a reader. What the table *can* be judged on is
whether the reader arrives, and there it is fine — see §5. I have written the
ranking up as a lesson proposal (§6), because choosing a title is a decision
the skill tells a contributor to make on search grounds and this is a fact
about search that no contributor can guess.

## 4. Noted only, and I would edit none of them

- **`Programs` is `numbers = [0]`.** On most tables a literal rather than an
  incantation would be a finding: `Programs` is for a reader who wants one more
  value. Here there is no next value, and the line is honest about that. The
  three sibling tables read the same way (`numbers = [1]`, `numbers = [42]`,
  `numbers = [-10..10]`).
- **`rigour details` is the house sentence for exact tables**, and it contains
  two `--` where the skill asks for a comma or a full stop: "An exact value --
  an integer, a rational or a polynomial -- so there is no precision to choose
  and nothing to be wrong about." It reaches the page as two hyphens. It is
  **not this table's text**: it is written by
  `numberdb_app/management/commands/set_rigour.py:33` and was applied in the
  2026-08 rigour migration, and 31 of the 70 `exact` tables the walk reached
  carry it verbatim. A fix belongs in that command, once, not in T0.
- **One tag, `integer`**, where two is typical. It reaches T1 and T42, so it
  is a way through the corpus and not a tag of one table. T2's `ring` and
  `Abelian group` are true of the integers and not of a number, and inventing
  a tag to reach two would make the list worse.
- **The definition is a noun phrase, not a sentence.** "The neutral element of
  addition in the ring of integers." reads as a dictionary entry and is
  completed by the title above it; the corpus writes both this and the full
  sentence (T7: "$\pi$ is the area of the unit circle."). It reads; I would
  leave it.

## 5. What I checked hardest, and it held

*The page renders, and there is nothing in it that only the HTML shows.* No
`Math input error`, no `<` eating a section, no `argument ()` — and no
mathematics at all in the table's own fields, so nothing to typeset and
nothing to break. The `Parameters` block is absent, which is right: there are
none. The value column's header cell is empty and the section carries the
site's default heading `Number`, not the fallback word `value`. The definition
renders as `<a class="HREF" href="Integers">integers</a>`, and the relative
href resolves from both URLs this page is served at, `/T0` and `/Zero`; the
trailing-slash forms answer 404 rather than serving a page with broken links.

*A reader who arrives holding the number is served, in the first screen.*
`search_integer(0)` returns 52 tables holding an exact 0 and puts **Zero
first**, ahead of Integers; the page then says, above the fold, the title, the
number, and one sentence saying what it is. That is the question this database
exists to answer and T0 answers it. (A bare `0` typed into the search box also
returns T0, but for an unrelated reason — it is read as a table *number*,
`search.py:668` — and that coincidence is already written up in
`agents/lessons/proposals/20260921T215138Z-critique.md`. I confirmed it and am
not repeating it here.)

*The pairing with the integers table is in the right direction and renders.*
T2's row for $n=0$ carries `equals: HREF{Zero}`, which prints on `/Integers`
as "0  equals: Zero" and links back here. `equals` is the strong claim — the
same number, which search reads — and it is stored on the general table
pointing at the specific one, so search answers with Zero and names Integers
beside it. T0 correctly carries no `equals` of its own; adding one would say
the same thing twice in the weaker direction.

*It is one table.* One number, no parameter, no axis to run along, and a
uniqueness proof rather than a range: if $z$ and $z'$ are both additive
identities then $z=z+z'=z'$. The completeness clause finishes the rendered
sentence properly — "Table is complete: yes (it holds the single number named
by the definition)" — and says what the table covers rather than that it is
finished.

*Every claim on the page is a fact about the mathematics.* Nothing about how
distinctive the value is, how good a search hit would be, or what a reader
should conclude. Nothing points at something without naming it: there is no
"the former", no "as above", and the one phrase a reader has to complete for
themselves is the relation in §1.
