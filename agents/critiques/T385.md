# T385, "Zeros of the Dedekind zeta functions of cubic fields": read as a reader

Read on 2026-09-21. A private draft: 70 cubic fields, ten positive ordinates
each, 700 entries of type `R` at 30 significant digits, 93,529 bytes of
document, `heuristic (agreement-checked)`. Nothing was changed.

**The table is in good shape and its central structural fact is missing from
the page.** The census is right, the program runs verbatim and reproduces the
stored digits, every link answers, the page sets cleanly, and the audit is
clean and deserves to be. What a reader is not told is that
$\zeta_K(s)=\zeta(s)L(s,\rho)$ for every cubic $K$ — so nine rows of this table
*are* the first zero of the Riemann zeta function, and every row of the four
cyclic fields is a zero of a Dirichlet $L$-function this corpus already holds.
The page says a sixth of that, for one field, in a clause that points at the
field rather than naming it. That is findings 1 to 3, and they are one comment
and one decision between them. Findings 4 to 8 are an editing pass. Everything
after that I am noting only because I noticed.

## 2. Findings, ranked

### 1. The page never says that $\zeta(s)$ divides $\zeta_K(s)$, and nine rows are the first Riemann zero — worth doing

A reader arrives holding `14.1347251417346937904572519836`. This table
answers, nine times over: it is stored under $D=-23$, $-31$, $-44$, $49$, $81$
and four more, and it is the only value in the table that appears in more than
one row. It is the first zero of $\zeta(s)$, which
`Zeros_of_the_Riemann_zeta_function` holds to 31 digits with a ball. Nothing
on this page explains why it is here. The nearest thing is the `Similar
tables` relation "stores zero ordinates for **the degree-one factor**
$\zeta(s)$" — a phrase that points at a factorisation the page has never
stated, in the one section a reader reads last.

The fact is one sentence: for any cubic field $K$, $\zeta_K(s)=\zeta(s)L(s,\rho)$
with $L(s,\rho)$ the Artin $L$-function of the two-dimensional representation
($\rho$ reducible into the two cubic characters exactly when $K$ is cyclic,
which is what comment (6) already says the other half of). So every zero of
$\zeta$ is a zero of every $\zeta_K$, and a reader holding 14.1347… has learned
something true rather than met a coincidence.

This is not a `repeats:` case and the table is right not to declare one:
`numberdb_app/models.py` reserves `repeats` for a value transcribed from
another table, and says of the other case — two tables agreeing because of a
theorem — that it is "most of what a database of constants is for" and must
not be folded. So the fix is not a field, it is a comment.

**Smallest change:** one comment stating the factorisation for both Galois
types, and one clause in the entry comment (or in that same comment) saying
that the ordinate 14.1347… in these rows is the first zero of $\zeta(s)$,
linking `HREF{Zeros_of_the_Riemann_zeta_function#1}`. T159's `comment-galois`
already writes the cyclic half of this sentence and is a model for it.

### 2. The cross-references to T4 are there for one field and not for the next — worth doing

Comment (6) says the entry comments for the conductor-7 field identify the
corresponding rows of the Dirichlet zero table, and six of the ten rows under
$D=49$ carry `Also T4 $(7,2,1)$` and its five siblings. I checked all six
against T4: every one resolves, and every value agrees to the last stored
place. That is good work. The trouble is what it teaches a reader about the
rows that carry nothing:

| T385 row | value | also in T4 as | says so? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 49,1,7 | 12.5325478226862740080723048004 | 7,2,4 | no |
| 49,1,8 | 13.8298678998613675706123680948 | 7,4,4 | no |
| 49,1,9 | 14.1347251417346937904572519836 | (the first Riemann zero) | no |
| 49,1,10 | 15.9374482046879595568895739989 | 7,2,5 | no |
| 81,1,1…10 | all ten | 9,7,k and 9,4,k, and the Riemann zero | none of them |

So for $D=49$ the page identifies six of the ten and is silent about four that
are equally identified; and for $D=81$, whose two cubic characters are in T4
as `9,4` and `9,7`, it identifies none of the ten. A reader comparing two
rows concludes the silent ones are zeros of something else. They are not.

Finish it or drop it: complete the cross-references for $D=49$ and $D=81$
(T4 has no characters of conductor 13 or 19, so $D=169$ and $D=361$ cannot be
done, and that is worth one clause), or drop the per-row links altogether and
let the comment of finding 1 carry it for the whole table. Both are defensible;
what is not is six out of twenty.

### 3. "the conductor-$7$ field" points at a row instead of naming it — worth doing

Comment (6) sends a reader to "the conductor-$7$ field". The table's
coordinates are $D$ and $k$; nothing on the page says that $D$ is a square
exactly for the cyclic fields and that $\sqrt D$ is then the conductor, so a
reader has to know that, work out $49$, and go looking. T159's
`comment-galois` states the rule in a sentence and then lists the conductors.
**Smallest change:** write "$D=49$" and, if the conductor is wanted, say
"$D=49$, the cyclic cubic field of conductor $\sqrt D=7$".

### 4. The Galois group is written `C3` and `S3` — worth doing

Comment (3) says the group is given "as $C3$ or $S3$", which MathJax sets as
an italic *C3*; the 700 entry comments write `C3` and `S3` as bare text, in no
math mode at all. The group is $C_3$ and $S_3$, and T159 — the table this one
says it follows — writes `$S_3$` in its prose and `$S_3$` in every entry
comment. Two characters per occurrence.

### 5. Comment (3) describes entry comments the table does not have — worth doing

It reads: "Each entry comment gives the reduced defining polynomial $f$, so
that $K=\mathbb Q(a)$ with $f(a)=0$". The entry comments give neither $f$ nor
$a$: they print `$x^3-x^2+1=0$`, an equation in $x$. T159's sentence is almost
word for word the same, and its entries print `$a^3-a^2+1=0$`, so its $a$ has a
referent. **Smallest change:** write the entry polynomials in $a$ as the
sibling does, which also makes the two tables' rows read alike; or, if $x$ is
wanted, reword the comment to "a defining equation $f(x)=0$, so that
$K=\mathbb{Q}(a)$ for a root $a$ of it".

### 6. The $k$ column is `1` on all 700 rows and the page never says why — worth doing

$k$ is right as a parameter: cubic fields do share discriminants, the sibling
tables are indexed this way, and a citation to a row of this table has to
resolve for a future $k=2$. But a reader scrolling 700 rows of a column that
never changes cannot tell whether that is a fact or a stub. It is a fact, and
T159 states the analogous one: "Every other negative $D$ here, and every
positive one, carries a single field, with $k=1$", together with the list of
the seven discriminants below 3000 that carry more. The smallest of those is
$|D|=972$. **Smallest change:** one clause in `comment-index` or in
`complete-note`: "no two cubic fields with $|D|\leq500$ share a discriminant,
so $k=1$ throughout".

### 7. `Links[Request72]`, "Requested in numberdb-data#72" — worth doing

The skill is explicit: a "table wanted" issue is answered in the issue, "the
table carries no trace of the request: it is encyclopedic, and who asked for
it is not a fact about the mathematics." That alone settles it, and this
particular link is worse than the general case. Issue #72 is titled "More
zeros of Dirichlet zeta functions" and its body reads, whole: "Maybe make
https://www.numberdb.org/Zeros_of_Dirichlet_L_functions into table of tables."
It asks for a restructuring of T4, not for this family; and it had already been
closed this morning by T380, a table of arithmetic factors in moments of
quadratic $L$-functions, which is a third thing again. A reader who follows
`[7]` to find out what this table is for reads a one-line note about a
different table. The issue comment answering it was posted, which is where the
answer belongs. **Smallest change:** delete the link.

### 8. `rigour details` describes a mechanism whose output would be ragged, and every value is 30 digits — worth doing

"It stores only digits that agree between those two computations" reads as: the
stored precision is whatever survived agreement, value by value. All 700 stored
values have exactly 30 significant digits. A reader who believes the sentence
concludes that agreement failed at the thirtieth digit 700 times, which is not
plausible and not what happened: 30 is the table's `digits`, and the agreement
check is a floor under it, not the thing that set it. **Smallest change:**
"each value is stored to 30 significant digits, and the two computations agree
to at least that many".

### 9. The definition makes $n$ an absolute index; comment (5) allows a zero to be missed — worth doing, one clause

The definition says the positive ordinates are "ordered increasingly from
$n=1$ for each field", so $n$ counts every critical-line zero. Comment (5)
says, honestly, that PARI's search "is not a certified enclosure method and may
miss zeros". Both cannot be relied on at once: a missed zero does not only
leave a gap, it shifts every $n$ above it, so the parameter that identifies a
row is the thing at risk. Say the consequence where the caveat is: "so a
missed zero would shift the index of every later one in that field".

### 10. Where the LMFDB labels came from is not said, and the trailing digit is not $k$ — worth doing, one clause

Every entry comment ends in a label like `3.1.23.1`; `rigour details` accounts
for the zeros and the field census and says nothing about the labels. It
matters because of a trap the sibling table documents: T159's `comment-index`
says "The LMFDB's own index for fields sharing a discriminant is not this
order (for $D=-2891$ its first field is $k=3$ here)". In this range every
discriminant carries one field, so the last digit is 1 everywhere and nothing
can go wrong; the next person to extend the table past $|D|=500$ walks into it.
One clause saying where the labels came from, and that the last component is
the LMFDB's index and not $k$, costs nothing now and saves that.

While there: the label is the most citable thing in each row and is not
clickable, whereas the two LMFDB links that *are* on the page go to listings of
every cubic field of a signature and are cited from nowhere.
`https://www.lmfdb.org/NumberField/3.3.49.1` answers 200; saying in comment (3)
that a label resolves under `lmfdb.org/NumberField/` gives a reader the click.

### 11. $r_1$ and $r_2$ are used in Formula (1) and explained after it — worth doing, one clause

The completed zeta function is correct as written — I checked it against the
standard $\Lambda_K(s)=|D|^{s/2}\Gamma_{\mathbb R}(s)^{r_1}\Gamma_{\mathbb
C}(s)^{r_2}\zeta_K(s)$, and the functional equation with it. But $r_1$ and
$r_2$ appear there with no gloss, and the page draws Formulas before Comments,
so their only explanation ("a cubic field with signature $(r_1,r_2)$", comment
(5)) is below. The signature is also in all 700 entry comments with no
statement of which coordinate is which. "where $(r_1,r_2)$ is the signature of
$K$: $r_1$ real places and $r_2$ complex ones" in the formula settles both.

## 3. Noted only because I noticed

- **Comment (5)'s list of trivial zeros omits $s=0$.** $\zeta_K$ vanishes there
  to order $r_1+r_2-1$, which is 2 for a totally real cubic field and 1 for a
  complex one. The comment is enumerating where $\zeta_K$ vanishes off the
  critical line, so the omission is visible to a reader who checks it; nothing
  on the page becomes wrong.
- **The `Similar tables` caption renames T4.** It is captioned "Zeros of
  Dirichlet $L$-functions"; the table's title is "Zeros of Dirichlet
  L-series". The caption is the better phrase and the slug resolves, but a
  reader lands on a page with a different name.
- **`Also T4 $(7,2,1)$`** names a table by its number and a row by its raw
  coordinates. It is compact, it resolves, and it tells a reader nothing about
  what is at the other end.
- **The entry comments are 38,738 characters against 21,700 of values**, and
  76 distinct texts printed 700 times: the polynomial, signature, group and
  label of a field are repeated on each of its ten rows. This is well inside
  every limit (the block is about 59 KB against a 320 KB soft limit), the skill
  names "the field a discriminant names" as exactly what an entry comment is
  for, and the table has no way to attach a note to a $D,k$ block. So there is
  nothing to do unless the repetition on screen bothers a reviewer more than it
  bothered me.
- **Parameter $D$'s constraint restates its title.** The line sets as
  "$D$ — discriminant of $K$ ($D$ the discriminant of a cubic field)".
- **The ordering rule is stated twice**, in the definition ("ordered
  increasingly from $n=1$ for each field") and in parameter $n$'s comment.
- **`rigour details` quotes a stored value to 30 digits** (the tenth ordinate
  of $D=-23$) where it is evidence for the choice of range; three digits would
  carry the same argument.
- **The sorting rule in comment (2) does not say which end of the coefficient
  vector leads.** "sorting the reduced polynomials lexicographically by
  coefficient vector" — T159 says $(a_2,a_1,a_0)$. It cannot bite in this
  range, since $k=1$ throughout, and it is the rule the next person extends by.

## 4. What I checked hardest and found nothing wrong with

- **The census.** `complete-note` claims every cubic field with $|D|\leq500$,
  58 complex and 12 totally real. The document holds exactly 70 fields, 58 with
  $D<0$ and 12 with $D>0$, and the set of $(D,k)$ pairs is **exactly** T159's
  515 fields restricted to $|D|\leq500$ — same discriminants, same $k$, no
  field in one and not the other. LMFDB would have been the independent check;
  it answered one search page and then a reCAPTCHA challenge two minutes later,
  as `docs/agent-environment.md` warns, so the census is confirmed against a
  published table of this corpus rather than against LMFDB.
- **The program.** Run verbatim in the container's `gp` — the three lines
  exactly as the page prints them — it answers
  `5.1156833288151175985533564203781…`, which is the stored value of row
  $(-23,1,1)$ to the last place, and the tenth entry of the vector is
  `17.101263303581831551948723444…`, the stored $(-23,1,10)$. The same run
  reports 67 zeros below height 60 for that field, against the ten stored,
  which is the margin `rigour details` claims. `Programs` here does what
  `Programs` is for.
- **The six cross-references.** Anchors exist on T4, values agree to the last
  place in all six, and the two conductor-9 characters were checked as well —
  which is how finding 2 was found.
- **The links.** All seven answer 200, including both LMFDB listings and the
  two PARI manual pages. Note that the audit taken through the API runs with
  `fetch=False`, so it did *not* check these; I did.
- **The rendering**, listed in §1.

## 5. Is it one table?

Yes. $D$, $k$ and $n$ are all numbers, all three index one quantity, the value
column heads `$t_n$` rather than the word `value`, and no parameter takes a
handful of names that the others repeat under. A reader holding an ordinate
wants exactly what the row gives them.

## 6. Does the definition define?

"Let $K$ be a cubic field of discriminant $D$, with Dedekind zeta function
$\zeta_K(s)$. Listed are the positive ordinates $t_n$ of critical-line zeros
$\zeta_K(1/2+\mathrm{i}t_n)=0$, ordered increasingly from $n=1$ for each
field." Two sentences, 257 characters against a corpus median of 195, no
caveat, no history, no range. It says
what the object is and stops, and two people would build the same table from
it — up to finding 9's wrinkle about what "ordered increasingly from $n=1$"
requires of the search, and up to $k$, which the definition leaves to
`comment-index`, correctly.

## 7. The parameters describe the family, not the run

$D$ is the discriminant of a cubic field, $k\geq1$, $n\geq1$ — the family. What
was computed is in `complete: no` with a note that finishes the sentence. That
is the right split and it is worth saying so, because it is the split the skill
says goes wrong most often.

## 8. The audit

`{"findings": [], "clean": true}`, and I agree with it: I found nothing it
should have caught and did not. Two things it could not see, neither of which
is its fault:

- **The overlap check could not reach the shared rows.**
  `_values_also_in_another_table` samples eight rows from the first 320, at a
  stride of 40. This table's blocks are ten rows deep, so 40 is a multiple of
  the period and every sampled row is an $n=1$ row — rows 1, 41, 81, …, 281,
  which are the first zeros of fields 1, 5, 9, 13, 17, 21, 25 and 29. The
  cyclic fields, whose rows are the ones T4 also holds, are fields 4, 7, 19 and
  49. It would have been suppressed anyway, since the check skips a table that
  already names the other in prose and T4 is in `Similar tables`. Both
  behaviours are right in general; together they mean a clean audit says
  nothing about finding 2.
- **Links were not fetched.** `GET /api/table/<tid>/audit` calls
  `findings_for(table)` with `fetch=False`, so the seven URLs were not tried.
  They all answer 200; I checked by hand.

One thing I could not settle either way: whether a generator is attached. A
draft's `/files/` page needs a session, and the API document carries no
manifest. The audit does check for an attached script, but only for a table
whose revisions record `numberdb=` in `produced_by`, so a clean result is
evidence and not proof. Worth a glance from somebody who can open
`/files/T385`.

## 9. Would a reader holding one of these numbers be served?

For 68 of the 70 fields, yes, and well: the row names the field by its defining
polynomial, its signature, its Galois group and its LMFDB label, and the
anchor is citable. For the nine rows holding 14.1347…, and for the twenty rows
of the two cyclic fields, every one of which is written down in another table
here, the page hands a reader a
number it shares with another table and says nothing about why. That is
finding 1, and it is the only thing here I would hold the table for.

(The twenty rows are the two cyclic fields $D=49$ and $D=81$: eighteen of them
are rows of T4, and the tenth ordinate of each is the first Riemann zero.)
