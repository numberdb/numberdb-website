# T239 critique: Matching polynomials of connected graphs

Read 2026-09-13. A private draft owned by zeta3, offered for review. It has
1992 entries: two forms for each of the 996 connected graphs on at most 7
vertices. The type is `Z[]` and the rigour is `exact`.

The table reads well. It renders cleanly, the 35 graph names match T237's
under the same keys, and every claim about the stored rows is true. This
time the graph6 key is explained on the page, which is what T237 and T238
were missing. What a reader will notice is smaller:

- one comment says the two forms are equivalent, and another gives them
  different numbers of distinct values without saying why;
- the Programs snippet enumerates matchings by hand, though Sage has a
  method for it, and the table's own `rigour details` names that method;
- three keywords are never explained on the page.

Findings 1 and 2 are worth doing. 3 to 5 are small and worth doing while the
file is open. 6 and 7 are smaller still. The rest are notes. One fault a
reader will see belongs to the site, not the table, and it comes first.

## A fault on the page that is not the table's

**"Size exception: ... (Unknown key)".** The page prints the size exception
under Data properties, followed by "(Unknown key)". The help page
(`templates/help.html`, "How much to record") and `limits.EXCEPTION_KEY` both
say to write exactly this key. But `property_names` in
`numberdb_app/views.py` (`table_context`, around line 1063) does not list
it, so the renderer falls through to its unknown-key branch. The table page
and `/preview` share that code. Every table that follows the help will look
as if it misspelt a field. **The fix is in the site:** add
`'Size exception': 'Size exception'` to `property_names`. The table should
not change. I have recorded this in `docs/agent-environment.md`.

## Findings, ranked

### 1. The forms are called equivalent, then counted differently, and nothing says why (worth doing)

**What a reader sees.** Two comments, one after the other:

> (3) The two forms are equivalent by formula (1), but they are both standard
> one-variable conventions. The bivariate matching polynomial carries the
> same coefficients with an extra homogenising variable and is not stored
> separately.
>
> (5) The 996 graphs here have 434 distinct signed matching polynomials and
> 379 distinct matching-generating polynomials.

**Why it is wrong.** If the forms were equivalent, they would have the same
number of distinct values. They are equivalent *given $n$*: formula (1)
multiplies by $x^n$, and $M(G,x)$ does not record $n$. $K_3$ and $K_{1,3}$
both have $M=3x+1$, and 51 of the 379 classes mix vertex counts that way.
That is exactly what a reader holding an $M$ needs to know, since the
search will offer graphs of different sizes. The rest of comment (3) is not
about the mathematics:

- "but they are both standard" answers the question of why the table stores
  both. `Size exception` already answers that.
- "The bivariate matching polynomial" names an object the page never
  defines.
- "is not stored separately" is about the table.

**Smallest change.** Rewrite `comment-forms` as: "The two forms carry the
same counts $m_k(G)$, and for a graph with $n$ vertices each determines the
other by formula CITE{formula-forms}. $M(G,x)$ does not record $n$, so graphs
of different sizes can share it: $K_3$ and $K_{1,3}$ both have
$M(G,x)=3x+1$." Then either delete the bivariate sentence, or define the
polynomial, as Wikipedia does:
$\mu(G;x,y)=\sum_k m_k(G)x^ky^{n-2k}$. That comment then explains the two
counts in `comment-shared-values`, which can stay as it is.

### 2. Programs writes out an enumeration where Sage has a method (worth doing)

**What a reader sees.** Programs holds a 21-line snippet that imports
`itertools.combinations`, builds every set of $k$ edges, and keeps those
whose endpoints are distinct. It ends with two assignments and prints
nothing. Meanwhile `rigour details` says "Sage's `matching_polynomial()`
agreed with the signed form on every row".

**Why it is wrong.** Programs is "the standard incantation for a reader who
wants one more value". In Sage that incantation is
`Graph(name).matching_polynomial()`, and the table already knows it. The
enumeration is fine for 7 vertices, but it is exponential in the number of
edges. A reader who copies it for a 12-vertex graph will wait, and will not
know that Sage had the answer.

**Smallest change.** Replace the code with the following. I ran it in the
Sage image, where it prints both stored rows of `EIe_`. The same derivation
of $M$ from `matching_polynomial()` agrees with all 996 stored rows.

```python
import numberdb.sage as numberdb
from sage.graphs.graph import Graph

G = Graph('EIe_')                  # C_6, by its name here
mu = G.matching_polynomial()       # x^6 - 6*x^4 + 9*x^2 - 2
x = mu.parent().gen()
n = G.num_verts()
M = sum(abs(mu[n - 2*k]) * x**k for k in range(n // 2 + 1))
print(mu, M)                       # M = 2*x^3 + 9*x^2 + 6*x + 1
```

### 3. Three keywords are names the page never mentions (worth doing, small)

**What a reader sees.** A search for "Hosoya index", "acyclic polynomial" or
"reference polynomial" reaches this table through `Keywords`. Nothing on the
page contains any of those words.

**Why it is wrong.** The reader who typed them now has to guess how their
term relates to $\mu$ and $M$. A chemist who typed "Hosoya index" is holding
an integer, and the page never says it is $M(G,1)$.

**Smallest change.** Add one comment: "$\mu(G,x)$ is also called the acyclic
polynomial, the reference polynomial and the matching defect polynomial
CITE{MathWorldMatching}. $M(G,1)=\sum_k m_k(G)$, the number of matchings
of $G$, is its Hosoya index; for $K_7$ it is $232$." MathWorld gives all
three names, and 232 is the stored $M(K_7,x)$ at $x=1$. "Matching defect
polynomial" is worth adding to `Keywords` too (see the notes).

### 4. The relation to the lattice entropy constants is vague where a precise one exists (worth doing, small)

**What a reader sees.** "Entropy constants of lattice models: contains dimer
entropy constants for infinite lattices, the limiting analogue of matching
counts in finite graph models".

**Why it is wrong.** "Limiting analogue" and "finite graph models" do not
say what is counted. T153's dimer model counts *perfect* matchings, and
T239 stores that count directly: for $n$ even, the constant term of
$\mu(G,x)$ is $\pm m_{n/2}(G)$. For example, $\mu(K_4,0)=3$. Also, T153
holds one dimer constant per lattice, so "constants" in the plural reads as
a family.

**Smallest change.** Change the relation to: "its dimer model counts the
perfect matchings of lattice regions; a graph $G$ with an even number $n$ of
vertices has $|\mu(G,0)|=m_{n/2}(G)$ of them".

### 5. `comment-forest` names a family the corpus holds without linking it, and states half the theorem (worth doing, small)

**What a reader sees.** "For forests, the signed matching polynomial is the
adjacency characteristic polynomial $\det(xI-A(G))$."

**Why it is wrong.** T237 holds exactly those polynomials under the same keys.
This is the first mention in Comments, so it should be a link. The skill's
rule is first mention, once per section. The audit misses it because the
phrase differs from the title. The theorem also runs both ways: $\mu=\det(xI-A)$
holds *exactly* when $G$ is a forest, and on the stored rows it holds on the
25 trees and nowhere else. "Exactly when" tells a reader holding a match
between the two tables that their graph is a tree. "For forests" does not.

**Smallest change.** "The signed matching polynomial equals the
HREF{Characteristic_polynomials_of_connected_graphs}[characteristic
polynomial] $\det(xI-A(G))$ of the adjacency matrix exactly when $G$ is a
forest." Say "when $G$ is a tree" instead if you prefer the word that fits a
table of connected graphs. Make the same change in the Similar tables
relation, which says "when $G$ is a forest".

### 6. The second parameter reads "form — form" and lists keys the page never shows (small)

**What a reader sees.** Under Parameters: "form — form (either signed or
generating)", with the two words in code type. The rows are labelled
$\mu(G,x)$ and $M(G,x)$, and "signed" and "generating" appear nowhere else a
reader looks.

**Why it is wrong.** The site supplies the dash between the display and the
title, and here both are "form". The constraint gives the address keys, not
the objects.

**Smallest change.** Make the title "polynomial" (or "form of the matching
polynomial"). Make the constraint "the signed matching polynomial
$\mu(G,x)$, keyed `signed`, or the matching-generating polynomial $M(G,x)$,
keyed `generating`".

### 7. `rigour details` tells the story of the build (small)

**What a reader sees.** "Before the draft was filled, the build checked that
the 996 graph6 strings were exactly the keys of ..." and "The exactness check
found no inexact coefficients".

**Why it is wrong.** `rigour details` is the right place for what was
checked, but a reader of a published table does not know what a draft is, or
that it was filled. And a coefficient that counts matchings cannot be
inexact, so that sentence reports a tool's check rather than a fact.

**Smallest change.** Start the paragraph "The 996 graph6 strings are exactly
the keys of ...", and delete the exactness sentence.

## Noted only

- **The definition restricts to connected graphs.** It defines $m_k$ "for a
  connected simple graph", but the matching polynomial is defined for every
  graph, and connectedness is what the table holds. The same was noted for
  T238, and it is harmless here. "Let $m_0(G)=1$" is redundant but not
  wrong.
- **Each graph's name is printed twice.** With both forms stored, every
  graph shows its key and its comment on two rows, so "This is the Moser
  spindle." appears twice in a row. This is defensible, because a search
  lands on one row and that row should carry the name.
- **"Signed matching polynomial" is the table's own label.** MathWorld calls
  $\mu$ simply the matching polynomial. Wikipedia calls
  $\sum m_kx^k$ one of the matching polynomials. The label is a reasonable
  way to tell the two apart, and it is used consistently. MathWorld's
  "matching defect polynomial" (Lovász and Plummer) is missing from
  `Keywords`.
- **$I$ in formula (5) is named, not defined.** "$I$ is the independence
  polynomial" is enough for most readers, and the corpus does not hold it.
  "$I(H,x)=\sum_k i_k(H)x^k$, with $i_k(H)$ the number of independent sets of
  $k$ vertices" would make the formula stand alone.
- **$He_n$ typesets as the product $H\,e_n$.** This matches the linked table
  (T104), so it is a corpus convention rather than a fault here.
- **`comment-roots` could say one more thing.** Heilmann and Lieb's theorem
  also gives that $M(G,x)$ has only real negative zeros, which is the form a
  statistical-mechanics reader uses. This is a matter of taste.
- **T237 does not link back.** Its Similar tables do not mention T239. Both
  are drafts, and the audit allows two drafts to link to each other.
