# T313: Chern–Simons invariants of hyperbolic knot complements

Read on 2026-09-17, against the skill fetched that day from
<https://numberdb.org/skill> (47,534 bytes). T313 is a draft holding 932 `R`
entries: the Chern-Simons invariant of each of the 243 hyperbolic prime knots
with at most ten crossings, each of the 223 chiral ones also as its mirror
image, each in both the $\operatorname{CS}$ and the $2\pi^2\operatorname{CS}$
normalisation. `rigour: heuristic (agreement-checked)`, `complete: yes`.

The mathematics is in order, the range is complete and it is one table rather
than two. Two faults are worth a person's afternoon, and neither is visible in
the document as prose:

* **Forty of the 932 entries are marked on the page "This number will not be
  found in the search."** They are not approximate because the invariant is
  hard; they are approximate because digits the generator already had were
  thrown away. I recomputed all 28 of the short ones with SnapPy: every one is
  the nine-decimal value with its trailing zeros stripped. For $9_{41}$ the two
  rows of the same number now contradict each other.
* **The whole `Formulas` section renders as two Python dictionaries**, and the
  TeX inside them is typeset letter by letter as `o p e r a t o r n a m e`.
  Every other table in the corpus stores a formula as a string; this one stores
  a mapping.

  session, which is what a draft does. I read the document with the key
  (`/api/table?id=T313`), which serves `full_yaml` and so is the same document
  the site renders, and drew it through `/preview?table=` in eight pieces under
  the request-line limit (the longest URL that answered was 4,133 bytes). All
  eight returned 200 and drew their sections: Numbers, Definition, Parameters,
  Formulas, Comments, Similar tables, Links, References, Data properties. A
  piece must carry `Links` and `References` or every `CITE` in it renders as
  bare text; with both present, the four citations resolve to `[1]`, `[2]`,
  `[5]`, `[6]`. Section order on a published sibling is Numbers, Definition,
  Parameters, Formulas, Comments, Programs, References, Links, Similar tables,
  Data properties, so the numbers are the first screen and `Formulas` the
  third. One piece was drawn with the rows of $9_{41}$, $9_{49}$ and
  $10_{17}$ in it: eight of its ten entries came back carrying the
  not-findable dagger, which is exactly the eight my own count of the
  threshold predicts.
- **TeX.** 272 distinct `$...$` fragments, typeset with `mathjax-full` (base +
  ams) in node: 0 errors. A control of three fragments, one with a missing
  brace and one with an undefined macro, reported 2 errors.
- **Audit.** `GET /api/table/T313/audit`, with and without `links=1&fetch=1`,
  returns the same two findings, both the structural "N names, each carrying
  entries of its own" question, for `knot` and for `normalisation`. I agree it
  is right to ask, and I think both answers are the good kind; see "What reads
  well". Where I disagree with it is small and is finding 4: it asks for the
  answer to be *in the definition*, and in this table the answer is in the
  Comments. It cannot see the `Formulas` mapping, and it has no notion of the
  search threshold, so it says nothing about finding 1.
- **Recomputed, because the headline finding depends on it.** Not a general
  re-verification: the generator already checks every value against KnotInfo's
  `chern_simons_invariant` column. I recomputed with SnapPy 3.3.2, in a
  throwaway container through `agents/sage.sh`, the Chern-Simons invariant of
  all 28 knots whose stored string is shorter than nine decimal places, plus
  $5_2$, $10_{20}$ and $10_{165}$ as controls. All 31 agree with the stored
  digits, and all 28 short ones have a zero in the ninth decimal place. SnapPy
  reports 55 to 57 digits of accuracy for every one of them.
- **Checked by parsing the entries.** The range is complete: for each $n$ the
  set of $k$ present is exactly $1..N(n)$ less the torus knots, so 243 knots,
  223 with a mirror row and 20 without, 932 entries. The entry order is $n$
  ascending, $k$ ascending, $K$ before $\bar K$, $\operatorname{CS}$ before
  $2\pi^2\operatorname{CS}$. Every mirror value is the exact negation of its
  partner. Every $2\pi^2\operatorname{CS}$ entry but two encloses $2\pi^2$
  times what its $\operatorname{CS}$ row claims, to within half a unit in the

- **Checked because they are cheap.** All four `Similar tables` slugs answer
  200, all four `Links` URLs answer 200 and the SnapPy one carries the anchor
  it names, `/files/T313` carries both `generate.py` and
  `knotinfo_prime_knots.py`. No prose field contains a raw `<`, a raw `>` or a
  `--`. The stored entries block is about 95 KB of YAML, well inside every
  limit.

## What reads well

- **It is one table, and for both of the reasons the skill allows.** `knot`
  names *what the number is of*: $K$ and $\bar K$ are two knots, and a reader
  who computed the invariant from the other diagram holds the negative, which
  a search by number does not try. `normalisation` is one number in two
  conventions, and the conversion is multiplication by $2\pi^2$, which is
  exactly the factor the skill's own test says nobody guesses: the two forms
  have to be stored because neither is reachable from the other by the half,
  the two, the pi a reader tries.
- **The value column is headed with what it holds**, `$\operatorname{CS}(S^3
  \setminus K)$ or $2\pi^2\operatorname{CS}(S^3\setminus K)$`, not with the
  word `value`. There is no `param-latex`; the two symbolic parameters carry
  `values` displays, so the rows read `$K$` and `$\operatorname{CS}$:` rather
  than restating a column header once per row.
- **The parameters describe the family.** $4\leq n\leq 10$ is the title's own
  range, not this afternoon's run, and `complete: yes` carries the rest.
- **The rigour level is the honest one.** SnapPy has a verified volume and no
  verified Chern-Simons, so the sibling volume table can say `proven` and this
  one cannot. `heuristic (agreement-checked)` with the agreement being between
  two independent implementations is the right call, and refusing to claim
  more is worth more than the claim would be.
- **The twenty amphichiral knots are stored as exact `0`**, not as
  `0.000000000`. That is the skill's rule applied correctly: amphichirality
  forces $\operatorname{CS}\equiv-\operatorname{CS}$, so the value is $0$ by an
  argument rather than by a run of zeros. See finding 11 for the one thing
  missing from it.

## Findings

### Worth doing

**1. Forty entries are withdrawn from search by number, because digits that
were computed were not written.** The table page marks each of them with a
dagger reading "This number will not be found in the search" — `views.py`
calls `findable_by_number`, which excludes a value whose interval is wider
than $10^{-5}$ relative to its own size. A reader arriving holding one of
these forty numbers, which is the one thing this database is for, is told
nothing.

They fall into two groups, both avoidable.

*Twenty-eight of the `cs` strings lost their trailing zeros before they were
stored.* `KNOTINFO_CS` in the generator carries `"0.05"` for $9_{41}$,
`"0.066236"` for $9_{49}$, `"-0.05199305"` for $8_{14}$, and twenty-five
more. Under the corpus convention a decimal states its own precision, so
`-0.05199305` claims $\pm10^{-8}$ where the source knows $\pm10^{-9}$, and
`0.05` claims $[0.04,0.06]$ — a fifth of the whole range the invariant lives
in. I ran SnapPy on all twenty-eight:

    9_41   stored 0.05          snappy 0.05000000000000000000   acc 57
    9_49   stored 0.066236      snappy 0.06623599987939511654   acc 55
    8_14   stored -0.05199305   snappy -0.0519930495397994121   acc 56
    7_7    stored 0.1329856     snappy 0.13298559988395077539   acc 56

Every one is the nine-decimal value with zeros dropped off the end, which is
what a float repr does to a transcribed column. *Smallest fix:* write the
twenty-eight strings to nine decimal places in `KNOTINFO_CS`
(`"0.050000000"`, `"0.066236000"`, `"-0.051993050"`, …). Four of the forty
daggers go at once, and so does the sharpest instance of the fault:

**$9_{41}$'s two rows contradict each other.** `cs` says `0.05`, which is the
interval $[0.04,0.06]$; `two-pi-squared-cs` says `1 +/- 0.197`, which the skill
defines as centre and radius exactly, so $[0.803,1.197]$. But $2\pi^2\cdot
[0.04,0.06]=[0.78957,1.18435]$, and $0.78957<0.803$. The two rows of the same
number, four lines apart on the page, exclude each other's values. Nothing is
*wrong* — the true value, $2\pi^2/20=0.986960440$, is in both — but the pair
cannot both be read as stated. (While there: SnapPy gives
$\operatorname{CS}(9_{41})=0.05000000000000000000$ to 57 digits, so this knot's
invariant is almost certainly exactly $1/20$. The skill says a run of zeros is
evidence and not proof, so the thing to do is write the digits and say in that
entry's comment that the value agrees with $1/20$ to fifty digits, not to claim
it.)

*Thirty-four of the `two-pi-squared-cs` entries are written one significant
digit short.* `_scaled_text` sets `digits = max(1, source_digits - 1)`.
Multiplying by an exact constant preserves relative accuracy, so that digit is
given away for nothing, and it is the digit that carries these rows over the
threshold: `-1.02630` has relative width $1.9\times10^{-5}$ where
`-1.0262995` would have $2\times10^{-7}$. *Smallest fix:* drop the `- 1`, or
write the ball in its `+/-` form with the radius it actually has.

Four entries stay unfindable after both fixes, $10_{20}$ and its mirror in
both normalisations, because
$\operatorname{CS}(10_{20})=0.000141354$ is small and nine *decimal places* is
only six significant figures there. SnapPy gives
$0.00014135427235347314\ldots$ with 56 digits of claimed accuracy. Whether to
keep KnotInfo's printed precision everywhere or SnapPy's own is the author's
decision and I would not overturn it; but this is the row where it costs
something, and it can be taken alone.

**2. The `Formulas` section renders as two Python dictionaries.** Both entries
are stored as mappings, `{display: ..., formula: ...}`. Every other table in
the corpus — I checked T141, T309, T310, T312 — stores the string alone, and
the renderer prints whatever the value is. So the page carries, in full:

    (1)  {'display': 'mirror image', 'formula': '$\operatorname{CS}(S^3\setminus
         \bar K)\equiv -\operatorname{CS}(S^3\setminus K)\pmod{1/2}$'}

with the backslashes doubled, because that is how Python repr writes a string.
MathJax then finds the `$...$` inside and typesets it without complaint: `\\`
is a line break, so the identity comes out as several lines of italic letters
spelling `o p e r a t o r n a m e C S S s e t m i n u s b a r K e q u i v`. It
does not say "Math input error"; it just reads as nonsense, on the third
screen, between the Parameters and the Comments. *Smallest fix:* store each
formula as its string, and use the key for the label the corpus uses,
`formula-mirror` and `formula-normalisation`.

**3. The title claims every hyperbolic knot complement and the table holds
243 of them.** "Chern–Simons invariants of hyperbolic knot complements" has no
range in it, and `complete: yes` sits underneath. Read together they say the
table holds the invariant of every hyperbolic knot complement, of which there
are infinitely many. All four siblings name the range in the title —
"Hyperbolic volumes of the prime knots with at most ten crossings" and the
three polynomial tables — so this is also the one table in the family a reader
browsing the titles cannot see belongs to it. *Smallest fix:* "Chern–Simons
invariants of the hyperbolic prime knots with at most ten crossings", which
makes `complete: yes` true as written and puts the table next to its four
relations in every listing.

**4. The Definition cites a data source where the object needs defining, and
does not say what the values are elements of.** It renders as

> The Chern-Simons invariant $\operatorname{CS}(S^3\setminus K)$ [5] of the
> complete hyperbolic knot complement $S^3\setminus K$, for the hyperbolic
> prime knots with at most ten crossings.

Three things. `[5]` is KnotInfo's description page: that is where the *numbers*
come from, not what the invariant *is*, and the two papers that define it,
Kirk–Klassen and Neumann, are declared in `References` and cited nowhere — with
the two Wikipedia links, four of the eight numbered items on the page have
nothing pointing at them, where both T141 and T312 cite every reference they
declare. Second, the sentence never says that
$\operatorname{CS}$ is an element of $\mathbb{R}/\tfrac12\mathbb{Z}$ and that
what is listed is a representative; a reader who arrives holding a number that
differs from a stored one by $1/2$ has to reach the fourth sentence of the one
Comment to find that out, and that reader is the whole audience. Third, the
audit asks that a table with two conventions say so in the definition, and this
definition does not. *Smallest fix:* one more sentence — the invariant is
defined modulo $1/2$ `CITE{KirkKlassen}` `CITE{Neumann}`, the table lists the
representative near zero, for each knot and its mirror image and in both
normalisations — and move `CITE{KnotInfo}` to where the values are described.

**5. There is no `Programs` section, and the naming trap is only in the
attached file.** T141 and T312 both have one; the skill asks for "the standard
incantation for a reader who wants one more value". A reader of this table who
wants the eleven-crossing case gets nothing on the page. Worse, the one thing
they must be told before they try is in `generate.py` and nowhere else:

> The exterior is built from KnotInfo's braid word, not from SnapPy's manifold
> name … SnapPy's built-in Rolfsen names use pre-Perko numbering for part of
> the ten-crossing table.

So `Manifold('10_83').chern_simons()` answers with a different knot from
row $10_{83}$ here, and the page that tells a reader the values came from
SnapPy's `chern_simons()` does not warn them. T141 puts exactly this warning
inside its `program-snappy` snippet. *Smallest fix:* copy that shape, with
`chern_simons()` in place of `volume()`.

**6. `Comments` is one unkeyed paragraph doing five jobs.** It renders as a
single block labelled "(1)" carrying the Rolfsen–Perko numbering, why the
mirror rows are here, why the two normalisations are here, the representative
convention, and which knots are excluded. T141 splits the same material into
`comment-which-knots`, `comment-numbering`, `comment-invariance`,
`comment-entries` and `comment-range`. The cost of one block is not only
reading: an unkeyed comment has no anchor, so nothing can link to the
convention or the exclusion, and the key `comment_1` is what the pipeline
generates for a bare string rather than a name anybody chose. *Smallest fix:*
five keyed comments, in that order.

**7. Six torus knots are claimed and five are named.** The Comment says "The
six torus knots with at most ten crossings and the unknot are not hyperbolic
and are not listed", and the constraint on $k$ names "$5_1$, $7_1$, $9_1$,
$8_{19}$, $10_{124}$". The sixth is $3_1$, excluded silently by $4\leq n$, and
a reader counting the list finds five and cannot tell whether one is missing
from the list or from the table. *Smallest fix:* say why $n$ starts at 4 — "the
only three-crossing knot is the trefoil $3_1$, a torus knot" — and sort the
five, which are currently $5_1, 7_1, 9_1, 8_{19}, 10_{124}$ with $8_{19}$ after
$9_1$.

**8. The reason given for keeping the mirror rows argues the other way.** "The
rows labelled $K$ and $\bar K$ record one invariant … they are kept together
because mirror reversal changes the sign modulo $1/2$." That sign rule is
precisely why one row *determines* the other; as a reason for storing both it
reads backwards. The real reason is the good one and is shorter: $K$ and
$\bar K$ are different knots. *Smallest fix:* state the sign rule as the fact
it is, pointing at formula (1), and let the sentence about why both are listed
say that a chiral knot and its mirror image are two knots.

### Smaller, and cheap

**9. Formula (2) states nothing and introduces a symbol used nowhere else.**
`$\operatorname{CS}_{2\pi^2}(S^3\setminus K)=2\pi^2\operatorname{CS}(S^3
\setminus K)$` defines a name for a quantity the table calls
$2\pi^2\operatorname{CS}$ everywhere else, and then says that name equals the
thing it names. Meanwhile the identity a reader would actually use is in the
gloss on a `Similar tables` line: that
$\mathrm{Vol}(S^3\setminus K)+i\,2\pi^2\operatorname{CS}(S^3\setminus K)$ is
the complex volume, modulo $i\pi^2$. *Smallest fix:* replace formula (2) with
that one, citing Neumann, and let the relation line go back to naming a
relation.

**10. The representative interval cannot hold every class it claims to.** "The
value of $\operatorname{CS}$ is the representative in $(-1/4,1/4)$ of an
element of $\mathbb{R}/(1/2)\mathbb{Z}$" — an element congruent to $1/4$ has no
representative in an open interval of length $1/2$. No stored value is
affected; the largest is $0.24729063$. *Smallest fix:* $[-1/4,1/4)$, or "every
value here lies in $(-1/4,1/4)$".

**11. Nothing says why twenty rows are exactly `0`.** They are the only exact
values on a page of approximations, and a reader seeing `0` among nine-decimal
numbers will read it as a rounding. The argument is one clause and the page
already has both halves of it: amphichiral knots are their own mirror image
(parameter `knot`), and mirror reversal negates the invariant (formula (1)), so
$\operatorname{CS}\equiv-\operatorname{CS}$ and the value is $0$. *Smallest
fix:* that clause, in the comment about the mirror rows.

**12. The parameter line renders `normalisation — normalisation of the
Chern-Simons invariant`,** and the constraint under it, "one of the normalised
invariant $\operatorname{CS}$ and the unnormalised invariant
$2\pi^2\operatorname{CS}$", repeats the two row labels immediately above it
without constraining anything. A title that says what the values are — "which
multiple of the invariant is listed" — costs one line and removes both.

### Noted only

**13.** The title sets an en dash, "Chern–Simons", and every other line on the
page sets a hyphen, "Chern-Simons". I checked that this costs nothing in
search: `api/lookup?text=Adams-Bashforth` and the same query with an en dash
both return "Adams–Bashforth coefficients", so the index treats them alike. It
is only that the two spellings sit two lines apart.

**14.** One tag, `period`, where the skill says two is typical. There is no
knot or topology tag in the corpus, and the rule for proposing one — three or
more tables would carry it — is now met five times over by this table and its
four siblings. Worth somebody's decision, not worth blocking on.

**15.** The same entry comment is stored on both normalisation rows of every
knot, so a reader sees "$10_1$; alternating" twice in a row, 466 times. It
costs about 20 KB in a 95 KB block, which is nothing, and the alternative —
a comment on the $\operatorname{CS}$ row only — looks like an omission. I would
leave it.

## Left for a person

Nothing is blocked. Findings 2 to 12 are prose and metadata edits to a draft
nobody has reviewed. Finding 1 changes stored values: not their correctness,
which I re-checked against an independent SnapPy run on all 31 knots I looked
at, but the precision they claim. That means going back through the generator
rather than editing the document — 56 `cs` entries for the dropped zeros, and
the whole $2\pi^2\operatorname{CS}$ column if the second half is done too.

The one thing I would not decide alone is the last part of finding 1 — whether
this table should keep KnotInfo's printed nine decimals at all, when the
generator computes 56 digits on the way past and throws 47 of them away. Doing
so is defensible: KnotInfo's column is the published source, and SnapPy's
accuracy estimate is an estimate. But it is a decision, and the table says
"rounds to the digits carried by KnotInfo" without saying it is one.
