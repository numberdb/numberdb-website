## Worth doing

### 1. The address is already `..._in_mathbb_P_n`, and a rename will not change it

The draft's slug is

    Chern_classes_of_smooth_complete_intersections_in_mathbb_P_n

`slug_for` in `numberdb_app/editing.py:738` drops mathematics from a title,
which is why T121 is at `Monomial_symmetric_polynomials` and not at
`Monomial_symmetric_polynomials_m_lambda`. It puts the symbols back,
transliterated, when what is left dangles — and "Chern classes of smooth
complete intersections in $\mathbb{P}^n$" leaves `... intersections in`, which
ends in one of the dangling words, so the fallback fires and `\mathbb{P}^n`
becomes `mathbb_P_n`. The code is working as designed; the title is what
chose this.

The reason to raise it now rather than after review is that **the slug does not
follow the title**: `test_the_slug_does_not_change` in
`numberdb_app/test_editing.py:919` fixes it at creation, "every link anybody has
written points at it". Nobody has written a link to this one yet, because it is
a draft. That window closes at publication and never reopens.

So it is a decision, not a fix: accept `mathbb_P_n` as the thing people paste
into papers, or recreate the draft under a title that slugs cleanly — "Chern
classes of smooth projective complete intersections" gives
`Chern_classes_of_smooth_projective_complete_intersections`, and `Keywords`
already carries "Chern class projective complete intersection", so nothing is
lost to search. I would recreate it. It costs a T-number and a refill today and
cannot be had at any price tomorrow.

### 2. The completeness note says what was computed and never says why

It renders as

> Table is complete: no (it holds every row with $n\leq 10$, every $d_i\leq 10$,
> and $\prod_i d_i\leq 100$)

Two things. As English, three clauses each starting "every" read as three
separate claims rather than one row having to satisfy all three, and a reader
has to guess the conjunction: "every $d_i\leq 10$" is not a row. "it holds
every row with $n\leq 10$, all $d_i\leq 10$ and $\prod_i d_i\leq 100$" costs
the same and cannot be misread.

The substantive half is that the skill asks this field for the argument —
"Say which range you chose and why in the completeness note. That sentence is
the argument, and it is what a reader checking whether their own number belongs
here actually reads." Three bounds are stated and no reason for any of them is.
A reader cannot tell why $\prod_i d_i\leq 100$ rather than 50 or 200, and the
three together land on **999 entries, one under the recommended 1000**, which
is what counting rather than choosing looks like from outside.

There is a real reason available: $\prod_i d_i$ is the degree of $X$, so the
bound says the table holds the complete intersections of degree at most 100,
which is where the named ones are — the quintic threefold, the quartic K3, all
five Calabi-Yau threefolds, every smooth surface in $\mathbb P^3$ up to degree
ten. Smallest fix: one clause. "... and $\prod_i d_i\leq 100$, which is the
degree of $X$, so it holds every complete intersection of degree at most 100."

### 3. A seventh of the table is 140 rows carrying 18 distinct values, two of them `1`

When $k=n-1$ the variety is a curve, $c(T_X)=1+c_1$, and formula (2) already
gives $c_1$. There are 140 such rows and they hold **18 distinct values**
between them: `-7*h + 1` is the entry for 18 different rows, `-6*h + 1` and
`-8*h + 1` for 17 each. Two rows — $n=2$, $d=(3)$ and $n=3$, $d=(2,2)$ — store
the polynomial `1`.

The other 859 rows hold 839 distinct values, which is as clean as a table gets.
So the curve rows are 14% of the table and about 2% of what it distinguishes.

The skill is explicit about the cost: "values that are a handful of small
integers ... match everything and tell nobody anything, and putting them in a
table makes search by number worse for everyone", and a search for the
polynomial `1` in a published T332 answers with two rows that say nothing.

This is a judgement for a person and I would not pretend otherwise. Either
constrain $k\leq n-2$, so every row is a variety of dimension at least two and
the value carries more than $\sum_i d_i$ does, and say in a comment that for
$k=n-1$ the class is $1+(n+1-\sum_i d_i)h$ by (2) — which serves the reader who
has one better than the row does. Or keep them and give the completeness note
a clause saying why.

### 4. "Calabi-Yau" carries the most interesting claim on the page and is never defined or linked

Comment (8) is

> The linear coefficient is $n+1-\sum_i d_i$, so the Calabi-Yau complete
> intersections in this grid are exactly the rows with no $h$ term.

and three entry comments use the term again. Nothing on the page says what it
means, and `Links` has two entries, neither of which is about it. The sentence
is in fact an implicit definition — Calabi-Yau here is $c_1=0$, trivial
canonical class — but it is phrased as a consequence, so a reader who does not
already know the term learns nothing and a reader who does cannot tell which
sense is meant (dimension one, where it is an elliptic curve, is included).

The skill: "Define notation where you use it", and "Link the first mention of a
thing to what explains it. A table if the corpus holds one; otherwise a
reference the table already declares." The corpus holds none — `text=Calabi`
and `text=K3` both return nothing — so the link has to be outward, and it costs
one `Links` entry.

Smallest fix, in (8): "By CITE{formula-first}, the rows with no $h$ term are
exactly those with $\sum_i d_i=n+1$, where the canonical class is trivial and
$X$ is Calabi-Yau CITE{CYWiki}." That defines the term, cites it, and fixes
finding 9 below in the same sentence.

### 5. The fold hides both of the independent checks, and leaves a circle

`rigour details` folds. What a reader sees with the page closed is one
sentence:

> The generator computes in the exact ring $\mathbb Z[h]$ from Formula (1).

and behind **more** is

> It checks the quintic threefold value $1+10h^2-40h^3$ from [1], verifies
> Formula (2) on every row, and compares the Euler characteristic from Formula
> (3) with Hirzebruch's generating function quoted in [2] on every row in the
> table.

The visible half says the table was computed from the formula the table itself
states, which is the one thing that is not evidence of anything. The hidden
half is the whole of the case: a value from an outside source and a generating
function from another one, both on every row. A sceptical reader reads the
first and stops.

The fold is `FOLD_ABOVE = 400` in `numberdb_app/prose.py`, and it measures the
*rendered* HTML, so each `CITE{...}` costs about 45 characters of anchor rather
than its 20 of source — which is why a note that looks like 374 characters folds
at all. Trimming will not clear it; reordering will.

Smallest fix: lead with the check.

> Every row was checked against Hirzebruch's Euler-characteristic generating
> function CITE{CIWiki}, and the quintic threefold against the value
> $1+10h^2-40h^3$ in CITE{ChernWiki}. The generator computes in the exact ring
> $\mathbb Z[h]$ from Formula CITE{formula-total}, and Formula
> CITE{formula-first} was verified on every row.

### 6. The fifth Calabi-Yau threefold is the one without a comment

Exactly five rows of this table are Calabi-Yau threefolds, and they are the five
classical ones. Four carry an entry comment:

    4  (5)        Quintic threefold; integrating the top Chern class gives ...
    5  (2,4)      Calabi-Yau threefold of type $(2,4)$.
    5  (3,3)      Calabi-Yau threefold of type $(3,3)$.
    6  (2,2,3)    —
    7  (2,2,2,2)  Complete intersection of four quadrics; a Calabi-Yau threefold.

A reader who knows there are five and finds four named will assume the fifth is
not one. Smallest fix: `Calabi-Yau threefold of type $(2,4)$.` again, with
$(2,2,3)$ in it, on entry `6,2,2,3`.

### 7. The Programs snippet uses the one import style the skill tells contributors not to use

    from sage.all import PolynomialRing, ZZ

The skill: "Name the rings you use, rather than importing `sage.all`. A modular
passagemath has no `sage.all`" — and passagemath is an install the skill
recommends by name. Every other Sage program block in the corpus that imports
anything does it the other way: T330, T331 and T263 all begin
`import numberdb.sage as numberdb` and then name the rings. T332 is the only one
reaching for `sage.all`.

I ran all three versions under `agents/sage.sh`. The page's snippet works.
Named imports *alone* do not:

    AttributeError: partially initialized module 'sage.rings.integer_ring'
    has no attribute 'ZZ' (most likely due to a circular import)

and named imports after `import numberdb.sage as numberdb` work, returning
`-40*h^3 + 10*h^2 + 1` and the table's longest entry unchanged. So the fix is
the corpus's three lines:

    import numberdb.sage as numberdb
    from sage.rings.integer_ring import ZZ
    from sage.rings.polynomial.polynomial_ring_constructor import PolynomialRing

While the block is open: it is the only Programs block I looked at with no
comment in it at all. `chern_complete_intersection(4, [5])` is the quintic
threefold, the most famous row in the table, and nothing says so. T330 and T331
carry `# Hilbert matrix, n = 3.` for the same reason. One line:
`# The quintic threefold: entry 4,5.`

### 9. Comment (8) restates Formula (2), which is four lines above it

"The linear coefficient is $n+1-\sum_i d_i$" is Formula (2),
$c_1(T_X)=(n+1-\sum_i d_i)h$, in words. The skill's section table gives
Comments "conventions, caveats, what the values mean at a special point ...
**not** formulas", and (2) is cited from nothing, so the page has a formula
nobody points at and a comment that says it again. The page puts `Formulas`
*before* `Comments`, so a reader meets (2) and then meets it again as (8). The
fix in finding 4 — "By CITE{formula-first}, ..." — removes the restatement and
creates the citation in one clause.

### 10. The first two citation markers a reader meets run [2] then [1]

The definition's first sentence cites `CIWiki` and its second cites
`ChernWiki`, but `Links` declares `ChernWiki` first, and the numbering follows
the declaration order rather than first mention. So the opening sentence of the
page renders

> ... a smooth complete intersection of multidegree $(d_1,\dots,d_k)$ **[2]**.
> The entry is the total Chern class ... the hyperplane class $h$ **[1]**;

Neither reference is uncited, so nothing is missing — it just counts backwards
in the first line anybody reads. Smallest fix: swap the two entries in `Links`,
so `CIWiki` is declared first. Nothing else on the page depends on the order.

### 11. "it depends only on $n$ and the multidegree" points rather than names

The definition ends

> ... written in powers of the hyperplane class $h$ [1]; it depends only on
> $n$ and the multidegree.

The nearest noun to "it" is the hyperplane class, and *that* does not depend on
the multidegree at all. What is meant is the substantive claim that makes the
table's indexing legitimate: the coefficients are the same for every smooth $X$
of that multidegree. Say that. "The coefficients $a_i$ are the same for every
smooth $X$ of a given multidegree."

That also gives $a_i$ something to do. As it stands the definition introduces
the notation and nothing on the page uses it again — (8) says "the linear
coefficient" where it could say $a_1$.

### 12. Why $k\leq n-1$ is never said, while the parallel exclusion is explained

The parameter constraint is "a nonempty nondecreasing list of integers at least
$2$, with $1\leq k\leq n-1$", and $k\leq n-1$ is a choice presented as part of
the family: it excludes $k=n$, where $X$ is a finite set of points and
$c(T_X)=1$. Comment (6) explains the *other* exclusion — degree-one equations —
in a whole sentence, so a reader who has read (6) will look for the companion
sentence about $k$ and not find it.

Smallest fix, appended to (6): "Taking $k=n$ is excluded for a different reason:
$X$ is then a finite set of points and $c(T_X)=1$."

("a nonempty ... list ... with $1\leq k$" says nonempty twice. Harmless.)

## Noted only, because I noticed

- `Similar tables` reads "Elementary symmetric polynomials —   store the
  universal elementary symmetric polynomials in the Chern roots". The relation
  is real and it is one step short of usable: what ties the tables is that
  $c_i$ is $e_i$ evaluated at the Chern roots, and a reader who does not
  already know the splitting principle cannot get there from "in the Chern
  roots". Also, T331's neighbouring entries use the singular ("stores"), the
  table being the subject; this one uses the plural.
- `rigour details` writes the quintic value as $1+10h^2-40h^3$ where the table
  stores `-40*h^3 + 10*h^2 + 1`. I was going to call this an inconsistency
  until I read the source: Wikipedia's Chern class article ends that
  calculation with exactly `1 + 10h^2 - 40h^3`, so the note is quoting, which
  is the better thing to do.
- The quintic's entry comment states a number, $-200$, inside a comment. The
  skill's rule about that is aimed at the table's own quantity in another
  normalisation, which an Euler characteristic is not, and Formula (3) derives

  usually a finding.
- The literal "comment: " before every entry comment is the site's, not this
  table's — T325 renders its 432 entry comments the same way.
- The title says "Chern classes" and the value header says $c(T_X)$, one total

  has to be exactly true of every row, and it is.

## What I checked hardest, and found sound

**Is the completeness claim true?** Yes, exactly. I enumerated every $(n,d)$
with $2\leq n\leq 10$, $1\leq k\leq n-1$, $2\leq d_1\leq\cdots\leq d_k\leq 10$
and $\prod_i d_i\leq 100$ — the family the parameters and the completeness note
describe between them — and compared against the stored keys: **999 expected,
999 stored, none missing and none extra**. A reader who works out whether their
row should be here gets the right answer. This is the single most useful true
thing on the page and finding 2 is only about the sentence that states it.

**Does the page's own program rebuild the page's numbers?** Yes. I ran the
Programs snippet verbatim under `agents/sage.sh`. It returns
`-40*h^3 + 10*h^2 + 1` for $(4,[5])$, character for character the stored entry
`4,5`, and it also reproduced `2,2`, `3,4`, `5,2,4`, `6,2,2,3`, `7,2,2,2,2` and
the table's longest entry, `10,9` at 108 characters. It prints nothing on its
last line under `sage -python`, which is the corpus's house style for a
Programs block and not a fault.

**Do the two links say what the table says they say?** Yes, both, verbatim.
The Chern class article's "Quintic threefold" section ends
`c(\mathcal{T}_X) = 1 + 10h^2 - 40h^3` and integrates it to $-200$; the
Complete intersection article's "Euler characteristic" section gives
Hirzebruch's generating function
$\sum_n \chi(X_n(a_1,\ldots,a_r))z^n = \frac{a_1\cdots a_r}{(1-z)^2}\prod_i (1+(a_i-1)z)^{-1}$.
The audit does not follow outward links, so I did, and these are the two claims
the rigour note rests on.

**Does it render?** Yes, whole. The rebuilt page is 511,127 bytes, status 200,
and it holds **all 999 entry anchors, every one of them unique** — `2,2`
through `10,2,2,2,2,2,3` — so any row is citable and no two rows collide on an
address even though the index runs $n$ and then a variable-length multidegree.
No `CITE-broken`, no `HREF-broken`, no `argument ()`, no raw `<` left in
mathematics, no section truncated. Every `CITE` resolves — `(1)`, `(2)`, `(3)`
for the formulas, `[1]`, `[2]` for the links — and both `HREF` slugs render as
links and both answer 200 (`Elementary_symmetric_polynomials`,
`Monomial_symmetric_polynomials`). The number block gets three columns headed
`$n$`, `$(d_1,\dots,d_k)$`, `$c(T_X)$`, under the heading `Polynomials`, which
is the site's own word for a `Z[]` table. All **30 distinct TeX fragments** in
the document typeset under
MathJax 3 with no error; the control — a deliberately unbalanced `\left` —
did error, and an undefined macro does *not*, because `AllPackages` includes
`noundefined`, so I also listed the macros used and they are twelve core ones
(`\mathbb`, `\subset`, `\sum`, `\prod`, `\dots`, `\leq`, `\geq`, `\in`, `\dim`,
`\chi`, `\left`, `\right`). No raw `<` or `>` anywhere in the document, and no
`--`.

**Is it one table?** Yes, and of the good kind. Both parameters are arguments
rather than names — $n$ is the ambient dimension and $d$ the multidegree of one
object — the value column is headed `$c(T_X)$` rather than the word `value`,
one symbol true of all 999 rows, and there is no `param-latex` restating a
heading one row at a time. `Symbolic` for a list-valued parameter is what T121,
T122 and T259 use.

**Is the range readable?** Yes. The longest entry is 108 characters and the
whole entries block is about 39 KB of values, a fifth of the soft limit, so the
next person can extend it. Entries run $n$ ascending, then $k$, then $d$
lexicographic, so the rows anybody arrives holding — the quintic threefold, the
quartic K3, the cubic surface — are near the top of their block rather than
buried.
