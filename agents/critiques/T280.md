# Critique of T280, "Counterexamples to Euler's sum of powers conjecture"

Read on 2026-09-16. The table is a private draft owned by zeta3.

## How it was read

- **Document:** `GET /api/table?id=T280` with the key returned it. There are
  23 solutions: 19 with $k=4$ and 4 with $k=5$. Each has the parts `a1`,
  `a2`, `a3`, `a4` (for $k=5$ only) and `b`.
- **Audit:** `GET /api/table/T280/audit` returned no findings, with
  `clean: true`. I did not run `manage.py audit_table`, because this runner
  has no Django.
- **Rendering:** `/skill` came through the SOCKS proxy, but the next request
  through it failed (`000`). `/T280` answers 404 without a session, so I
  rendered the draft through `/preview?table=` in nine pieces. Each prose
  section went with one solution, and the entries went in groups of four or
  five. All nine returned 200 (two larger pieces returned 400 at 4.2 KB and
  were split). No piece contained `Math input error`, `HREF-broken`,
  `CITE-broken` or `argument ()`.
- **Sources:** I read the Wikipedia article's source text, the arXiv
  abstract page of Braun 2603.05549, and the OEIS A003828 b-file. The OEIS
  entry itself is behind a Cloudflare challenge and I could not read it. I
  resolved all three reference identifiers through `doi.org` and Crossref.
- **Corpus:** a text search for "equal sums of like powers", "fourth powers",
  "taxicab", "Diophantine", "perfect powers", "Fermat" and "Euler" found no
  table that the prose should link to.

## The audit

It is clean, and I agree as far as its rules go. The `part` parameter, with
the names $a_1,\dots,a_4,b$ and the value heading "$a_i$ or $b$", is the good
kind: these are parts of one solution, just as in T279. A reader holding
$85282$ wants $55$, $3183$, $28969$ and $85359$ next to it. The audit's rules
do not cover the two most important findings below, one about what the
definition admits and one about identifiers.

## Findings, ranked

### 1. The definition admits many rows the table does not hold. Worth doing.

The definition reads: "Primitive integer solutions of
$a_1^k+\cdots+a_{k-1}^k=b^k$". The parameter constraint adds only that $b$
is positive. Comment (1) says the summands may be negative. Under those
words, several things count as solutions that the table leaves out:

- **The same identity with a different $b$.** The Braun row is
  $(-1340632)^5+719115^5+1331622^5+1956213^5=1956878^5$. If $1956213^5$ is
  moved to the right and the rest to the left, it becomes
  $(-1331622)^5+(-719115)^5+1340632^5+1956878^5=1956213^5$. That is also a
  primitive integer solution with $b>0$, and I checked it with exact
  arithmetic. The Scher–Seidl row gives $b=14068$, $6237$ and $5027$ in the
  same way. Nothing in the definition says which rearrangement is "the"
  solution.
- **Trivial solutions.** $1^5+(-1)^5+0^5+2^5=2^5$ is primitive and has
  $b>0$. There are infinitely many of these. The generator refuses zero
  summands and cancelling pairs (`rigour details` says so too), but the
  definition does not.
- **Sign variants for $k=4$.** $(-95800)^4+217519^4+414560^4=422481^4$ is an
  integer solution. The generator requires positive summands for $k=4$, but
  the definition does not.

Two people following the definition would therefore not build the same
table. The table itself is sensible: in every row $b$ has the largest
absolute value, which is how Wikipedia and Braun write these identities. It
is only the words that fail to say so.

Smallest fix: state the conditions the generator already applies, and state
them in the definition. For example: "Solutions in nonzero integers of
$a_1^k+\cdots+a_{k-1}^k=b^k$ with no common factor, $b>|a_i|$ for every $i$,
and no two $a_i$ summing to zero; for even $k$ the $a_i$ are positive." The
condition $b>|a_i|$ is the one that picks a single representative of each
identity. The constraint on `b` should then say "the largest term" rather
than "a positive integer appearing as $b$".

### 2. Both DOIs are wrong. Worth doing; it is two strings.

- Elkies1988 has `doi: 10.1090/S0025-5718-1988-0930229-3`, and `doi.org`
  answers **404** for it. The correct DOI is
  `10.1090/S0025-5718-1988-0930224-9`. Crossref gives its title as
  "On A⁴+B⁴+C⁴=D⁴", by Noam D. Elkies, pages 825–835. It matches the MR
  number 0930224 that Wikipedia gives.
- Frye1988 has `doi: 10.1109/SUPERC.1988.74142`, which resolves to **a
  different paper**: Stampen and Davis, "Multi-issue coalitions in the
  Congress (voting alignments on supercomputer)", pages 152–166. The correct
  DOI is `10.1109/SUPERC.1988.74138`. Crossref gives its title as "Finding
  95800⁴+217519⁴+414560⁴=422481⁴ on the Connection Machine", by Frye, pages
  106–116.

A reader who clicks "(doi)" under References gets an error page for Elkies
and a political-science paper for Frye.

### 3. The completeness bound $b\leq1986560000$ has no source a reader can find. Worth doing.

`complete-note` says the table holds "every fourth-power solution with
$b\leq1986560000$ listed by OEIS A003828". The b-file it cites is
"synthesized from sequence entry" and holds exactly the 19 terms stored here,
ending at $1871713857$. The number 1986560000 does not appear in that file,
in the Wikipedia article, in the generator, or in the batch proposal. The
proposal said the search bound "is not stated where I could read it". The
OEIS entry may state the bound, but I could not read it either.

A bound is what a reader holding a quadruple checks their number against,
so it has to say where it comes from. Smallest fix: if the entry does state
it, say so, e.g. "every solution with $b\leq1986560000$, the bound to which
OEIS A003828 CITE{OEIS003828} states its list is complete". If it does not,
drop the bound and write "the 19 values of $b$ listed by OEIS A003828".

### 4. The page does not say that two of the rows are not counterexamples in Euler's sense. Worth doing; it is one sentence.

The title calls every row a counterexample. Euler's conjecture concerns sums
of *positive* $k$-th powers. When the negative term of the Scher–Seidl row
is moved across, it gives $220^5+14132^5=5027^5+6237^5+14068^5$, which is not
a fifth power written as a sum of four positive fifth powers. The Braun row
is the same. Comment (1) goes as far as the rearrangement and stops before
the consequence. A reader holding $14132$ is told they have a counterexample
to Euler's conjecture. Wikipedia counts these rows among its four "primitive
counterexamples", so the title can stay, but the comment should state the
fact.

Smallest fix: add to comment (1) something like "The resulting identity has
five terms on the two sides together, so it does not contradict Euler's
conjecture for positive summands; the sources count these rows as solutions
of the signed equation."

### 5. Comment (3) names Frye without citing him, and "Frye's smallest solution" reads as one of several. Cheap; do it with #2.

"The row $b=422481$ is Frye's smallest solution" suggests Frye found several
solutions and this is the least of them. What is meant is that Frye found
the smallest solution. `Frye1988` is cited only from `sources`, never from
the prose. Also, the first sentence of comment (3) repeats `complete-note`.
Fix: "The row $b=422481$ is the smallest solution, found by Frye
CITE{Frye1988}, and $b=20615673$ is the first solution Elkies found
CITE{Elkies1988}." Then drop the sentence about OEIS.

### 6. Noted only

- `complete-note`: "the four fifth-power primitive solutions **known in**
  CITE{WikiEuler}". Solutions are known *to* somebody and *listed in* a
  source. "listed in" is the word.
- The parameter list reads "solution — right-hand base (a positive integer
  appearing as $b$ …)". The heading is right for the column, which holds
  equations. The title and constraint describe $b$, so the line contradicts
  itself. A title such as "solution, indexed by $b$" would agree with both.
- The `Similar tables` relation for T278, "in the same circle of
  perfect-power Diophantine equations", is vague. "Records small values of
  $|x^3-y^2|$, another measure of how close perfect powers come" says what
  the relation is.
- Entries run with $k=4$ first. So $27^5+84^5+110^5+133^5=144^5$, the 1966
  counterexample most readers know, sits below 76 fourth-power rows. Ordering
  by $k$ is the natural order, and I would not change it for this alone.
- No reference for Lander and Parkin 1966 or Scher and Seidl 1996. The rows
  they found are cited only through Wikipedia.

## What reads well

- **The page renders cleanly.** All 23 equation labels, the long
  `rigour details` with $v=-31/467$, and the references with mathematics in
  their titles set without error.
- **A reader holding a number is served.** Each row puts the whole identity
  next to the number, so a reader holding $2767624$ sees at once that it is
  $a_3$ in $673865^4+1390400^4+2767624^4=2813001^4$.
- **The parameters describe the family.** $k$ is "an integer exponent $k>2$",
  not $\{4,5\}$. What was computed sits in `complete-note`.

Findings 1–4 are worth a repair. 1 and 2 are the most important: 1 is a
definition that does not define, and 2 sends a reader to the wrong paper.
Finding 5 is cheap to fix alongside 2. The items under 6 are noted only
because I saw them.
