# T317: Cusp shapes of the hyperbolic prime knots with at most ten crossings

Read on 2026-09-18, against the skill fetched the same day from
<https://numberdb.org/skill> (47,534 bytes, byte-identical to yesterday's
copy; fetched directly, because the SOCKS proxy at `127.0.0.1:1080` refuses
every connection on this machine and `ALL_PROXY` is empty). T317 is a draft of
466 complex entries at 90 digits: the cusp shape $\tau(S^3\setminus K)$ of
each of the 243 hyperbolic prime knots with at most ten crossings, and of the
mirror image of each of the 223 that are chiral. `rigour: proven`,
`complete: yes`. It is the eighth knot table in the corpus and is built to the
pattern of T313, whose three-parameter index -- $n$, $k$, and a `knot`
parameter taking `K` and `mirror` -- it copies word for word.

**How I read it.** The HTML routes authenticate by session, so `GET /T317`
answers 404 to the zeta3 key. I rebuilt the page by the recipe the T315 and
T316 critiques left in `docs/agent-environment.md`: the site's own code and
templates, the document as `/api/table?id=T317` serves it, a throwaway sqlite
database inside the `agents/sage.sh` container, and the two patches a *real*
or complex table needs there (`RangeField.get_placeholder` to `%s`, and
`Number.save` nulling `value_range` and `frac_range`). That gave status 200
and 366,096 bytes of HTML with all 466 rows, the column headers, the entry
comments, the prose and the reference numbering. Every quotation below is from
that page.

`audit_table` cannot run here -- no Django, no database.
`GET /api/table/T317/audit` answers with one finding, the two-tables question
about the `knot` parameter. **I disagree with it**, and say why at the end.
Seven of the findings below are outside what the audit checks at all: it
verifies that every `CITE` resolves and never that every `Link` is cited
(`audit_table.py` `_check`, which reads only `unresolved_citations`); it never
opens a link to see whether the page is the one the title promises
(`findings_for(table, fetch=False)` is what the API endpoint calls); it reads
a `Similar tables` relation not at all; and its rule against linking to a
draft stands down on a draft, so `HREF{T316}` is silent today and will fire
the day this table is published.

**The table is in good shape.** The page renders whole and clean. The
mathematics checks out everywhere I could reach it independently: the
`Programs` block runs as written and reproduces stored digits, formula (2) is
exact to all ninety places, formula (1) holds on all 223 chiral pairs, the 20
purely imaginary rows are exactly the 20 amphichiral knots, and no two of the
466 values agree even to six decimal places. What the findings are about is
reading: one link that names a method SnapPy does not have, a Definition that
defines a piece of mathematics by naming a program, a numbering discrepancy
stated by half, and twenty rows that print a bracket where a reader expects a
digit.

## What I checked hardest, and found nothing wrong with

* **The `Programs` block runs and gives the page's numbers.** Preparsed, with
  `Integer` in scope (`sage -python` has no preparser -- the note at
  `docs/agent-environment.md:989`), the three lines import and evaluate. The
  braid `[1, -2, 1, -2]` returns
  `0.?e-117 + 3.46410161513775458705489268301174473388561050762076125611161395890386603381760007416229237351449715135125228283081341?*I`,
  which is the stored $4_1$ row to every one of its 90 digits. I ran four more
  KnotInfo braid words the same way -- $5_2$, $6_1$, $6_3$, $8_{18}$ -- and
  each reproduced its stored row to the last written place.
* **$4_1$ is $2i\sqrt3$ to all ninety digits.** Formula (2) says
  $\tau(S^3\setminus4_1)=2\sqrt{-3}$; the stored imaginary part differs from
  `2*Decimal(3).sqrt()` at 130 working digits by $3.5\times10^{-90}$, against
  a last written place of $10^{-89}$. That is a closed form matched against
  the stored digits, not against the code that made them.
* **Formula (1) holds on every chiral pair.** For all 223 knots with a mirror
  row, the mirror's imaginary part is the same string and its real part is the
  same string with the sign flipped: 466 rows, 0 violations. So
  $\tau(S^3\setminus\bar K)=-\overline{\tau(S^3\setminus K)}$ is not only
  stated, it is what the table holds.
* **The 20 rows with a zero real part are exactly the 20 amphichiral knots.**
  $4_1$, $6_3$, $8_3$, $8_9$, $8_{12}$, $8_{17}$, $8_{18}$, $10_{17}$,
  $10_{33}$, $10_{37}$, $10_{43}$, $10_{45}$, $10_{79}$, $10_{81}$, $10_{88}$,
  $10_{99}$, $10_{109}$, $10_{115}$, $10_{118}$, $10_{123}$ -- the standard
  list, and precisely the set of knots with no `mirror` row. $243+223=466$.
* **The family is the one the prose claims.** $1+2+3+7+21+49+165=248$ prime
  knots from four to ten crossings, five torus knots among them, 243 rows;
  every $k$ from 1 to $N(n)$ present with no gaps and nothing extra.
* **A reader with a handful of digits gets one answer.** All 466 stored
  strings are distinct, and rounding both components to six decimal places
  still leaves 466 distinct pairs. Six digits identify the row.
* **The page does not break.** Balanced `$` in every field, no raw `<`, no
  `--`, nothing in the HTML reading as a MathJax input error or an
  `(Unknown key)`. The `\begin{pmatrix}` in comment (6) survives to the page
  intact.
* **Every value is indexed.** The audit's `_indexed_as_many_as_written` check
  did not fire, so all 466 -- including the twenty written with a `[0, 0]`
  real part -- answer a search by their digits. Finding 4 below is cosmetic,
  not a searchability hole.
* **Everything the page points at exists.** All five `HREF` slugs and all four
  `Links` answer 200. Four of the five `Similar tables` captions are their
  target's title exactly.

## Findings, in the order I would fix them

### 1. `Links[5]` names a method SnapPy does not have, and points at a page that never mentions it

The Definition's only citation. It reads

> SnapPy documentation: CuspNeighborhood.cusp_info and verified computations
> — https://snappy.computop.org/verify.html

Two things are wrong with four words. `cusp_info` is not a method of
`CuspNeighborhood`: inside the container,
`hasattr(snappy.CuspNeighborhood, 'cusp_info')` is `False`, while
`hasattr(snappy.Manifold, 'cusp_info')` is `True` -- and `Manifold.cusp_info`
is what the `Programs` block, the generator and the rigour note all actually
call. And `verify.html` contains the string `cusp_info` **zero** times: I
fetched it (200, 26,695 bytes) and counted.

So a reader who wants to know what SnapPy means by "shape" -- which is the
whole of what this table holds -- follows [5] from the Definition and arrives
at a page about interval certification that does not discuss the quantity.
`https://snappy.computop.org/manifold.html` answers 200 and carries
`id="snappy.Manifold.cusp_info"`.

**Smallest fix:** title "SnapPy documentation: `Manifold.cusp_info`", url
`https://snappy.computop.org/manifold.html#snappy.Manifold.cusp_info`. If the
verified-computation page is wanted as well, make it a second link and cite it
from the rigour note, which is the sentence it belongs to.

### 2. The Definition defines the mathematics by naming a program

> The cusp shape $\tau(S^3\setminus K)$ is SnapPy's longitude translation
> divided by its meridian translation for the complete hyperbolic metric on a
> one-cusped knot complement [5].

Two people cannot build this table from that sentence unless both of them have
SnapPy, and the object it describes exists without SnapPy. The definition a
reader needs is one clause longer and has no software in it: the cusp of a
one-cusped hyperbolic 3-manifold is a Euclidean torus $\mathbb C/\Lambda$; the
meridian and longitude of a knot complement act on it by translations $m$ and
$\ell$ which form a basis of $\Lambda$; the cusp shape is $\ell/m$.

The page already knows this -- comment (6) says "the meridian-longitude basis
is part of the definition", which is a remark about a definition the
Definition did not give. Compare the siblings, whose definitions are
mathematics and which name SnapPy only in `Programs`: T141's is "The volume
$\mathrm{Vol}(S^3\setminus K)$ of the complete hyperbolic metric on the
complement of a hyperbolic knot $K$", and T313's is "an element of
$\mathbb{R}/(1/2)\mathbb{Z}$".

**Smallest fix:** replace "SnapPy's longitude translation divided by its
meridian translation" with the ratio of the longitude translation to the
meridian translation of the Euclidean cusp torus, and move the sentence "this
is what SnapPy's `Manifold.cusp_info('shape')` returns" into comment (6),
which is where the convention already lives.

### 3. Comment (4) says SnapPy's names differ "in part" and never says which names moved

> SnapPy's built-in names Manifold('n_k') [5] use Rolfsen's pre-Perko
> ten-crossing table in part and interchange the knots named $10_{83}$ and
> $10_{86}$ relative to KnotInfo, so they are not used to construct the
> entries.

The interchange is stated and is true -- I checked it. SnapPy's
`Manifold('10_83').cusp_info('shape')[0]` is $2.3597\ldots+8.2893\ldots i$,
which is this table's $10_{86}$; SnapPy's `10_86` is $-3.8716\ldots+8.2340\ldots i$,
which is this table's $10_{83}$ mirror. Good: T316's page omitted this
entirely and its critique had to supply it.

"In part" is the problem. What it stands for is a five-name shift, and a
reader cannot recover it from those two words:

    snappy Manifold('10_162') -> -8.6192... + 2.0100...i  = this table's 10_161 again
    snappy Manifold('10_163') ->  5.1671... + 5.7433...i  = this table's 10_162
    snappy Manifold('10_164') -> -4.7328... + 7.5598...i  = this table's 10_163
    snappy Manifold('10_165') -> -0.5229... + 6.7891...i  = this table's 10_164
    snappy Manifold('10_166') -> -1.7764... + 5.7385...i  = this table's 10_165 mirror

(I ran all of these; the first is the Perko duplicate, giving the same knot
twice under two names.) So somebody holding a cusp shape from SnapPy for any
of five knots will find it on this page under a different name, and nothing on
the page warns them.

Worse, the page contradicts itself without the sentence. The entry comment on
$10_{161}$ reads

> the Perko pair, listed twice by Rolfsen as $10_{161}$ and $10_{162}$

and four rows below it sits a row labelled $10_{162}$ holding an unrelated
number. Both statements are true -- Rolfsen's $10_{162}$ was the duplicate and
this table's $10_{162}$ is Rolfsen's $10_{163}$ -- and a reader has no way to
reconcile them from anything on the page. This is exactly the fault T316 was
critiqued for on 2026-09-18, inherited one table later in a weaker form.

**Smallest fix:** T141 already has the sentence; take it. "The knots Rolfsen
listed as $10_{161}$ and $10_{162}$ are one knot, so there are 165 prime knots
with ten crossings rather than 166, and Rolfsen's $10_{163}$ to $10_{166}$ are
$10_{162}$ to $10_{165}$ here." Two clauses, five rows a reader can otherwise
get wrong.

### 4. Twenty rows print `[0, 0]` where the build proved `0`

Every amphichiral row on the page reads

> [0, 0] + i * 3.46410161513775458705489268301174473388561050762076125611161395890386603381760007416229237

`[0, 0]` is legal -- the skill lists `[2, 2.3728596]` as one of four ways to
write a real -- so nothing is broken. But it says the wrong thing, and it says
it in brackets on twenty of the 466 rows, which is the only place on this page
where a value does not look like a number.

The site's own convention is explicit about what the two spellings mean.
`numberdb_app/test_interval_spelling.py` opens:

> a zero-width interval is written as an interval -- `[3/2, 3/2]`, not `3/2`
> -- so that the page says which of the two things it is: a value declared
> exact, or interval arithmetic that landed on a point.

This is the first of those: a value declared exact. It did *not* land on a
point. SnapPy returns `0.?e-117` for $4_1$'s real part, and the generator
replaces it, deliberately -- `_pure_imaginary(z) = z.parent()(0, z.imag())`,
commit da25f9e -- after checking the enclosure contains zero, because formula
(1) with $\bar K=K$ proves the real part is exactly zero. The build's own
lesson file (`agents/lessons/proposals/20260918T001830Z-build.md`) writes the
reasoning out. Then the value was written in the spelling that denies it.

**Smallest fix:** make the real component exact rather than a zero-width ball,
so `to_text` takes the `int`/`Fraction` branch (`clients/python/numberdb/_write.py:112`)
and writes `0`. The rows then read
`0 + i * 3.46410161513775...`, which is the spelling the skill gives for type
`C` and the claim the generator actually established.

### 6. The Chern–Simons relation states a difference that is not one

> Chern-Simons invariants of the hyperbolic prime knots with at most ten
> crossings — the same hyperbolic knot complements, with mirror images
> distinguished

Every other relation on this page names a *difference* -- "included there and
excluded here", "not distinguished by the Alexander polynomial", "rather than
its complex shape" -- so a reader reads this one as a difference too, and
concludes that T317 does not distinguish mirror images. It does; distinguishing
them is what half its rows are. T313's document carries the identical `knot`
parameter with the identical `K`/`mirror` values, and its 932 rows are these
same 466 under each of its two normalisations.

**Smallest fix:** state the actual difference and drop the clause that isn't
one: "the same 466 rows, knot by knot and mirror by mirror; a real invariant
of the hyperbolic structure rather than a complex invariant of the cusp".

### 10. The only tag is one that no knot table in the corpus carries

`Tags: algebraic`. T141 carries `volume` and `period`, T313 carries `period`,
T316 carries `volume`. So sideways browsing does not connect this table to its
own family in either direction: a reader on T141's or T313's tag pages never
reaches T317, and a reader who clicks `algebraic` from here lands among T35

`algebraic` is also a claim the page never makes anywhere else. It is true --
the cusp shape lies in the cusp field of the complement -- and the table is
good evidence for it: `algdep` at 280 known bits finds $x^2-12$ for $4_1$,

page says the values are algebraic numbers, so the tag asserts on its own.

**Smallest fix:** add `period`, which both T141 and T313 carry, and if
`algebraic` stays, let a comment say the cusp shape lies in the cusp field, so
that the tag rests on something a reader can see.

## Noted only because I noticed

* **Formula (2) singles out $4_1$, and at least two other rows have closed
  forms of the same kind.** $\tau(S^3\setminus8_{18})=i\sqrt{16+32\sqrt2}$ and
  $\tau(S^3\setminus10_{123})=\tfrac52 i\sqrt{10+2\sqrt5}=10i\sin(2\pi/5)$
  both hold to all 90 stored digits -- I confirmed the second in exact decimal
  arithmetic (difference $2.9\times10^{-91}$) and found both by `algdep`.
  These are numerical identifications, not proofs, so I am not suggesting they
  be written as formulas. What I would suggest is that formula (2) say why
  $4_1$ has a formula of its own; as it stands it states a fact and leaves a
  reader holding $7.8265\ldots i$ with no reason to think their value is
  equally clean.
* **The value column is headed $\tau(S^3\setminus K)$ and 223 of its rows hold
  $\tau(S^3\setminus\bar K)$.** T313 does exactly the same, so this is house
  style rather than a slip -- and T313 shows the alternative, since its header
  names both of the conventions *its* bundled parameter selects. Worth one
  character of thought if the header is ever touched:
  $\tau(S^3\setminus K)$ or $\tau(S^3\setminus\bar K)$.
* **"The unknot is left out for the same reason."** The reason just given was
  that the torus knots have Seifert fibred complements. That is true of the
  unknot too, so the sentence is not wrong -- but the unknot is outside a
  table of *prime* knots before hyperbolicity is reached at all, which the
  title already settles. A reader who stops to check the sentence learns
  nothing from it.
* **Comment (6)'s `pmatrix` puts a bare `&` into the page's HTML.** The body
  contains `$\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\ c&d\end{pmatrix}$` unescaped, not `a&amp;b`.
  Browsers recover from `&` followed by no valid entity name, so MathJax gets
  the right text and nothing is visibly wrong -- and it is the site that does
  not escape prose, not this table. Recorded in `docs/agent-environment.md`
  beside the existing note that link titles are not escaped either.
* **"Chern-Simons" in the caption, "Chern–Simons" in the title it copies.** A
  hyphen for an en dash. Only worth a keystroke if finding 6 reopens the line.
* **"neighboring"** is the one American spelling on a page whose sibling T313
  writes "normalisation". Moot if finding 9 is taken, since the word goes.

## The audit's one finding, and why I disagree with it

> parameter knot takes 2 names (K, mirror), each carrying entries of its own,
> so this reads as 2 tables sharing one title and one value column.

The check is right about the shape and wrong about this table, and the rule's
own docstring says which answers are good ones: "if they are one object in
several parts, or one number in several conventions, say so in the definition
and leave them together".

`knot` is not naming a quantity. It is finishing the index. $n$ and $k$ do not
identify a knot -- $n_k$ and $\bar n_k$ are two knots with one Rolfsen name --
so `knot` completes what $n$ and $k$ start, exactly as $k$ completes $n$. One
quantity is listed, $\tau$ of a hyperbolic knot complement, and the value
column is headed with its symbol rather than falling back on the word `value`,
which is the tell the rule itself relies on. The Definition already names the
mirror images ("and their distinct mirror images"), so the escape clause is
satisfied on the page and not only in my reading of it; comment (5) says it
again in so many words. And the two halves are bound by a stated theorem,
formula (1), which two separate tables could not carry between them.

What the check cannot see is that `K` and `mirror` are *arguments spelled as
words*. `_reads_as_a_number` steps aside for `nu = 0, 1, 2` and cannot step
aside here, because the argument happens to be an involution rather than an
integer. T313 -- published, reviewed, and the table this one was copied from
-- has the same parameter with the same two values.

I would leave it as it is.
