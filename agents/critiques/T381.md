# T381, Discriminants of the general polynomial of degree $n$

A critique. Nothing here was changed; somebody decides.

The table is five rows: the discriminant of $\sum_{i=0}^n a_ix^i$ as a
polynomial in $\mathbb Z[a_0,\dots,a_n]$, for $n=1,\dots,5$. It is a good
table. The page renders, the definition defines, the parameter describes the
family and not the run, and the one decision that had to be argued -- why it
stops at degree five -- is argued, correctly, in the completeness note. The
five findings below are small, and two of them are about text a reader meets
somewhere other than the table page: an attached file, and a search box.

## The audit

Clean, and I agree with it: every `CITE{}` resolves -- the four to formula
labels render as `(1)` to `(4)`, which is what they should -- both `Links`
answer, both tags reach other tables, the generator is attached and opens with
its run commands, the value column is headed with a quantity and not the word
`value`, and the one-quantity checks have nothing to say because this is one
quantity of one object under one integer parameter.

Two of its rules came close and stayed silent for reasons worth knowing.

The "family named and not linked" check reads `rigour details` and matches a
table's title in prose; T381 writes $e_{n-i}$ and never the words "elementary
symmetric polynomials", so there is nothing for it to match (finding 2). And
the checks on prose look for editorial phrases and for pointing at a formula
by position; "The source [1]" is neither (finding 5). Neither is a bug in the
audit.

## Worth doing

### 1. The attached generator tells a reader to publish through `agents/sage.sh`

`generate.py` is a tab on the table page and is downloaded by somebody who has
neither this repository nor a way to guess what is in it. Its opening
docstring gives the three Sage commands, correctly, and then:

> On the NumberDB build machine, where arguments are not passed through
> agents/sage.sh, publish with:
>
>     $ cat "$NUMBERDB_KEY_FILE" | NUMBERDB_KEY_FROM_STDIN=1 NUMBERDB_PUBLISH=1 \
>           agents/sage.sh generate.py

None of `agents/sage.sh`, `NUMBERDB_KEY_FILE` or "the NumberDB build machine"
exists on a reader's laptop, and a reader who tries to follow it learns
nothing except that this file was written somewhere else. It is the same
reason the skill gives for keeping `NUMBERDB_ASSISTED_BY` out of a generator:
the file outlives the run.

The body carries the matching machinery -- `_key_from_stdin()`, the
`NUMBERDB_TABLE` environment override, and `fill_draft_once`, which reimports
five private names out of `numberdb._generate` to work around "the client's
empty upsert probe". The last of those is a real workaround and I would leave
it, with its comment; the paragraph above is the part a reader reads first.

**Smallest fix:** delete that paragraph, leaving the three `sage -python`
lines. If the stdin path is worth documenting, one sentence saying what
`NUMBERDB_KEY_FROM_STDIN=1` does, without naming this machine's wrapper.

This is first because it is the only finding that publishes something to a
reader that is not about the mathematics at all.

### 2. $e_{n-i}$ is used undefined, and the table that defines it is not linked

Under "How they were obtained", a reader meets:

> It checks Formula (2) by substituting
> $a_i=a_n(-1)^{n-i}e_{n-i}(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)$ for every stored degree

Nothing on the page says what $e_k$ is. A reader who knows will guess the
elementary symmetric polynomials and be right; a reader who does not has a
symbol and no definition, which the skill forbids in exactly these words
("Define notation where you use it").

The corpus holds them: **T118, "Elementary symmetric polynomials $e_k$"**,
slug `Elementary_symmetric_polynomials`, whose definition is
$e_k(x_1,\dots,x_n)=\sum_{i_1<\dots<i_k}x_{i_1}\cdots x_{i_k}$ -- the same
object, in the same letter. So the fix names the notation and links a family
the corpus holds, in one phrase.

**Smallest fix:** "...by substituting
HREF{Elementary_symmetric_polynomials}[the elementary symmetric polynomials]
of the roots, $a_i=a_n(-1)^{n-i}e_{n-i}(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)$, for every
stored degree". The caption form, not the bare `HREF{...}` after the name,
which is the audit's other rule about links.

### 3. The page gives two different rings for the same entries

Comment (7): "The entry for degree $n$ is written in the variables
$a_0,\dots,a_n$."

`rigour details`, two screens below: "The generator computes exact integer
coefficients in $\mathbb Z[a_0,\dots,a_5]$".

Both are on the page, and they disagree for every row but the last. The stored
values are what comment (7) says -- the degree-2 row is `a1^2 - 4*a0*a2` and
mentions nothing above $a_2$ -- so the provenance note is describing the
generator's fixed six-variable ring, which is an accident of how it was
written rather than a fact about the entries. A reader who notices has to work
out which one to believe.

**Smallest fix:** write $\mathbb Z[a_0,\dots,a_n]$ in `rigour details`.

### 4. The word a reader types to say *which* discriminant is in no indexed field

Somebody holding the fourth row types "quartic discriminant"; holding the
third, "cubic discriminant". The words `quadratic`, `cubic`, `quartic` and
`quintic` are in none of the five fields the text index reads. Those are the
title and `Keywords` (weight A), `Tags` (B), the `Definition` (C) and the
`Comments` (D), and nothing else -- `editing.py:664-667`. The only place
either word reaches the page is `rigour details` ("compares the quadratic and
cubic rows"), which is outside the index; the rest are formula labels the page
prints as `(3)` and `(4)`.

What this costs is position, not the hit, and it is worth being exact about
why. `full_text_query` ANDs the earlier words with a prefix match on the last
(`search.py:608-614`), but neither the dropdown nor the results panel filters
on that query -- both rank with `ts_rank` and keep everything above 0.01
(`views.py:2047-2057`, `search.py:679-683`), and `ts_rank` scores the query's
lexemes without regard to its operators. Live, today:
`/suggestions?term=zzqqzz+discriminant` answers with ten tables while
`term=zzqqzz` answers with none. So T381, once published, will come back for
"quartic discriminant" on the strength of "Discriminants" in its title -- but
so does everything else whose prose mentions a discriminant, and the word that
says which one the reader means adds nothing to the score. Today that query
answers with "Residues of Dedekind zeta functions of quartic fields" and
"Regulators of quartic fields" first, because they match both words and T381
would match one.

**Smallest fix:** add "quadratic discriminant", "cubic discriminant",
"quartic discriminant", "quintic discriminant" to `Keywords`, which is the
same weight as the title. Four phrases, and the table is then the best answer
to the query rather than one of a dozen.

### 5. "The source [1]" points at a citation instead of naming it

Comment (8) reads: "The source [1] gives the same discriminant convention
through the root product in Formula (2)."

A reader has to jump to `Links` to find out that "the source" is Wikipedia's
article on the discriminant, and comes back having learned a name that could
have been in the sentence. The skill's rule is about "the former" and "the
first factor", and this is the same move: the name is shorter than the phrase
pointing at it.

**Smallest fix:** "Wikipedia's article on the discriminant CITE{DiscriminantWiki}
defines it by the root product of Formula (2), which is the convention used
here."

## Noted only, because I noticed

* **Formulas (3) and (4) restate rows 2 and 3.** The quadratic and cubic
  discriminants are written out in the Formulas section and are also two of
  the five entries. They earn part of their place -- these are the two forms
  everybody has met, and displayed LaTeX reads better than
  `a1^2*a2^2 - 4*a0*a2^3 - ...` -- but their other reason for existing is that
  `rigour details` cites them as the things the generator compared against. I
  would leave them.
* **`Programs` imports `sage.all`**; the attached `generate.py` names its
  rings, as the skill asks, and the two disagree about the same
  three-line incantation. A reader on a modular passagemath hits
  `ModuleNotFoundError` on the block that is there to be pasted. Four lines
  would fix it, and nothing is wrong for a reader with full SageMath.
* **No `Similar tables`.** The corpus has no resultant table and no other
  discriminant-of-a-polynomial table, so `Links` pointing at Wikipedia is
  right. T160, "Minimal discriminants of number fields by degree and
  signature", is the only candidate, related by $\operatorname{disc}(f) =
  [\mathcal O_K:\mathbb Z[\alpha]]^2\operatorname{disc}(K)$ for a monic
  integral $f$ defining $K$. That is a true relation and a real one, but it is
  a relation between this table's *specialisations* and that table's entries,
  so I would not insist.
* **"normalized"** in Formula (1) beside "normalisation" elsewhere in the
  corpus. One letter; mentioned only so that nobody else spends a minute on it.

## What I checked hardest and found nothing wrong with

* **The rendering.** The whole page was rebuilt, not just inspected as YAML.
  The `<` inside $\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}$ in Formula (2) reaches the page as
  `&lt;` and eats nothing -- that is the fault that cost a section earlier this
  year. No `CITE-broken` anywhere. The four `CITE{formula-*}` references render
  as `(1)`, `(2)`, `(3)`, `(4)`, and they are right: the page draws Formulas
  before Comments, so the numbers the definition and the provenance note cite
  are the formulas they mean, not the comments. The long provenance note folds
  after its first paragraph. Entry anchors `id="1"` to `id="5"` are there, so
  `HREF{slug#3}` would land on a row. The degree-5 row is one unbroken line of
  1338 characters; that is at the edge of readable, and no worse than the
  Fibonacci polynomial at $n=100$ the skill cites as the house limit.
* **The claim in the completeness note**, because it is a claim about the site
  made inside a table, and if it were false it would be a bad one. It is true.
  `MAX_VARIABLES_FOR_CANONICAL_FORM = 6` in `utils/numbers/polynomial.py` and
  in the shipped client's copy, `clients/python/numberdb/_polynomial.py:54`;
  beyond six variables `canonical_under_renaming` raises rather than running a
  factorial search, and nothing catches it. A degree-6 discriminant has seven
  variables and would have no search key at all. The degree-5 entry, at six
  variables, canonicalises in 0.09 s to a 1565-character key. So the range
  stops exactly where the value would stop being findable, which is the right
  reason and is the one given.
* **Whether a reader holding one of these is served.** Polynomial search is
  invariant under renaming the variables, so the schoolbook $b^2-4ac$ finds
  this table: `parse_polynomial('b^2-4*a*c').canonical_text()` equals the key
  of the stored `a1^2 - 4*a0*a2`, checked. That is the best thing about this
  table and it needs no change -- it is also why finding 4 is worth doing and
  not more than that: the reader who arrives with the polynomial in hand is
  already served, and only the reader typing words is not.
* **Whether it is one table.** One parameter, one quantity, no parameter whose
  values are names, no `param-latex`, a value column headed
  $\operatorname{disc}(f)$. Yes.
* **Parameter against run.** `n` is "$n\geq 1$", the family; the five degrees
  actually stored are in `complete: no` with a note. That is the split the
  skill asks for, done correctly, and it is worth saying so: it is the thing
  most often got wrong.
