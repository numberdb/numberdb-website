## Findings, ranked

### 1. The normalisations are called by their keys in the prose and by other labels in the table (worth doing)

The table labels its rows $\alpha_{s,s}=1$ and $\beta_s=1$. Everywhere
else, the page calls the same two things <code>alpha-s-one</code> and
<code>nabla</code>, set in code type. That happens in the parameter list,
three of the four formulas, three of the four comments and a
Similar-tables relation. `formula-normalisations` then adds a third
notation, $\alpha^{(1)}_{s,j}$ and $\alpha^{\nabla}_{s,j}$, which appears
nowhere else on the page. A reader can guess that "alpha-s-one" means
$\alpha_{s,s}=1$. Nothing on the screen says that "nabla" means
$\beta_s=1$, except the backward-difference sum in `comment-notation`, and
that sum uses $\nabla$ without defining it. The skill says it directly:
never name a parameter by its key in prose. The labels are already good,
so the prose should use them.

Smallest fix: replace each "the <code>alpha-s-one</code> normalisation" or
"row" with "the normalisation $\alpha_{s,s}=1$", and do the same for
<code>nabla</code> and $\beta_s=1$. Write `formula-normalisations` without
superscripts:

> The coefficients with $\alpha_{s,s}=1$ are those with $\beta_s=1$ divided
> by $H_s$, and in that normalisation $\beta_s=1/H_s$.

In `comment-notation`, define the symbol where it is used: "where
$\nabla y_{m}=y_{m}-y_{m-1}$". The parameter's constraint can read
"$\alpha_{s,s}=1$ (as in Wikipedia) or $\beta_s=1$".

### 2. The Definition does not fix the scale, and names normalisations it has not introduced (worth doing)

> For $1\leq s\leq6$, the backward differentiation formula coefficients are
> the rationals $\alpha_{s,j}$ and $\beta_s$ in
> $\sum_{j=0}^{s}\alpha_{s,j}y_{n+j}=h\beta_s f(t_{n+s},y_{n+s})$, the
> zero-stable $s$-step BDF method of order $s$ [1]. The two normalisations
> determine one another.

Three things go wrong here.

- **Order $s$ fixes the coefficients only up to a common factor.** The scale
  is fixed by the normalisations, and the Definition never states them. Two
  people building from it could get different numbers.
- **"The two normalisations"** points back at something the reader has
  not yet met.
- **The phrase after the formula is an appositive to an equation**, and
  "zero-stable" in it reads as if zero-stability picked out the method.
  Order $s$ is what picks it out. Zero-stability is what limits $s$ (see
  finding 3).

Smallest fix:

> The backward differentiation formula (BDF) coefficients are the rationals
> $\alpha_{s,j}$ and $\beta_s$ for which
> $\sum_{j=0}^{s}\alpha_{s,j}y_{n+j}=h\beta_s f(t_{n+s},y_{n+s})$ is the
> $s$-step method of order $s$ CITE{Wiki}. That method is unique up to a
> common factor, and the table holds it in two normalisations,
> $\alpha_{s,s}=1$ and $\beta_s=1$.

### 3. The range is stated as the family, and the reason for it reads circularly (worth doing, small)

The Definition opens "For $1\leq s\leq6$", and the parameter's constraint
says the same. `comment-range` ends "the table therefore stops at the six
methods that are used as BDF methods". That reads as circular: BDF methods
are used as BDF methods. BDF methods with $s\geq7$ exist and have order
$s$. They are just not zero-stable. So $s\leq6$ describes which part of
the family was worth computing, and that is what `complete-note` is for.
Whoever built the table may reasonably answer that "the BDF methods" means
the six usable ones. If so, the Definition should say "zero-stable" as the
condition, not as an appositive.

Smallest fix, if the family is all $s$: set the constraint to $s\geq1$,
drop "For $1\leq s\leq6$" from the Definition, and set `complete: no` with
the note "it holds the six zero-stable methods, $1\leq s\leq6$, which are
the ones used in practice". `comment-range` can then keep only its first
clause.

### 4. A reader holding a nabla-row number is not told what it most likely is (worth doing)

The $\beta_s=1$ rows, such as $\tfrac12,-2,\tfrac32$ or
$\tfrac14,-\tfrac43,3,-4,\tfrac{25}{12}$, are the one-sided (backward)
finite-difference weights for $y'(t_{n+s})$ with accuracy of order $s$.
Wikipedia's "Finite difference coefficient" page tabulates the forward
weights, and these are those weights in reverse order with the signs
changed. That page is where most people meet these numbers. A reader who
came from a stencil computation finds a page about stiff ODE solvers, and
nothing on the page connects the two. The Central-finite-difference
relation half says it ("with BDF using the one-sided stencil at the newest
node"), but it is on the Similar tables line, not in a comment, and it
does not say which rows. `Keywords` has no "finite difference".

Smallest fix: add one sentence to `comment-notation`:

> The coefficients with $\beta_s=1$ are the backward finite-difference
> weights for $y'(t_{n+s})$ of order $s$.

Also add "backward finite difference coefficients" to `Keywords`.

### 5. $H_s$ is used before it is defined, and it is defined with "If" (worth doing, small)

`formula-nabla` says "$\alpha_{s,s}=H_s$". The definition of $H_s$ comes
one formula later, as the condition of `formula-normalisations`: "If
$H_s=\sum_{k=1}^{s}1/k$, then …". Smallest fix: write
$\alpha_{s,s}=H_s=\sum_{k=1}^{s}1/k$ in `formula-nabla`, and start the next
formula at its claim.

### 6. `comment-normalising` ends with a sentence a reader cannot resolve (worth doing, small)

> Other coefficients belonging to the method are stored, including
> coefficients whose value is $1$ only in a special case.

The reader has to work out which coefficients are meant. They are $\beta_1=1$
in the normalisation $\alpha_{s,s}=1$, and $\alpha_{1,1}=H_1=1$ in the
normalisation $\beta_s=1$. Smallest fix: name them, or delete the sentence.
The first sentence already says which rows are left out, and so it implies
every other row is there.

### 7. The entry comments are fragments, and the $s=1$ one shows $\tfrac{1}{1}$ (small)

> Backward Euler method, from newest to oldest: $\tfrac{1}{1}(1,-1)$, with
> $\beta_1=1$.
>
> 3-step BDF formula, from newest to oldest: $\tfrac{1}{11}(11,-18,9,-2)$,
> with $\beta_3=\tfrac{6}{11}$.

The whole method shown at the head of each block is worth having. A reader
has to guess, though, that the tuple lists coefficients of
$y_{n+s},\ldots,y_n$, and $\tfrac11$ looks like a template artefact. The
$\beta$ it quotes is also the row directly below. Smallest fix: write the
method as a sentence, for example "This is the backward Euler method,
$y_{n+1}-y_n=hf_{n+1}$." and "This is BDF3,
$y_{n+3}-\tfrac{18}{11}y_{n+2}+\tfrac{9}{11}y_{n+1}-\tfrac{2}{11}y_n=\tfrac{6}{11}hf_{n+3}$."
Each equation is written newest first, so `comment-ordering` still
explains it.

### 8. `formula-lagrange` does not link the Lagrange table at its first mention in Formulas (small)

"the Lagrange basis polynomial for the nodes $0,1,\ldots,s$" is plain
text. The notation agrees with the linked table (see above), so the link
is safe:
`HREF{Lagrange_basis_polynomials_for_equally_spaced_nodes}[Lagrange basis polynomial]`.

### 9. Two Similar-tables relations do not say what they mean (small)

- **Adams–Moulton:** "using values of $f$ rather than backward differences
  of $y$". BDF combines *values* of $y$ in its own stored form. The
  contrast that is true is that Adams–Moulton combines several values of
  $f$ with two of $y$, and BDF several of $y$ with one of $f$.
- **Adams–Bashforth:** "using explicit values of $f$". "Explicit values" is
  not a term. "the explicit counterpart, combining past values of $f$" is
  what is meant.
