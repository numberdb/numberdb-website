## Worth doing

### 1. The definition never says over which field

The Definition renders as

> Let $X\subset\mathbb P^n$ be a smooth complete intersection of multidegree
> $(d_1,\dots,d_k)$ [1]. The entry is the topological Euler characteristic
> $\chi(X)$ [2]; it depends only on $n$ and the multidegree.

$\mathbb P^n$ over what? The entry for $n=2$, $d=(2)$ is $2$, which is the
Euler characteristic of a smooth conic over $\mathbb C$, a sphere. Over
$\mathbb R$ the same equation gives a circle and $\chi=0$; the first row of the
table is the row where the two answers differ. "Topological Euler
characteristic" tells a reader who already knows the convention which one is
meant, and tells a reader who came holding the number nothing — and the skill's
test for a definition is that two people would build the same table from it.
Two people would not.

The smallest fix is one subscript: `$X\subset\mathbb P^n_{\mathbb C}$`, or
"a smooth complex complete intersection". T332 omits the field in the same
sentence and should get the same word, though it matters less there, because a
Chern class in $\mathbb Z[h]$ is not something a reader computes over
$\mathbb R$ by accident.

### 2. The completeness note says what the range is and not why

It renders as

> Table is complete: no (it holds every row with $n\leq 10$, every $d_i\leq
> 10$, and $\prod_i d_i\leq 100$)

That is what was computed. The skill asks the note to carry the argument for
the range, because it is the sentence a reader checking whether their own
number belongs here actually reads — and T332, built from the same grid in the
same batch, has that argument:

> ...; the product is the degree of $X$, so the table includes every complete
> intersection of degree at most $100$ in this grid

A reader of T333 is left to work out for themselves that $\prod_i d_i$ is the
degree of $X$ and that the third bound is therefore a bound on the degree and
not an arbitrary cut. Nothing else on the page says it either. The fix is to
carry T332's clause over verbatim; the two notes should read alike anyway,
since they describe the same 999 rows.

### 3. The Calabi–Yau rows are named one at a time, and one of them is missed

Seven entries carry comments. Four of them name Calabi–Yau threefolds:

    n=4  (5)         -200   Quintic threefold.
    n=5  (2,4)       -176   Calabi-Yau threefold of type $(2,4)$.
    n=5  (3,3)       -144   Calabi-Yau threefold of type $(3,3)$.
    n=7  (2,2,2,2)   -128   Complete intersection of four quadrics; a
                            Calabi-Yau threefold.

There are five complete-intersection Calabi–Yau threefolds, and the fifth,
$n=6$, $d=(2,2,3)$, is in the table at $\chi=-144$ with no comment at all. So a
reader who searches $-144$, or scrolls to it, meets two rows holding that
number, one labelled a Calabi–Yau threefold and one bare, with no way to tell
that they are the same kind of object. The quintic's comment is also the one
that does not use the phrase, and `Keywords` promises "Calabi-Yau complete
intersection Euler characteristic" to a searcher.

Naming them one at a time cannot be finished: 53 of the 999 rows satisfy
$\sum_i d_i = n+1$ and are Calabi–Yau, not five. T332 solves this in one
sentence, in `comment-c1`:

> the rows with $a_1=0$ are exactly those with $\sum_i d_i=n+1$; for complete
> intersections this is the Calabi-Yau condition, with trivial canonical class

The fix is the same sentence here, adapted: a comment saying that the rows with
$\sum_i d_i=n+1$ are exactly the Calabi–Yau complete intersections, so that
every one of the 53 is named rather than four of them. That needs `CYWiki` in
`Links`, which T332 already declares. Keeping the four entry comments beside it
is fine — they name the famous ones — and the $(2,2,3)$ in $\mathbb P^6$ row
should get one too, or none of them should.

### 4. Why the multidegree stops at $k=n-1$ is unexplained here, and explained in T332

The parameter renders as

> $(d_1,\dots,d_k)$ — multidegree (a nonempty nondecreasing list of integers at
> least $2$, with $1\leq k\leq n-1$)

and Comment (4) explains one of the two bounds:

> Degree-one equations are omitted: a linear equation only replaces
> $\mathbb P^n$ by a smaller projective space, so it gives the same variety
> under another address.

T332's comment of the same key is word-for-word this one plus a second
sentence, which T333's copy has lost:

> Taking $k=n$ is excluded for a different reason: $X$ is then a finite set of
> points and $c(T_X)=1$.

So a reader of T333 is told why $d_i\geq 2$ and not why $k\leq n-1$, and
$k\leq n-1$ is the bound that excludes a whole dimension of the family. The fix
is one sentence, with the Euler characteristic in place of the Chern class:
taking $k=n$ is excluded because $X$ is then $\prod_i d_i$ points and
$\chi(X)$ is its degree.

### 7. "The same grid is used by ..." points at something the page never names

Comment (6) renders as

> The same grid is used by [the complete-intersection Chern-class table].

"Grid" appears nowhere else on this page — T332 uses the word in its own
completeness note, but a reader here has not read that. The claim itself is
exactly true: I compared the two documents' keys and the 999 $(n,d)$ pairs are
the same set, which is worth saying. It is also most of what `Similar tables`

multidegree in that table. If it goes, nothing is lost.

### 8. "under another address" is the site's word, not the mathematics'

In Comment (4), "it gives the same variety under another address" means: the
same variety would appear as a row with a different $n$ and multidegree.
"Address" is how this corpus talks about slugs and entry keys, and a reader
meeting it inside a sentence about linear equations has to guess. "as a row
with a smaller $n$" says it in the table's own terms. T332 has the same
sentence and the same remark applies to it.

### 9. $m=n-k$ is defined twice, and the second time is after it was used

Formula (1) opens "If $m=n-k$, then ..." and Comment (3) says "If $k$ is the
length of the multidegree, then $\dim X=m=n-k$." The page puts `Formulas`
before `Comments`, so the formula defines its own $m$ and the comment repeats
it. The content the comment adds is $\dim X=m$; it would read better as a
statement of that, "The dimension of $X$ is $m=n-k$, where $k$ is the length of
the multidegree." Both also use "If ... then" where nothing is conditional.

### 10. The Sage snippet imports `numberdb` and never uses it

`Programs` opens with `import numberdb.sage as numberdb`, and the twenty lines
that follow never mention the name. I know why it is there — it initialises
Sage, so the `from sage.rings...` imports below it work under `sage -python` —
but `Programs` is the incantation for a reader who wants one more value, and
this one asks that reader to install the numberdb package to compute a number
that has nothing to do with the database. Either a comment on the line saying
what it does, as the skill's own generator template carries, or drop it and let
