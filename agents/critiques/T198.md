## Findings, ranked

### 1. The definition does not say what an Airy function is

The definition reads: "This table gives the standard real values of
the Airy functions $\operatorname{Ai}(x)$ and $\operatorname{Bi}(x)$,
and of their first derivatives $\operatorname{Ai}'(x)$ and
$\operatorname{Bi}'(x)$ [1] [2], for rational real arguments $x$."
Airy's equation appears nowhere on the page. The word "standard" is
carrying the definition, and what it stands for is in comment (3),
after 244 rows: "The normalisation is the standard pair of solutions of
Airy's differential equation in [2]", a sentence whose grammar is off
(a normalisation is not a pair of solutions) and which still does not
write the equation down. Two people reading this page could build
different tables only if they did not know Airy functions already, but
the skill's test is exactly that reader.

Smallest change: make the definition the defining sentence and let the
comment keep what is a property. Something like: "$\operatorname{Ai}$
and $\operatorname{Bi}$ are the standard pair of real solutions of
Airy's equation $w''=xw$, with the initial values in
CITE{formula-initial-values} CITE{DLMFAiryEquation}. The table holds
$\operatorname{Ai}(x)$, $\operatorname{Bi}(x)$, $\operatorname{Ai}'(x)$
and $\operatorname{Bi}'(x)$ at rational $x$." The initial values pin
the pair exactly, which "standard" does not. Comment (3) then loses its
first sentence and keeps the decay, growth and oscillation, which is a
fact about the mathematics and reads well. "Rational real arguments"
should be "rational arguments"; a rational is real.

### 2. Two keywords the page never explains

`Keywords` carries "Tracy-Widom distribution" and "WKB turning point".
Neither phrase, nor anything about random matrices or semiclassical
turning points, appears anywhere a reader can see. Somebody who types
"Tracy-Widom" is brought to a table of $\operatorname{Ai}(a/b)$ that
does not mention it, and the values they hold are not these ones: the
Tracy–Widom distribution is built from the Airy kernel and a Painlevé
II solution, not from Airy values at rationals. The same fault was
named on T197 in this batch. Drop the two keywords, or, if the
connection is wanted, write one sentence in a comment saying where Airy
functions arise and let the keyword point at that sentence. Dropping is
the smaller change and the honest one.

### 3. The parameter line reads "function — function"

The key, the title and the display of the first parameter are all the
word "function", so the parameter list's first line says nothing. The
second line, "$x$ — argument ($x\in\mathbb{Q}$)", shows what the first
should look like. Smallest change: a title that names what the reader
chooses, for instance "the function evaluated, one of
$\operatorname{Ai}$, $\operatorname{Ai}'$, $\operatorname{Bi}$,
$\operatorname{Bi}'$". Worth doing; it is one field.

### 4. A sentence of prose inside formula (1)

Formula (1) ends: "The Gamma values are in $\Gamma(1/3)$ and
$\Gamma(2/3)$", with the two symbols linking to rows of the Gamma
table. It is a good pair of links, but it is a sentence, and Formulas
hold identities rather than prose about them. Its home is comment (4),
which already says the four entries at $x=0$ are Gamma values and
points at (1); moving the sentence there costs nothing. The Gamma table
is also in Similar tables, so after the move it is still named twice,
which is right: once where a reader meets the values and once in the
list. Noting only; a person may prefer the links beside the formula.

### 5. Four functions in one table: a decision, not a fault

The nearest precedent is this table's own siblings: T55 to T58 are four
tables for the zeros of exactly these four functions, and T197 in this
batch was critiqued today for holding six functions under one title.
The case for one table here is stronger than it was there.
$\operatorname{Ai}$ and $\operatorname{Bi}$ are defined as a pair by
one equation, the Wronskian in formula (2) needs all four columns from
the same table, and every one of the four is findable by name from the
title and the keywords, which was the skill's concern. The cost is what
it always is: the definition comes after 244 rows, and a reader
arriving at $\operatorname{Bi}'(1)$ meets it on row 195. I would leave
the table as it is, and I am writing this down so that whoever decides
T197 sees the two cases side by side rather than as one rule applied
twice.

### 6. The Bessel glosses under Similar tables

The gloss on the $J_\nu$ table, "Airy functions at negative arguments
are related to Bessel functions of orders $1/3$ and $2/3$", is true
and vague: the relation is DLMF 9.6.6, $\operatorname{Ai}(-x)=
\tfrac{\sqrt x}{3}\bigl(J_{1/3}(\zeta)+J_{-1/3}(\zeta)\bigr)$ with
$\zeta=\tfrac23 x^{3/2}$, and the page never states it. The gloss on
the $Y_\nu$ table, "gives the companion Bessel solution appearing in
the connection formulas for negative Airy arguments", is a stretch:
DLMF writes those formulas with $J_{\pm1/3}$ and $J_{\pm2/3}$, and
$Y_\nu$ enters only after rewriting $J_{-\nu}$ through $J_\nu$ and
$Y_\nu$. Smallest change: add the connection formula for
$\operatorname{Ai}(-x)$ and $\operatorname{Bi}(-x)$ to Formulas and
let both glosses say "the Bessel functions in formula (3)". Noting
only; the links do no harm as they stand.
