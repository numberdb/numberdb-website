document and 1654 characters of stored values, the largest being $(m,n)=(3,3)$
at 476 characters. Nothing was changed.

the convention, the range and the reason for the range all hold up under

the one real source on the page twelve to one. That is finding 1 and it is the

## 3. `comment-order` puts an identity in `Comments` and does not say the thing a reader needs — worth doing

As rendered, Comment (2) reads in full:

> The resultant is ordered: $\operatorname{Res}(g_n,f_m)=(-1)^{mn}
> \operatorname{Res}(f_m,g_n)$ after the two polynomials are interchanged.

Three things a reader has to guess. **"The resultant is ordered"** — ordered by
what, into what order? The sentence names no order and the identity that
follows is not one. **"after the two polynomials are interchanged"** describes
something the left-hand side has already done, so it reads as though the
identity holds only once some further step is taken; a reader cannot tell
whether "after" is a condition or a restatement. And the fact that is missing
is the one the comment exists for: **which polynomial's roots the product runs
over**, and therefore that the rows $(1,2)$ and $(2,1)$ are two different
entries rather than the table saying one thing twice.

That last point is not cosmetic. The table stores both antidiagonal halves —
$(1,2)$ is `a0^2 - a0*b1 + b0` and $(2,1)$ is `-a1*b0 + b0^2 + a0` — and a
reader who does not understand the ordering convention will read that as a
duplication.

The same convention is named a second time, in a second word, in the
Definition: "the resultant $\operatorname{Res}(f_m,g_n)$ [1], **normalized** by
Formula (1)". For *monic* $f_m$ and $g_n$ there is no leading-coefficient
factor to normalize — the general $\operatorname{lc}(f)^{\deg g}$ is $1$ here,
which is why Formula (1) can write the bare product. What Formula (1) actually
fixes is the ordering, and it matters exactly because interchanging the two
multiplies by $(-1)^{mn}$. So the Definition calls it a normalization and the
comment calls it an order, and a reader meeting both may reasonably think the
table has two conventions to keep track of. (T381's "normalization" is a real
one, $a_n^{\deg g}$; the word looks carried across from the table where it fit.)

Separately, the skill's section table puts identities in `Formulas` and keeps
formulas out of `Comments`.

**Smallest change**, two edits that also merge the two words into one:

- `Formulas`, a sixth entry beside the others:
  `$\operatorname{Res}(g_n,f_m)=(-1)^{mn}\operatorname{Res}(f_m,g_n)$.`
- `comment-order`: "The order of the two polynomials matters. The entry is the
  product of $g_n$ over the roots of $f_m$, as in Formula CITE{formula-roots};
  interchanging them multiplies the result by $(-1)^{mn}$, so the entries at
  $(m,n)$ and $(n,m)$ are different polynomials."
- Definition: "…the resultant $\operatorname{Res}(f_m,g_n)$ CITE{ResultantWiki},
  with the product taken over the roots of $f_m$ as in Formula
  CITE{formula-roots}."

I checked the convention against the stored values before proposing this
wording, on the two rows where it is checkable by hand: $f_1=x+a_0$ has root
$-a_0$, and $g_2(-a_0)=a_0^2-a_0b_1+b_0$, which is the stored $(1,2)$; and
$\prod(\alpha_i+b_0)=a_0-a_1b_0+b_0^2$ over the roots of $f_2$, which is the
stored $(2,1)$. The table does what Formula (1) says.

## 5. The Sylvester matrix is used three times and never explained — worth doing

## 6. `Programs` is the shape T383 was repaired out of yesterday — worth doing

The snippet opens

    from sage.rings.integer_ring import ZZ
    from sage.rings.polynomial.polynomial_ring_constructor import PolynomialRing

and the name `numberdb` is never used again. `Programs` is "the standard

`from sage.all import ZZ, PolynomialRing` with a closing `print(disc)`; the

rule T384 broke. T381 also uses `from sage.all import PolynomialRing, ZZ`.
T384, built after all of them, is the only one of the four on the

Second, smaller: the last line is a bare `f.resultant(g)`. That prints in a
Sage REPL and prints nothing under `sage -python`. T383's repaired snippet ends

## 7. The audit is clean, and I agree with what it checked

`GET /api/table/T384/audit` returns `{"findings": [], "clean": true}`. Every
mechanical check it ran passes and should: both `HREF` targets resolve, every
`CITE` key exists, the value header is a symbol rather than `value`, neither
parameter takes name-like values, and `complete`/`complete-note` are both
present.

The silence worth naming is §2. **Nothing mechanical distinguishes an
off-topic GitHub issue from a source.** Twelve links to
`github.com/numberdb/numberdb-data/issues/N` are well-formed, point at a
repository that exists, and answer 200. An audit that fetched them would still
pass them. The rule they break is about what a link is *for*, and that is a
judgement, which is why it reached a reader instead of a checker. Two rules the
audit could mechanise are worth considering by whoever owns it: a link in
`Links` that no prose cites, and a link whose host is the data repository's
issue tracker.

Note also that `--links` is a flag on `manage.py audit_table` and cannot be
asked for over `/api/table/<tid>/audit`, so this run did not fetch the declared
links through the audit at all. I fetched all thirteen by hand instead: the
twelve issue titles from the GitHub API, and
`https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resultant`, which answers 200 without a
redirect and is the right article.

## 8. What I checked hardest and found sound

Reported because a critique that only lists faults is not a reading.

- **Is it one table?** Yes, and unambiguously. Both parameters are *degrees* —
  numbers, not names — and neither repeats under a handful of words. The value
  column is headed `$\operatorname{Res}(f_m,g_n)$`, which is true of every row;
  the table never falls back on `value`. There is nothing here to split.
- **Do the parameters describe the family or the run?** The family: `$m\geq1$`
  and `$n\geq1$`, with no trace of the afternoon's grid. The run lives where it
  belongs, in `complete: no` with a note that finishes the sentence: *"it holds
  every pair $m,n\geq1$ with $m+n\leq6$; the next antidiagonal would use seven
  coefficient variables, more than polynomial search accepts."*
- **Is that reason true?** Yes, and it is the site's own number. The
  renaming-invariant search key is found by trying permutations of the
  variables, and `utils/numbers/polynomial.py` sets
  `MAX_VARIABLES_FOR_CANONICAL_FORM = 6`, raising `ParseError('too many
  variables for a renaming-invariant key')` above it. An entry at $(m,n)$ is
  written in exactly $m+n$ variables, so the bound $m+n\leq6$ *is* the search
  constraint rather than a count somebody liked. This is the good version of
  what the skill asks for — a range chosen by the mathematics of the corpus and
  not by a target size — and it answers the obvious reader's question, "why only
  fifteen rows?", in the one line a reader would look at.
- **Would somebody holding one of these numbers be served?** Yes, in the first
  screen. The entries block renders first, headed `$m$ | $n$ |
  $\operatorname{Res}(f_m,g_n)$`, and the rows run in the order the document
  writes them — $m$ ascending, $n$ ascending inside it — so $(1,1)$,
  `-a0 + b0`, is the first row on the page and the large $(3,3)$ is buried
  where it belongs. Polynomial search is renaming-invariant, so a reader whose
  variables are called $p$ and $q$ still matches.
- **Are the claims facts about the mathematics?** Every sentence of the
  Definition, Comments and Formulas is. There is no remark anywhere about how
  distinctive a value is or what a search would return. The one sentence about
  the website, "more than polynomial search accepts", is in `complete-note`,
  where the skill asks for the reason a range was chosen, and it is a fact
  about the index rather than an apology for the table.
- **Do the `Similar tables` relations hold?** "uses the same resultant
  normalization" of T381 is true: T381's Formula (1) is
  $\operatorname{Res}(f,g)=a_n^{\deg g}\prod_{f(\alpha)=0}g(\alpha)$, the same
  product over the roots of the first argument that T384's Formula (1) takes
  with $a_n=1$. It is also honest in what it does *not* claim — T381's
  discriminant is a resultant of $f$ with $f'$, and $f'$ is not monic, so
  T384's entries do not specialise to T381's and the relation is stated as a
  shared convention rather than a shared value.
- **Size.** 15 entries, 1654 characters of values, largest 476. Nothing is
  near any limit, and nothing needs to be.

## 9. Noted only because I noticed

- **`comment-coefficients` opens by restating the Definition.** The Definition
  already writes $f_m(x)=x^m+\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}a_i x^i$, which says that $a_i$
  multiplies $x^i$; the comment's first sentence says it again. Its second
  sentence — which variables the $(m,n)$ entry is written in — is the one that
  adds something. (In T381 the same opening earns its place, because there the
  polynomial is a bare $\sum a_i x^i$ and the comment goes on to say which end
  is the leading coefficient.) Dropping the first sentence would cost nothing.
- **"the next antidiagonal"** in `complete-note` makes a reader work out which
  pairs are meant. "the pairs with $m+n=7$" names them, is shorter, and stays
  right if the range ever moves. This is the smallest possible instance of
  "name the thing, do not point at it" and I would not spend a revision on it
  alone.
- **`rigour details` says the coefficients are computed "in polynomial rings
  over $\mathbb Z$ from the resultant"**, which is circular — it computes the
  resultant from the resultant. What it means is Sage's `resultant`, as the
  `Programs` snippet shows. T381's version of the sentence names its ring,
  $\mathbb Z[a_0,\dots,a_n]$, where this one says "polynomial rings over
  $\mathbb Z$"; $\mathbb Z[a_0,\dots,a_{m-1},b_0,\dots,b_{n-1}]$ would say it.
- **T382 is not among the `Similar tables`** while T381 and T383 are. I looked
  at whether this is a gap and concluded it is not worth reporting as one:
  three siblings is already a lot to name, T381 is the general case both others
  specialise, and nothing is broken. Recording it so the next reader does not
  re-derive the question.
