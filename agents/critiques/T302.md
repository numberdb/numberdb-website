## The audit's finding

I disagree that it needs action. This is the good kind of answer: one number
in two conventions. $C_{n,j}=w_{n,j}/n$, and $n$ is in the row, so each value
gives back the other. A reader holding $7/90$ (Boole's rule on $[0,1]$) and a
reader holding $14/45$ (the same rule with unit step) should land in the same
place. The Definition already does what the finding asks for. It defines
$w_{n,j}$ outright, then says "The table gives the same weights in two
invertible normalisations", and `formula-cotes` states the relation. The
value header "$w_{n,j}$ or $C_{n,j}$" is the column's own tell. Here it is
honest, and the per-row labels `$w_{n,j}$:` and `$C_{n,j}$:` are the right use
of `values` on a parameter whose values are words. The finding will stay on a
draft that has already answered it (see the T278 proposal of 2026-09-16). It
is a question for the reviewer, not a fault.

The audit misses findings 1 to 4 below. None is a pattern it looks for.

## What reads well

The Definition defines and then stops. The quadrature identity, the node set
and the exactness degree are enough for two people to build the same table.
The parameters describe the family ($n\geq1$, $0\leq j\leq n$), and the range
of this run is in `complete-note`. The Formulas are formulas, and what was
checked is in `rigour details`, where it belongs. A reader who searched a
weight such as $64/45$ lands on a row labelled $n=4$, $j=1$, $w_{n,j}$, under
the title "Newton–Cotes weights". The rule it came from is named or written
out on that rule's first row. That serves somebody arriving with a number.

## Findings, ranked

### 1. The prose names rows `step` and `unit-interval`, and the page never shows those words (worth doing)

A reader sees rows labelled $w_{n,j}$: and $C_{n,j}$:. The prose then refers
to them by their parameter keys:

- `comment-step`: "The `step` rows use spacing $h=1$. … The `unit-interval`
  rows divide the same weights by $n$".
- `comment-ordering`: "in the same order as $f(0),\ldots,f(n)$ in the step
  form".
- `complete-note`, which renders as "Table is complete: no (it holds every
  closed rule with $1\leq n\leq 12$, in both the step and unit-interval
  normalisations)".
- The parameter list: "normalisation — normalisation (either `step` or
  `unit-interval`)". Its title only repeats the display name, and its
  constraint names keys.

No row on the page carries either word, so "the `step` rows" leaves a reader
matching a code word against labels that are symbols. The skill says: never
name a parameter by its key in prose; use its display symbol.

Smallest fix:
- `comment-step`: "The rows $w_{n,j}$ use spacing $h=1$. … The rows $C_{n,j}$
  divide the same weights by $n$, giving …".
- `comment-ordering`: drop "in the step form" (see finding 6).
- `complete-note`: "it holds every closed rule with $1\leq n\leq 12$, both as
  $w_{n,j}$ and as $C_{n,j}$".
- Parameter `title`: something that says what the choice is, for example
  "normalisation: unit step or unit interval". Where the constraint names the
  keys, it could name the symbols instead.

### 2. Each rule is split across alternating rows, so it cannot be read down the page (worth doing now or never)

The nesting is $n$, then $j$, then normalisation. On the page, Simpson's rule
reads 1/3, 1/6, 4/3, 2/3, 1/3, 1/6, with the labels switching between
$w_{n,j}$ and $C_{n,j}$ on every row. For $n=12$ that is 26 alternating rows.
Every place in the table that shows a rule shows it as a vector:
`comment-reduced` gives $\tfrac{2}{45}(7,32,12,32,7)$, and so does each first
row's comment. The rows are the one place a reader cannot read it that way. A
reader wanting to check "is the fourth weight of the $n=6$ rule really
$27/140$?" has to count every other row.

Nesting as $n$, then normalisation, then $j$ would show $w_{4,0},\ldots,w_{4,4}$
in one run and $C_{4,0},\ldots,C_{4,4}$ below it. The row a reader arrives at
would sit among its own rule. The table is a draft, so the parameter order is
still free. After publishing, it is frozen with the anchors (`4,1,step` would
become `4,step,1`). The change goes in the generator: the order of
`Parameters` and the nesting of `Numbers` must change together, because the
site does not check that they match. If `comment-ordering` survives, its
sentence about order becomes true of what a reader sees, which it only half
is now. This is a judgement about reading, not a rule in the skill. The
builder may prefer the pairing of $w$ with $C$, but it is worth deciding
before publication rather than inheriting.

### 3. `comment-negative` says the rules "stop being positive", and the next rule is positive (worth doing)

> The closed equally spaced rules stop being positive: the first negative
> weights in this range occur for $n=8$, where $w_{8,2}=w_{8,6}=-3712/14175$.
> The rules with $n=10,11,12$ also have negative weights.

"Stop being positive" means they stay that way, but every weight of the $n=9$
rule is positive: $\tfrac{9}{89600}(2857,15741,1080,19344,5778,\ldots)$. The
reader who looks at the rows below finds this out. A reader who does not is
misled. "In this range" also hedges a fact that is not about the range.
$n=8$ is the first closed rule with a negative weight, and the table starts at
$n=1$. The sentence is only right if a reader works out that $n=9$ was left
out on purpose.

Smallest fix: "The rule with $n=8$ is the first with negative weights,
$w_{8,2}=w_{8,6}=-3712/14175$ and $w_{8,4}=-3632/2835$. The rule with $n=9$
has positive weights, and the rules with $n=10,11,12$ have negative weights
again." The classical result is that every closed rule with $n\geq10$ has a
negative weight. If the builder has a source for it, cite it and state it
instead of the list.

### 4. Similar tables uses $n$ for Gauss–Legendre in the other table's sense (worth doing, one word)

> Nodes and weights of Gauss–Legendre quadrature — the interpolatory rule with
> $n$ free nodes, exact to the highest possible degree

On this page $n$ is the number of subintervals, so the $n$-th rule has $n+1$
nodes. In the linked table $n$ is the number of nodes. A reader will pair the
$n$-th row here with the $n$-th rule there, and those rules have different
numbers of nodes. Smallest fix: "the interpolatory rule with free nodes, exact
to the highest possible degree".

### 5. The title does not say "closed" (worth considering)

The Definition holds only the closed rules, which use both endpoints. The open
Newton–Cotes rules, such as the midpoint rule and Milne's rule, are also
Newton–Cotes weights and are not here. "Newton–Cotes weights" usually means
the closed rules, so this is a mild overclaim rather than a wrong title. The
cost of fixing it is small, and it keeps the title free for an open-rule
table later: "Weights of the closed Newton–Cotes rules", with "Newton–Cotes
weights" moved into `Keywords`.

### 6. Noting only

- **`comment-ordering` says little the page does not already say.** "The node
  index $j$ is counted from the left endpoint" is the parameter's title word
  for word. The symmetry is `formula-symmetry`. And "a reader may hold either
  end of the rule" is a remark about the site, not the mathematics. After
  finding 2 the comment could go entirely.
- **`comment-neighbours` restates Similar tables.** It covers the same four
  tables with nearly the same relations. That is not against the
  once-per-section rule, but it adds nothing on this page.
- **`complete-note` does not say why the range stops at $n=12$.** The skill
  asks for that sentence. Only the builder knows the reason, so I am not
  proposing one.
- **The entry comments for $n\geq5$ begin "The closed Newton-Cotes rule with
  $n=5$:".** That repeats the $n$ the row already shows, and it uses a hyphen
  where the title has an en dash. The vector alone,
  "$\tfrac{5}{288}(19,75,50,50,75,19)$.", says the same.
- **`rigour details` says the generator "builds the Lagrange moment
  equations".** The moment equations are not Lagrange's. The Lagrange
  construction is the integral in `formula-lagrange`, which is a different
  method. "builds the moment equations
  $\sum_j w_{n,j}j^m=n^{m+1}/(m+1)$, $0\leq m\leq n$, over $\mathbb{Q}$" says
  what was done.
