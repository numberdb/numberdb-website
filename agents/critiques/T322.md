## Worth doing

### 1. "even lengths greater than $4$ remain open" is a stronger claim than the source makes, and it is false

Comment (5) reads:

> Barker sequences are known only for lengths $2,3,4,5,7,11,13$, and it is
> conjectured that no longer Barker sequence exists [2]. The nonexistence is
> proved for odd lengths greater than $13$, while even lengths greater than
> $4$ remain open.

A reader takes the last clause to mean that lengths 6, 8, 10 and 12 are open
questions. They are not. An exhaustive search over all normalized sign words
finds no Barker sequence at any even length from 6 to 20 -- one second in plain
Python -- and the literature has ruled out every even $n$ far beyond that. What
is open is the *existential*: whether there is any even length greater than 4.

The reference beside the sentence says it that way. Willms's abstract, which
`arxiv: 2104.00502` serves, reads "for even length it is still open, whether a
Barker sequence of even length greater 4 exists". Wikipedia's lead is the same
shape. Somewhere between the source and the comment an $\exists$ became a
$\forall$, and the compressed form is the one a reader gets.

**Smallest fix:** "while the existence of an even Barker sequence longer than
$4$ is open."

### 2. `rigour details` says the values are polynomials "over $\mathbb Z[x]$"

The first line of "How they were obtained", the one shown before the `more`
fold, reads:

> The values are exact polynomials over $\mathbb Z[x]$.

Polynomials over $\mathbb Z[x]$ have coefficients in $\mathbb Z[x]$, which is
not what this table holds. The attached generator says it correctly in its own
docstring ("exact polynomials over ZZ"); the table lost the ring on the way in.
It is small, it is wrong, and it is the sentence with the least around it to
correct it.

**Smallest fix:** "exact elements of $\mathbb Z[x]$", or "exact polynomials
over $\mathbb Z$".

### 3. Formula (2) ends with a sentence that is not a formula and that the Definition already made

> The aperiodic merit factor of the sequence is $F=n^2/(2\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}c_k^2)$.
> Barker sequences minimize each $|c_k|$ to at most $1$.

Three things about the second sentence. It is prose in a `Formulas` section,
which the skill's table of homes puts in `Comments`. "Minimize ... to at most"
is muddled: minimizing is not a bound, and a reader has to decide whether
"minimize" is a claim of extremality or a restatement. And whichever it is, the
Definition has already said $|c_k|\leq1$ in its first sentence, so the only
reading that adds anything is the extremal one, which the sentence does not
actually assert.

Meanwhile $F$ is introduced and then tied to nothing on the page: no entry
holds it, and the one place a reader could put it -- the
`Merit_factors_of_the_Legendre_sequences` link in `Similar tables` -- names a
different family. The formula earns its place if it is given a number.

**Smallest fix:** drop the second sentence, or replace it with the value, which
I checked: $F=169/12$ at $n=13$, the largest in this table.

### 5. Three places skirt the length-1 sequence and none of them names it

Comment (3) says "a *nontrivial* Barker sequence can be represented with
$a_0=a_1=1$". The parameter list says "length ($n\geq2$ is the length of a
normalized Barker sequence)". Comment (5) says Barker sequences are "known only
for lengths $2,3,4,5,7,11,13$". All three are circling the same fact -- the
sequence $(1)$ is vacuously Barker, there being no $k$ with $1\leq k<1$, and
the normalization $a_0=a_1=1$ cannot express it -- and none of them says it.
"Nontrivial" is a word the page uses and never defines, and $n\geq2$ is a bound
with no reason attached.

The table is not out of step with its own source here: MathWorld, which it
cites, also starts its list at 2. Wikipedia says "Apart from the trivial
sequence of length 1", which is the clause that is missing.

While that constraint is being edited: as rendered it reads "length ($n\geq2$
is the length of a normalized Barker sequence)", where the parenthesis repeats
the label the site has just printed.

**Smallest fix:** the constraint becomes "$n\geq2$: the normalization
$a_0=a_1=1$ needs two terms", and comment (5) opens "Apart from the trivial
sequence of length $1$, Barker sequences are known only for lengths ...".

### 6. "the eight sequences of lengths $2,3,4,5,7,11,13$"

In `complete-note`, and so on the page as

> Table is complete: no (it holds every normalized Barker sequence presently
> known, namely the eight sequences of lengths $2,3,4,5,7,11,13$ listed by [1])

Seven lengths, eight sequences. A reader counts, gets seven, and has to scroll
back to the rows to find that $n=4$ appears twice. The page never says so
anywhere else either.

**Smallest fix:** ", two of them of length $4$".

## Noting only

**7. Comment (4) is already on the page twice over.** "Coefficients run low
degree first: the sign in position $j$ of the sequence is the coefficient of
$x^j$" is the Definition's $B_a(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}a_jx^j$ restated in words,
and "With this indexing, $c_0=n$" is the closing clause of formula (1), which
the page draws immediately above it. The first clause is also answering a
question this table does not raise: "low degree first" is a convention about a
coefficient *list*, and every value here is displayed as an expression,
`x^12 - x^11 + ...`, where there is no order to choose. The comment could go
entirely without a reader losing anything. I am not urging it, because spelling
a formula out in words is a defensible kindness.

**8. The attached `generate.py` talks to this build machine.** `/files/T322/generate.py`
serves the file to anybody with the table, and its header says:

> On the NumberDB build machine, where arguments are not passed through
> agents/sage.sh, publish with:
>     $ cat "$NUMBERDB_KEY_FILE" | NUMBERDB_KEY_FROM_STDIN=1 NUMBERDB_PUBLISH=1 \
>           agents/sage.sh generate.py

That names a script in a repository the downloading reader does not have, and
line 1 cites `numberdb-data#160`, the "table wanted" issue -- the trace of the
request that the skill says a table does not carry. All four generators in this
batch share the paragraph, so it is a batch decision rather than this table's,
and the T321 critique has already written it down once.

**9. `Programs` restates the indexing convention instead of answering a
question.** The snippet turns a sign word into the polynomial printed beside it
on the same page; it hands a reader nothing they do not already have, because
this family is finite and all of it is here. The incantation that would earn
the slot is the search: enumerate the words beginning `++`, keep those with
$|c_k|\leq1$, and the table's own completeness claim falls out in a second. The
generator already contains it as `normalized_words_of_length`. A suggestion,
not a fault -- the snippet is correct and it runs.

**10. The Willms reference renders two links to one page.** It carries both
`arxiv: 2104.00502` and `doi: 10.48550/arXiv.2104.00502`, so the page reads
"(arXiv) (doi)"; the DOI 302s to `https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.00502`, which is
where the arXiv link goes. Harmless, and the Borwein-Mossinghoff entry beside
it is the good case, where `doi` and `mr` go to different places.

## What I checked hardest, and what came back right

* **The page renders.** Status 200, 31,578 bytes, all eight rows with their
  `$n$`, `$a$` and `$B_a(x)$` headers, the prose in the page's own section
  order (Formulas before Comments), the reference numbering, the tag strip
  matching `Tags`. The one bare `<` in the document -- `$1\leq k<n$`, in the
  Definition, the position that ate a section on another table this year --
  reaches the page as `&lt;` and eats nothing; the sentence and the two
  citations after it are all there. The `Programs` block keeps its
  indentation inside `<pre>`, so it survives a copy and paste.
* **The values are what the page says they are.** Each stored polynomial is
  exactly its sign word read low degree first; each satisfies $|c_k|\leq1$ for
  $1\leq k<n$; each has $c_0=n$; the autocorrelation identity of formula (1)
  holds for all eight. An exhaustive search over normalized words returns
  **exactly these eight** through length 20, seven lengths beyond the claim the
  table makes. The merit factors implied by formula (2) are $2$, $9/2$, $4$,
  $4$, $25/4$, $49/6$, $121/10$, $169/12$; the last two are the 12.1 and 14.08
  the literature quotes for lengths 11 and 13.
* **The sources say what the table says they say.** The Borwein-Mossinghoff DOI
  matches Crossref on title, both authors, 2008 and *Number Theory and
  Polynomials*; the arXiv DOI resolves. And the odd-length claim in comment (5),
  which I first took to be uncited, is stated in the abstract of the reference
  beside it -- the citation covers both sentences, which is why finding 1 is
  about the even half and not the odd.
* **The `Programs` block runs as stored**, under `sage -python` in the Sage
  container, and returns
  `x^12 - x^11 + x^10 - x^9 + x^8 + x^7 - x^6 - x^5 + x^4 + x^3 + x^2 + x + 1`
  in `Univariate Polynomial Ring in x over Integer Ring`: the $n=13$ entry,
  character for character.

Six findings, and the first three are each one sentence of editing. This is a
small, well-made table; what it needs is a proofread of its prose, not a
rebuild.
