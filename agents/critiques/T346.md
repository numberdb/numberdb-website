## What I checked hardest and found sound

* **The page renders, whole.** Nine sections in the page's own order
  (`Numbers`, `Definition`, `Parameters`, `Formulas`, `Comments`, `Programs`,
  `Links`, `Similar tables`, `Data properties`); 588 entry anchors, all
  present and all unique, `11,496,20008,2,1` through `17,33,-81,5,12`; no
  `Math input error`, no `argument ()`, no literal `<` in the metadata, no
  `--` anywhere in the prose, no damaged escape.

* **It is one table, and of the good kind.** $N$, $c_4$, $c_6$ are parts of
  one object — the skill names this exact case, "an elliptic curve is stored
  as $N$, $c_4$, $c_6$ because $c_4$ and $c_6$ identify the curve and $N$ says
  at a glance which curve it is" — $n$ is an argument, and $k$ is an index into
  one polynomial's roots. Nothing here is a second table wearing the title.

* **The definition's arithmetic reproduces the table.** Formula (3) claims
  $\deg F_{E,n}=(n^2-1)/2$ for odd $n$ and $n^2/2+1$ for even; the stored rows
  run to $k=3,4,9,12$ at $n=2,3,4,5$ for every one of the 21 curves, which is
  3+4+9+12 = 28 rows a curve and 588 in total. PARI agrees: I measured
  `elldivpol` at degrees 3, 4, 9, 12 on 11a1, so comment (7) is right that
  $F_{E,n}$ is PARI's polynomial, and formula (1) is right that for even $n$ it
  is $F_{E,2}\cdot\psi_n/\psi_2$ — since $\psi_2^2=F_{E,2}$ in the coordinate
  ring, the two say the same thing.

* **The ordering claim holds.** Comment (5) says roots are ordered by
  increasing real part and then increasing imaginary part; I sorted all 84
  (curve, $n$) groups by $k$ and found no violation of it anywhere.

* **A reader who arrives holding one of these numbers is served.** They land
  on a row, and the row says $N=11$, $(c_4,c_6)=(496,20008)$, $n=2$, $k=3$ and
  *Cremona label 11a1*. That is the answer to their question, on the screen
  they arrive at, without scrolling to the definition.

* **The repeated entry comment earns its place**, which is the opposite of
  what I expected to write. "Cremona label 11a1." is on all 28 rows of that
  curve, 588 times in all for 21 distinct labels, and the skill warns about
  exactly this shape — a label repeating what the block heading above it
  already says. Here it does not repeat the heading, it *translates* it: the
  heading says `496, 20008`, which names the curve to nobody, and the page
  puts 7,100 lines of numbers above the prose, so the entry comment is the
  only thing on a reader's screen that names the curve at all. Leave it.

* **The range is better chosen than its note admits** — see finding 6.

## Findings

### Worth doing

**1. The parameter $k$ is titled "root number", which in this subject is a
different invariant.** The page renders

>  $k$ —   root number ($1\leq k\leq \deg F_{E,n}$)

and this is a table of elliptic curves over $\mathbb{Q}$, where *root number*
is the sign $w(E)=\pm1$ in the functional equation of $L(E,s)$ — the quantity
in the parity conjecture, and a thing a reader of this page is very likely to
have met that week. A reader who knows the term reads the parameter as $\pm1$
and then sees it running to 12. A reader who does not know it learns a wrong
name for a real thing. What $k$ actually is is the index of a root in the
order comment (5) fixes. Smallest fix: title it `index of the root of
$F_{E,n}$`. Nothing else changes; the key `k` is an address and stays.

**2. 188 of the 588 values write an imaginary part that is exactly zero as an
interval.** They render as

>  4.346308158205394421969490790583425873686674714448356240649746034768642180978897371577968958294578772 + i * [0, 0]

which is 32% of the column, and the bracket notation appears nowhere in the
Definition, the Comments or the Formulas. It is not a typo and it is not
unsearchable — `utils/utils.py:_split_complex_terms` parses bracketed
components deliberately, and `numberdb_app/test_interval_spelling.py` tests
that spelling — but in this corpus `[0, 0]` and `0` say different things, and
that test file says which: "a zero-width interval is written as an interval …
so that the page says which of the two things it is: a value declared exact,
or interval arithmetic that landed on a point."

Here it is the first of those and the page writes the second. These roots are
the real roots of a rational polynomial, obtained in `QQbar`, where being real
is exact information the generator has and throws away by passing the root
through `ComplexIntervalField(350)` whole. The published complex table T317
writes the same situation the other way, `0 + i * 3.46410161513775…`, on the
values whose real part vanishes. And comment (6) on this very page promises
that "when the real and imaginary parts of a root are rational, those parts
are stored exactly" — zero is rational, and these 188 rows are the case the
sentence does not reach.

Smallest fix, in `complex_value` in the generator: when the root is real,
return `numberdb.ComplexInterval(RealInterval(lower, upper), Fraction(0))`
rather than the whole `CIF` element. The package writes each component by its
own rule, so the value becomes `4.34630… + i * 0` and comment (6) becomes true
of every row. (This is also the general lesson; it is in the proposals file.)

**3. The column is headed $x(P)$ and no $P$ is ever introduced.** `$x(P)$` is
the first thing a reader meets, at the top of the value column, and the word
"point" does not occur on the page. The Definition — which sits below all 588
rows — says instead "The entry is the $k$-th root of the $x$-coordinate
division polynomial $F_{E,n}(x)$", where "the $k$-th root of" also has to be
read twice, because that phrase means a radical everywhere else in
mathematics. One sentence fixes both:

> The entry is the $x$-coordinate $x(P)$ of a point $P\in E[n]\setminus\{O\}$:
> the root numbered $k$ of the division polynomial $F_{E,n}(x)$, whose roots
> are exactly those $x$-coordinates.

**4. `Requested in numberdb-data#10` is in `Links` and nothing cites it.** It
renders as a dangling `[3]` under `Links`, between the Sage and PARI manuals.
The skill is explicit in both directions: a "table wanted" issue is answered
in the issue, "the table carries no trace of the request: it is encyclopedic,
and who asked for it is not a fact about the mathematics"; and "a reference is
for citing, not for listing". Nothing on the page cites it — `CITE{RequestedIn10}`
occurs zero times. Delete the entry.

**5. Nothing says that every 2-torsion $x$-coordinate is in the table twice.**
$E[2]\subset E[4]$, so each of the three roots of $F_{E,2}$ is again a root of
$F_{E,4}$: 63 of the 588 rows are a second copy of another row, and I checked
that it is all of them, every curve, no exceptions. That is correct and it
follows from the definition, but a reader who lands on 14a1's $x=1$ and finds
it under both $n=2$ and $n=4$ has to work out for themselves whether one of
them is a mistake. It is a fact about the mathematics, so it belongs in
`Comments`:

> $E[m]\subseteq E[n]$ whenever $m$ divides $n$, so a root of $F_{E,m}$ is
> again a root of $F_{E,n}$: the three $x$-coordinates of the $2$-torsion
> appear under $n=2$ and again under $n=4$.

**6. The completeness note says what the range is and not why.** It renders as

> Table is complete: no (it holds every curve in Sage's mini Cremona database
> [1] with conductor $N\leq17$, and every $n$ with $2\leq n\leq5$)

The skill wants that sentence to carry the argument — "say which range you
chose and why in the completeness note … it is what a reader checking whether
their own number belongs here actually reads". A reader holding a value from
37a1 gets no idea whether 17 is a limit of the method, of the database, or of
somebody's afternoon. It is none of those: I counted the mini Cremona database
and $N\leq17$ is 21 curves and 588 rows, $N\leq20$ would be 28 curves and 784,
and $N\leq26$ is already 1,260 — past the recommended thousand. So 17 is the
largest round bound that fits, and the note should say so. Better still, say
what the range *covers*, which I also checked: those 21 curves realise
rational torsion of orders 1, 2, 4, 5, 6 and 8, so the small cases a reader is
most likely to arrive from are all present. Something like:

> it holds every curve in Sage's mini Cremona database CITE{SageCremona} with
> conductor $N\leq17$, at every $n$ with $2\leq n\leq5$: 21 curves, which is
> as far as this family goes inside a thousand entries, and enough to realise
> rational torsion of orders 1, 2, 4, 5, 6 and 8.

**7. The `Programs` snippet cannot give a reader the $k$ this table uses.** It
renders as eight lines ending `f.roots(QQbar, multiplicities=False)`, and two
things are missing from it. It never says which curve `pari([0, -1, 1, 0, 0])`
is — it is 11a1, the first row of the table, and a reader has no way to know
that — and it does not sort, so the roots come back in whatever order QQbar
hands them over, which is not the order comment (5) fixes. The skill asks
`Programs` for "the standard incantation for a reader who wants one more
value", and one more value here means one more $(N,c_4,c_6,n,k)$. Two added
lines make the snippet reproduce a row rather than a set:

    from sage.rings.complex_interval_field import ComplexIntervalField
    CIF = ComplexIntervalField(350)
    E = pari([0, -1, 1, 0, 0]).ellinit()        # Cremona 11a1
    ...
    roots = f.roots(QQbar, multiplicities=False)
    roots.sort(key=lambda z: (CIF(z).real().center(), CIF(z).imag().center()))

### Noting only

**8. The trailing clause in comment (5) cannot be attached to anything.** "For
fixed $(N,c_4,c_6,n)$, roots are ordered by increasing real part and then by
increasing imaginary part **after they are represented in $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$**."
$\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$ carries no order, so being represented in it is not
what makes "increasing real part" mean something; the clause is a note about
how the computation went, in a sentence that is otherwise a clean statement of
a convention. Dropping the last six words loses nothing. (The convention
itself is right: I checked it on all 84 groups.)

**9. "recovered from $(c_4,c_6)$" does not say by what rule.** The $x$
-coordinates depend on the model and not only on the isomorphism class, so
this is the one phrase in the definition that a second person would have to
guess at. It is not lost — comment (4) says the model "matches the curve
convention in [the division-polynomial table]", and that table spells the
reduction out in full, $b_2\in\{-4,-3,0,1,4,5\}$ and the rest. Its own
definition says "recovered from $(c_4,c_6)$ **by Formula (1)**"; this one
dropped the four words that point. Worth adding them back if anything else in
the definition is being edited, not worth an edit of its own.

**10. Not this table: the companion's comment about PARI's convention is
wrong, and this table's formula (1) sends readers there.** *Division
polynomials $\psi_n$* says in `comment-even-polynomials`: "For even $n$, the
$x$-only polynomial without the two-torsion factor is
$\psi_n/(2y+a_1x+a_3)$. PARI/GP's `elldivpol` … and Sage's default
`division_polynomial` … use the $x$-only convention". Measured on 11a1:
Sage's `division_polynomial(n, two_torsion_multiplicity=0)` gives degrees
0, 4, 6, 12 at $n=2,3,4,5$, which is that convention; PARI's `elldivpol`
gives 3, 4, 9, 12, which is not — it is $\psi_n\cdot(2y+a_1x+a_3)$, as that
table's own formula (5) correctly states two lines earlier. So the comment
contradicts the formula above it, and it is PARI that is misdescribed. T346 is
right and its nine rows at $n=4$ are the evidence; a reader whom T346's
formula (1) sends next door for "the notation" meets the contradiction.
The edit belongs to the other table.

**11. Not this table, and already known:
`https://numberdb.org/files/T346/generate.py` answers 200 to a caller with no
key, no cookie and no header** — the whole generator of a private draft —
while `/T346` correctly answers 404. This is the missing `_refuse_a_draft` in
`table_files` and `table_file` (`numberdb_app/views.py`), written up from the
T235, T324 and T332 runs and still open. I read the generator that way. It is
repeated here only so that whoever publishes this draft knows its source has
been public since the day it was attached.

## Summary

Seven things worth doing, of which 1 and 2 are the two I would not publish
without: a parameter titled with another invariant's name, and a third of the
value column spelt in the form the corpus reserves for something else. 3, 4,
5, 6 and 7 are each a sentence or two. The mathematics I could reach all
checks out — the degrees, the ordering, the curve count, the PARI convention —
and the table's shape, its links, its tags and its rendering are sound.
