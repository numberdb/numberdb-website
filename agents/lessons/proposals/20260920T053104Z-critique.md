## A printed table of decimals usually rounds; a truncation check passes on one source and fails on the next

What happened: T284 is built from Mossinghoff's small-Salem-number list, whose
30-place decimals are truncations, and its generator asserts exactly that --
`check_source_truncation` requires the recomputed interval to lie inside
`[printed, printed + 10^-30)`. It passes on all 47 rows. Asking what it would
take to extend the table past 1.3, I ran the same check against the 25 rows
Sac-Épée lists in $(1.3, 49/37)$ and it failed on the seventh: the Salem root
is $1.30647353753260\ldots$ and the paper prints `1.306473537533`. Ten of the
25 are rounded up, so the check rejects genuine values. Mossinghoff's own
fixed-degree lists round too, and say so in a separate format page: "Measure
... rounded (not truncated) at the last digit shown."

Neither convention is wrong, and neither is guessable from the digits: with
30 places printed, rounding and truncation differ on about half the rows, so
a check that passes on 47 rows really is evidence about *that* source and
none at all about the next one.

What the skill says now: "Check new values against something independent" and
"If your recomputation disagrees with a stored value, suspect your
recomputation first." Nothing about the printed digits of the source being an
interval of a particular shape.

What it should say: a source's printed decimals are an interval, and which
interval depends on a convention the source states elsewhere or not at all.
Truncation means $[p, p+u)$ and rounding means $[p-u/2, p+u/2)$, where $u$ is
the last place. Read the source's format note before writing the check; where
there is none, check against the rounded interval, which contains the
truncated one, and let the tighter claim be a thing you verify separately
rather than a thing you assume. A check that fails on a value you have
independently computed correctly is a bug in the check, and this is the
commonest form of it.

Evidence: `/tmp/salem_window.py` run through Sage, 2026-09-20. First run:
`AssertionError: printed decimal is not a truncation:
1.3064735375326053343134713464751215420813069220483292634099332975690540433558?
vs [1306473537533/1000000000000, 653236768767/500000000000)`. With the check
relaxed to a rounding, all 25 rows pass and ten print "rounded up, not
truncated". Sources: arXiv:2409.11159v3 §3.2 and
`http://www.cecm.sfu.ca/~mjm/Lehmer/misc/tableformat.html`.

## When a family accumulates, the accumulation point is the hard ceiling of any "less than B" table

What happened: T284 holds the 47 Salem numbers below 1.3 and the question was
how far it could go. The answer is not a size or a cost: the plastic constant
$\rho = 1.3247179\ldots$ is the smallest known limit point of the Salem
numbers, and Salem's construction puts infinitely many of them just below it.
So "Salem numbers less than $B$" is a finite family for every $B < \rho$ and
an infinite one for $B \geq \rho$, and the count diverges as $B \to \rho^-$.
I measured it on the one construction: for $n \leq 89$, Salem's construction
on $\rho$ alone gives 14 Salem numbers in $(1.3, 49/37]$ and 63 in
$(49/37, \rho)$, with no bound on the second as $n$ grows. The entire
headroom of a table of that shape is an interval of width 0.0247, and the
useful bounds inside it are named constants rather than round numbers: 1.3 is
the literature's, and $49/37$ is one paper's, chosen to sit $4\cdot10^{-4}$
below $\rho$.

The sibling table shows the other case. T286 holds 79 Pisot numbers below the
golden ratio, an infinite family accumulating at $\varphi$, and it can,
because Dufresnoy and Pisot classified that family: there is a formula, an
increasing order, and therefore a place to stop with a reason, which its
`complete-note` gives. Nothing of that kind exists below $\rho$ for the Salem
numbers, so the same shape of bound produces a table in one case and not in
the other.

What the skill says now: §3 says to include what is common and stop, and that
"which parameters are the interesting ones is a fact about the family". It
does not say that a family may have a boundary that is a fact rather than a
choice.

What it should say: before choosing a bound, ask whether the family has limit
points. If it does, the smallest one is the ceiling, and no bound at or above
it gives a table however generous the limits are. Below it, say in
`complete-note` which named constant the bound is and what is on the far side
of it, because that sentence is what tells a reader holding a number just
above the bound whether their number is missing or out of scope. And where
the family accumulates but is *classified*, as the Pisot numbers below
$\varphi$ are, the bound can approach the limit point and the reason to stop
becomes an ordinary one: the entries stop being distinguishable, or stop
being things anybody meets.

Evidence: `/tmp/salem_ceiling.py` through `agents/sage.sh`, 2026-09-20 --
"14 Salem roots in (1.3, 49/37]", "63 Salem roots in (49/37, rho)", all 14
of the first group present in Sac-Épée's published table. T284's own
`comment-bound`, and T286's `complete-note`. Sac-Épée, arXiv:2409.11159v3,
§1.3.

## Search the corpus by the number as well as by the name: the table you need may hold it as a row

What happened: T284 names the plastic constant in a comment, as the limit
point that caps its range. Looking for a table to link it to, I searched
titles and tags for "plastic", "Pisot" and "Salem" and found nothing, and
nearly wrote that the corpus does not hold it. It does. T286 is "Pisot
numbers less than the golden ratio", and the plastic constant is its entry
`1`, named in that row's comment as the smallest Pisot number. No word in
its title, tags or definition is a word I would have searched for. A lookup
of `1.3247179572447` answered with it in one call, and that one call was the
most useful thing in the whole reading: it is the table the critique's main
finding is about.

The skill already gives the reason: an entry's own comment is in no index, so
a constant that appears only as a row cannot be found by its name at all.
That sentence is written as a rule about *making* a title. It is equally a
rule about *reading* the corpus, and the second use is the one that was
missed.

What the skill says now: §1 says to search the database before writing
anything, and gives `search_text` for titles, tags, definitions and comments.
Nothing tells you to search by the number, and §1's own warning about entry
comments appears far away in §2, where it is about titles.

What it should say: when a table is going to name a constant, look it up by
its digits as well as by its name. The text index does not reach an entry
comment, so the name of a number that lives in somebody else's table is
invisible to a text search, and the constant you are about to link to
Wikipedia may be a row here. Two calls, not one: search the name for the
family, look up the number for the row. A hit gives you the slug and the
entry key together, which is what `HREF{slug#key}` needs.

Evidence: `GET /api/lookup?text=1.3247179572447` answered `T286 |
Pisot_numbers_less_than_the_golden_ratio | index 1` and
`T222 | Growth_rates_of_hyperbolic_Coxeter_triangle_groups | index
[inf,3]`, 2026-09-20; text searches for "plastic" and "Pisot" over the
public table listing matched nothing.
