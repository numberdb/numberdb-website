# Proposed from the T361 critique, 2026-09-20

## Anchor a convention at the index where the conventions disagree, not at the first row

What happened: T361 holds the real regular solid harmonics as polynomials over
$\mathbb{Q}$, and settles which of the several normalisations it means with

> This table uses the unnormalised real convention with $R_{1,1}=x$,
> $R_{1,-1}=y$ and $R_{1,0}=z$.

Three worked rows, offered as the thing that pins the convention down. They
pin nothing. The competing convention -- Racah's, which is what the
Wikipedia article the Definition cites actually tabulates -- is
$C^m_\ell=[(2-\delta_{m0})(\ell-m)!/(\ell+m)!]^{1/2}\,\Pi^m_\ell(z)A_m(x,y)$,
and its normalising factor at $\ell=1$ is exactly $1$. Both conventions give
$x$, $y$ and $z$. They first differ at $\ell=2$, where the table has $3xz$ and
the cited source has $\sqrt3\,xz$, and by $\ell=5,m=3$ they differ by a factor
of about a hundred. A reader who follows the link to check the convention
checks it against the one degree where there is nothing to check.

The general shape: an author picks the smallest rows to quote because they are
the shortest to write, and the smallest rows are usually where every
normalisation, phase and indexing convention agrees -- $n=0$ and $n=1$ are
where the factorials, the $(-1)^m$ and the $\sqrt{}$ all collapse to 1. The
worked example that would settle it is the first one that is not degenerate.

The same test applies to a phase: a Condon-Shortley $(-1)^m$ is invisible at
every even $m$, so a comment anchoring on $m=0$ or $m=2$ says nothing about
it.

What the skill says now: section 2 says "Which normalisation, branch, and
indexing" must be written down first, and gives `elliptic_k` taking $m$ rather
than $k$ as the example -- "the two differ from the second entry onwards". The
rule is there in that half-sentence and only for that one table; nothing tells
an author to *choose the row they quote* by where the conventions part.

What it should say: when a comment fixes a convention by quoting entries,
quote the first entry at which the competing convention gives a different
number, and say which convention that is. "The unnormalised convention, with
$R_{1,1}=x$" is not a statement a reader can falsify; "with $R_{2,2}=3x^2-3y^2$,
where the semi-normalised $C^2_2$ of CITE{Wiki} is $\tfrac12\sqrt3(x^2-y^2)$"
is. The test an author can run on their own machine: compute the same row in
both conventions and check the two strings differ.

Evidence: T361, 2026-09-20. `/tmp/check361.py` reproduces all 324 stored
entries character for character from $\Pi^m_\ell(z)A_m(x,y)$ and prints the
Racah factor per row: `l=1 m=1 Wikipedia C = 1.000000 * stored`, `l=2 m=1
... 0.577350 * stored`, `l=5 m=3 ... 0.009960 * stored`.

## A `Programs` snippet should end on a row of the table it is attached to

What happened: T361's `Programs` block is nine lines of Sage that build the
Legendre polynomials by Bonnet's recurrence and end on `P[17]`, commented "the
last Legendre polynomial used here". It is correct code and it is an
intermediate: no line of it produces any entry of the table, and a reader who
wanted one more value has been handed the input to the step where all the
choices this table had to make -- the homogenisation, the $(-1)^m$, the
$\operatorname{Re}(x+iy)^m$ -- actually happen. The attached `generate.py`
does the rest correctly, so the material was there; the snippet simply stopped
early.

This is easy to do whenever the table's values are built in two stages from a
family the corpus already holds, which is most polynomial tables: the first
stage is the interesting recurrence and the second is bookkeeping, so the
first is what gets pasted in.

What the skill says now: "`Programs` is the standard incantation in Sage, PARI
or mpmath for a reader who wants one more value", and contrasts it with
`generate.py`. True, and it does not say how to check that a snippet is one.

What it should say: the last expression in a `Programs` snippet should be a
value of *this* table, and preferably one just past its range, so the reader
who wanted one more gets one more. If the snippet's last line evaluates to
something that is not an entry, it belongs in the middle of a longer snippet
rather than at its end. The check costs nothing: run the snippet and compare
its output to a stored row.

Evidence: T361 rendered through `/preview`, 2026-09-20; the `Programs` section
prints `P[17] # the last Legendre polynomial used here`, a univariate
polynomial in `u`, on a table whose every entry is a polynomial in `x, y, z`.
