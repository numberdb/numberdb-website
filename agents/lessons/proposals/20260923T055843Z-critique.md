## A source table's attribution column is data, and transcribing without it loses the one thing the reader cannot recover

What happened: T440 is transcribed from Wikipedia's "Known van der Waerden
numbers" table, which has three columns -- the number, the value, and who
computed it. The draft transcribed the first two. Its `References` keeps four
of the twelve works the source names, and no entry cites any of them. Counting
the source's attributions over its 124 rows, the four kept references cover 76
rows and 48 come from work the table does not list -- including $w(2;6,6)=1132$
(Kouril and Paul), the largest and most-cited value in the table, and
$w(2;3,3)=9$ (Chvátal). A reader who lands on 1132 and wants the paper is
handed four papers, none of which is it.

The value is recomputable and the attribution is not. Anybody can check 1132
against the source; nobody can reconstruct from a bare table of integers which
of twelve groups over fifty-five years produced which row, and for a table of
research computations that is most of what a reader came to find out. The
corpus already does this the other way round: T6 gives every non-trivial row a
comment naming its source ("the upper endpoint is the Angeltveit-McKay
computation CITE{AnM3}"), and T6 is the table T440 declares as similar.

This is not specific to Wikipedia. OEIS b-files carry an author and a date,
LMFDB rows carry the computation that produced them, and a published table of
records carries a citation per record. The transcribing step is where those
get dropped, because the value is the thing being copied.

What the skill says now: "A reference is for citing, not for listing.
`CITE{Koshy}` in a comment earns its place; a bibliography nobody points at is
furniture." And, for entry comments, that they are "worth having when they say
something the row cannot". Both are true here and neither is phrased as
something to check while transcribing -- the author who drops a column is not
thinking about the bibliography at all, and the resulting table passes the
audit and `verify` alike.

What it should say: when a table's values are transcribed rather than computed,
copy the source's attribution with them. If the source names who produced each
value, that belongs in the entry comment, and every work it names belongs in
`References`. A `References` list that covers some of the rows is worse than
none, because it reads as the provenance of all of them. The check is
countable: for each reference, which rows does it account for, and which rows
account to nothing?

Evidence: 2026-09-23, T440 critique. `GET /api/table?id=T440` (164 entries,
`rigour details`: "transcribed from the source table in Wikipedia's Van der
Waerden number article"); the article's wikitext parsed to 124 rows with
attributions Ahmed 63, Kouril 17, Landman-Robertson-Culver 12, Brown 11,
Chvátal 8, Beeler and O'Neil 6, Ahmed-Kullmann-Snevily 2, and one each to
Beeler, Stevens and Shantaram, Kouril and Paul, Schweitzer, Karki.
`References` holds Landman-Robertson-Culver, Ahmed 2009, Ahmed 2013, Karki.

## A closed form that is already in `Formulas` should not also be a block of rows

What happened: T440 holds 164 entries, of which 40 are the two identities the
table states in `Formulas`: `$w(1;k)=k$` for $k\leq20$, and
`$w(r;2,\dots,2)=r+1$` for $r\leq21$. Their values are exactly the integers
1 to 22, and fifteen of those twenty-two occur in the table only in a trivial
row. The values box, which is the first thing on a table page, opens on twenty
consecutive rows reading $n = n$. Searching numberdb for 9, for 17 or for 22
already returns the 100-result cap; these rows add forty more hits to queries
that answer nothing today.

The generator's docstring calls them "the finite trivial rows that cover the
same displayed range", so the author knew what they were. They were added
*alongside* a well-chosen family, not instead of one -- which is why the
existing rule did not catch them.

What the skill says now: "values that are a handful of small integers -- class
numbers are mostly 1, 2, 3 -- match everything and tell nobody anything, and
putting them in a table makes search by number worse for everyone. Those belong
in the comment on the entry they explain." That paragraph sits under "Is this
table already here, under another name?" and reads as a rule about *choosing a
family*: do not propose a table whose values are all small integers. An author
whose family is right does not read it as being about them, and this is the
second draft in a week to fail it the same way (the T439 critique found twelve
rows holding the integers 2 to 13).

What it should say: the same rule applies to rows added to a good table.
Before storing a row, ask whether a formula in the document already gives it
for every parameter rather than for the range you enumerated -- if it does, the
formula is the better answer and the row is only search noise. Anchor rows have
a place and it is a small one: T6 keeps two trivial rows out of ten, not forty
out of a hundred and sixty-four.

Evidence: 2026-09-23, T440 critique. 40 of 164 rows generated from
`MAX_ONE_COLOR_LENGTH = 20` and `MAX_ALL_TWO_COLORS = 21` in
`/files/T440/generate.py`, against `formula-one-color` and
`formula-length-two` in the same document; distinct trivial values
`[1..22]`, of which `[1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8,10,11,12,13,15,16,19]` appear nowhere
else in the table. `GET /api/lookup?text=17` returns the 100-result cap.
