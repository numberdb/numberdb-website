# Lessons from the ideas run of 2026-09-22T22:49Z (the Markov and Lagrange spectra)

Three things this run met that the skill does not cover. All three are true
for a contributor with a laptop; nothing here is about this deployment.

## Check a family's *index* against OEIS, not only its values

What happened: the batch proposes several families of real numbers indexed by
the Markov numbers. The values are quadratic irrationals and no OEIS entry
holds them, so there was nothing to check against there -- but the *index* is
A002559, and comparing the tree enumeration against it caught what a check is
for: whether the family's members are the ones everybody else means. The
enumeration matched all 42 terms OEIS lists. Separately, the one lone constant
of the batch had its digits in A118472, and 40 of them matched the closed form.

So a family of reals gets two free checks from a database of integers: the
sequence its parameter runs over, and any member famous enough to have its
decimal expansion stored.

What the skill says now: OEIS appears as a link target, as one of the four
kinds of check ("a specialisation to a sequence with an OEIS number"), and --
since a lesson earlier this month -- as the place where a polynomial family is
kept as a coefficient triangle. Nothing says to look up the *index*, and a
builder whose values are transcendental or algebraic naturally concludes OEIS
has nothing to offer.

What it should say: when the values are not integers, search OEIS for what the
table is indexed *by*. A family indexed by a named integer sequence has an
independent copy of its own enumeration there, which is the half of a table
most likely to be quietly wrong -- the values are computed from a formula and
checked against it, while the list of parameters is whatever the generator's
loop produced. And search it for the individual constants too: `fmt=json`
returns a record whose `data` field is the digit string, which compares
directly against a computed value.

Evidence: `https://oeis.org/search?q=id:A002559&fmt=json` against a Markov
tree built from the Vieta involution: 42 terms, exact match, and it would have
caught a tree that dropped the triples with a repeated member.
`https://oeis.org/search?q=id:A118472&fmt=json` against
$(2221564096+283748\sqrt{462})/491993569$: 40 digits, exact match.

## An encyclopedia's normalisation is stated for the generic member, and the first entry is usually not generic

What happened: Wikipedia states Markov's theorem with the side condition
$0<k<p/2$ on the auxiliary integer that defines the Markov form. That
condition has no solution for the first two members of the family: $p=1$ needs
$k=0$ and $p=2$ needs $k=1=p/2$. The correct range is the closed one,
$0\le k\le p/2$, under which $k$ is unique -- checked against an exhaustive
search on all 40 triples in range. A builder who trusted the strict inequality
would have had a generator that raised on its first two entries, which is the
good case; the bad case is the second half of the same story. The same family
defines its real numbers as roots of that form, and for every member but the
first the root wanted is the positive one, while for the first member *both*
roots are negative. "The positive root" is a rule that is right from the second
entry onward, produces no error, and quietly stores the wrong number once.

What the skill says now: "Which normalisation, branch, indexing" must be
written down before computing, with `elliptic_k`'s parameter and "the $n$th
zero" as the examples. Both examples are about a convention that is wrong
*everywhere* if it is wrong at all.

What it should say: compute the first three entries by hand, against the
values the source quotes, before freezing a convention -- the degenerate cases
of a family sit at the front of the table, where every reader starts, and a
rule that is right generically can fail exactly there. A source's side
conditions are written for the generic member; the first two entries are the
ones to test them on.

Evidence: Wikipedia, *Markov number*, "Markov's theorem": $0<a<p/2$, against
the triples $(1,1,1)$ and $(1,1,2)$, whose forms are $x^2+3xy+y^2$ and
$2x^2+4xy-2y^2$ with $k=0$ and $k=1$. And the roots of the first of those,
$-0.3819660\ldots$ and $-2.618\ldots$, against $\sqrt2-1$ for the second.

## A parameter defined by a congruence is computed with a modular inverse, not by searching for it

What happened: the auxiliary integer above is defined by
$k\,m_2\equiv\pm m_1\pmod m$. The obvious generator loops $k$ from $0$ and
tests the congruence, which is fine while you are working on the first ten
entries -- $m\le233$ there -- and is $O(m)$. The table wants $m$ up to
$10^{12}$; the loop had not finished one entry after a minute. Rewritten as
`pow(m2, -1, m)` and two representatives, all 152 forms came out in about a
millisecond, and agreed with the search on every entry where the search
finishes.

What the skill says now: it is careful about exactness in a generator (float
division, `QQ(a)/QQ(b)`) and about measuring the largest entry before choosing
a range. It says nothing about the cost of `enumerate` or of the parameter
arithmetic, and the failure does not look like a bug: the generator works, on
the entries you tested it on.

What it should say: if a parameter is defined by a congruence, a divisor
condition or an inverse, compute it -- `pow(a, -1, n)`, `xgcd`, a factorisation
-- rather than searching for it. A linear search over a residue is invisible in
testing and decides the table's range later, silently, by not finishing. More
generally: before choosing the range, time one entry at the *largest* index
intended, not at a convenient one. The skill already says to measure the
largest entry's *size* for the same reason; its cost deserves the same
sentence.

Evidence: 152 Markov forms below $m=10^{12}$, 0.001 s by modular inverse;
the equivalent search, at the single largest triple
$(34, 9629807441, 982145940029)$, still running when it was killed at 60 s.
Agreement of the two routes on all 40 triples below $10^6$.
