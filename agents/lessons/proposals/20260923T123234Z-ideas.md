# Lessons from the ideation run of 2026-09-23T12:32Z (approximation-theory batch)

## `'%.24f' % x` on a high-precision real prints a double, so a check "disagrees with OEIS" at the 19th digit

What happened: the batch's checks compared three families against OEIS and
two of them appeared to diverge past digit 18. The Lebesgue constant
$L_1=\frac13+\frac{2\sqrt3}{\pi}$ printed as
`1.435991124176917432819778` against A226654's
`1.435991124176917432355986`, and the Halphen constant came out
`9.28902549192081927742` against A073007's `9.28902549192081891875`. Both
were printed with `'%.24f' % x` and `'%.40f' % x` on `RealField(120)` and
`RealField(200)` elements. `%`-formatting calls `__float__`, so everything
past the 17th digit is what the double happened to round to -- invented, and
invented *differently* for two independently computed values, which is
exactly what a real disagreement looks like. Reprinted with
`.str(digits=40)`, the same computations agree with A226654, A226655,
A226656 to all 40 digits and with A073007 to its last published digit.

What the skill says now: two lessons in `PROPOSALS.md` about
`RealNumber.str(20)` printing in base 20, and the rule to compare balls
rather than printed digits. Nothing about `%`-formatting.

What it should say: never print a value being checked with `%`, `f'{x:.40f}'`
or `float(x)` -- all three go through the double. Use `.str(digits=n)` for a
Sage real, `mid().str(digits=n)` for a ball, `mpmath.nstr(x, n)` for mpmath.
And the diagnostic is worth stating, because it is the cheap way to recognise
this: a disagreement that begins at the 17th or 18th significant digit,
whatever the working precision, is a printing artefact until proved
otherwise. A genuine disagreement usually starts where the mathematics is
wrong, not where `binary64` runs out.

Evidence: `/tmp/checks.py` and `/tmp/checks3.py` under `agents/sage.sh`,
2026-09-23; the same script prints `"%.24f" : 1.435991124176917432819778`
and `.str(digits=30): 1.43599112417691743235598632996` for one value on two
adjacent lines.

## A MathWorld citation to OEIS can name the wrong sequence, so read the sequence before you trust the check

What happened: MathWorld's *Lebesgue Constants* page gives the asymptotic
$L_n \approx \frac{4}{\pi^2}\log n + c$ and cites **A086052** for $c$.
A086052 is "Sum of first n 6-almost primes" -- `64, 160, 304, 464, ...`. The
constant $0.98943127383114695174\ldots$ is **A243277**, found by searching
OEIS for its digits. Taking the cited A-number as the independent check would
have produced a table entry checked against nothing at all, and a `CITE` a
reader would follow to a page about almost primes.

What the skill says now: "Check new values against something independent",
and OEIS is named as the cheapest such source.

What it should say: an OEIS A-number quoted by a secondary source is part of
the secondary source, not part of OEIS. Fetch the sequence and read its
`%N` line before using it as a check or writing it into a `CITE`; the
one-line form `https://oeis.org/search?q=id:A243277&fmt=text` is enough to
see the name, the formula and whether a b-file exists.

Evidence: `https://mathworld.wolfram.com/LebesgueConstants.html` (retrieved
2026-09-23) against `https://oeis.org/search?q=id:A086052&fmt=text` and
`https://oeis.org/search?q=0.9894312738&fmt=text`, which returns A243277.

## `RealBall` has no `elliptic_k`, so an elliptic constant can be computed to 45 digits and still not be certified in Sage

What happened: the one-ninth constant was computed from its elliptic
characterisation -- find $k$ with $K(k^2)=2E(k^2)$, then
$1/\Lambda=\exp(\pi K(1-k^2)/K(k^2))$ -- with `elliptic_kc` and `elliptic_ec`
on `RealField(400)`. Those are precision-honest: the root matched A086199 to
all 45 digits printed and $1/\Lambda$ matched A073007 to its last digit.
The attempt to redo it in balls failed at once:

    RBF(1/2).elliptic_k()
    AttributeError: 'sage.rings.real_arb.RealBall' object has no attribute 'elliptic_k'

`RealBall` exposes no complete elliptic integral at all -- `hasattr(RBF(0.5),
'elliptic_k')` is `False`. `ComplexBallField` has the whole set
(`elliptic_k`, `elliptic_e`, `elliptic_f`, `elliptic_pi`, the Carlson
`elliptic_r*` forms, `modular_*`), and `(CBF(1)/2).elliptic_k()` returns
`[1.85407467730137 +/- 3.40e-15]` -- the same parameter convention $m$ as
`elliptic_kc`, not the modulus. So the certified route exists; it goes
through `CBF` and back, and a generator planned around `RBF` discovers that
only when it runs.

What the skill says now: a lesson that the two ball classes expose different
subsets of arb, with `legendre_Q` and `psi` as the examples.

What it should say: the same rule with the elliptic integrals named, since
they are what a singular-value, lattice-sum or rational-approximation table
reaches for: the complete integrals are on `CBF` only, and
`elliptic_kc`/`elliptic_ec` on `RealField(n)` do honour $n$ (45 correct
digits at 400 bits, checked against two OEIS b-files) if the plain
high-precision route is the one wanted -- with the rigour then recorded as
"high precision, checked", not "certified".

Evidence: `/tmp/checks2.py`, `/tmp/checks3.py` and `/tmp/cbf.py` under
`agents/sage.sh`, 2026-09-23; A086199 and A073007.

## `source_names_it` 404 on a hyphenated name is usually the URL, not the family

What happened: screening the one-ninth constant,
`mathworld.wolfram.com/OneNinthConstant.html` and
`en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-ninth_constant` both answered 404. Read as the
screen intends, that says the family is not called this by anybody -- and it
is a constant with two OEIS entries, a 10000-digit b-file and a chapter in
Finch. MathWorld keeps the hyphen: the page is `One-NinthConstant.html`, a
form no other MathWorld URL in this batch uses (`FavardConstants.html`,
`BernsteinsConstant.html`, `Landau-KolmogorovConstants.html` -- note that one
keeps its hyphen too). Wikipedia simply has no article.

What the skill says now: cite a source and run `source_names_it` on it.

What it should say: a 404 is a fact about the URL. Before concluding a family
is unnamed, try the hyphen in and out of the MathWorld path, try the
possessive with and without the `'s`, and try OEIS -- which answers
`?fmt=text` and whose `%N` line names the family as well as any encyclopedia.
Only a 200 whose text lacks the distinguishing words is evidence about the
name.

Evidence: three 404s and one 200 on the same constant, 2026-09-23, recorded
in the batch's screening table.
