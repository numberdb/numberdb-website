# Lessons from the ideas run of 2026-09-23T03:37Z (Ramsey theory)

Three things this run met that the skill does not cover. All three are true
for a contributor with a laptop; the deployment notes from the same run are in
`docs/agent-environment.md`.

## A combinatorial threshold comes in two conventions that differ by one, and no run of digits can tell them apart

What happened: the batch is four tables of "the least $N$ at which something
is forced". Every one of those quantities is also written in the literature as
"the largest $N$ at which it can still be avoided", and the two differ by
exactly one. This is not a rare pitfall: it is the *normal* state of the
subject.

Checked by brute force rather than taken from a page. The largest $N$ whose
$\{1,\dots,N\}$ splits into three sum-free sets is $13$, and $14$ does not
split. So the third Schur number is $13$ in one convention and $14$ in the
other, and both are in print -- Wikipedia's *Schur's theorem* gives
$S(1..5) = 2, 5, 14, 45, 161$ while much of the literature gives
$1, 4, 13, 44, 160$ for the same five facts. The same run verified that
Ramsey and van der Waerden numbers use the forcing convention
($R(3,3) = 6$ because $K_5$ can be coloured and $K_6$ cannot; $W(2,3) = 9$
because $\{1..8\}$ can be and $\{1..9\}$ cannot), so a family can be internally
consistent with its neighbours and still be off by one against half its own
literature.

Why the existing guidance does not catch it. The skill's convention warnings
are about quantities whose two readings differ by a *factor* or a
*parameterisation*: `elliptic_k` taking $m$ rather than $k$, the two AGM square
roots, `mathieu_cem` in degrees. Those are caught by a control value, because
the two readings disagree visibly. An off-by-one in a *threshold* is not: both
conventions produce a plausible small integer, the published tables agree with
each other, and there is no degenerate parameter at which the two coincide.
For an exact-integer table there is not even a digit to be suspicious of.

What the skill says now: "Which normalisation, branch, indexing" -- with
examples that are all analytic, and "'The nth zero' needs a statement of where
counting starts", which is the closest thing and is about indexing rather than
about the value.

What it should say: when the quantity is a *threshold* -- the least size at
which something is forced, the largest at which it can be avoided -- state
which of the two in the definition, in the form "the least $N$ such that every
... contains ...", and name the other convention and the offset in `Comments`.
A reader holding $44$ and a reader holding $45$ are both holding a Schur
number, and only the comment tells the second one so. Settle which convention
the sources use by computing the smallest case *both ways* and seeing which
answer the published number is: that is one brute-force search, it is the only
check that distinguishes them, and agreement with a published table does not
distinguish them at all.

Evidence: 2026-09-23 ideas run, plain Python 3, no Sage.
`[1..13]` splits into three sum-free sets and `[1..14]` does not (backtracking
with colours introduced in order, under a second); a good $2$-colouring of
$K_5$ exists and none of $K_6$ over all $2^{15}$ colourings; a $3$-AP-free
$2$-colouring of `[1..8]` exists and none of `[1..9]`;
$z(4,4;2,2) = 9$ over all $2^{16}$ zero-one matrices; $R(P_3,K_3) = 5$,
matching Chvátal's $(m-1)(n-1)+1$.

## A word-presence check on a source is vacuous for a one-word name, and blind to a PDF

What happened: the screen checks that the cited page contains the
*distinguishing* words of the name, which for "Folkman numbers" is the single
word "folkman". Wikipedia's *Folkman's theorem* contains it, so the check
passes -- and that article is about a theorem on sums, not about Folkman
numbers, which are a different object. The same thing happened the other way
round on "Graph Ramsey numbers $R(G,H)$" against MathWorld's *Ramsey Number*
page: the check passed on "ramsey" and "graph", while the page's "generalized
Ramsey number" is the *hypergraph* one and general graphs appear in a single
sentence about somebody else's 1983 tabulation. Both proposals would have read
perfectly well with a citation that does not support them, which is the exact
failure `source_names_it` exists to prevent.

What the skill says now: nothing -- `source_names_it` is described in the
ideation prompt, not the skill, but the underlying advice ("cite a source, and
check the source names it") is general and is what a contributor does by hand.

A third case, sharper than either, and it fails the other way. The strongest
source for the batch was Radziszowski's *Small Ramsey Numbers*, and checking a
family against it returns "the source does not mention ramsey, complete,
graphs" -- because the check strips HTML tags from what are PDF bytes and
finds nothing at all. **A cited paper cannot be checked this way**, so a
proposal whose only good source is a PDF has to say that it was read by a
person instead, and say what was read: which table, on which page, of which
revision.

The same run met the reason a PDF source is easy to get wrong. The journal's
article URL, `.../eljc/article/view/DS1`, is an HTML viewer shell containing
the word "Download" and nothing of the mathematics; the document itself is at
`.../eljc/article/download/DS1/pdf/`, which redirects and answers
`application/pdf`. Checking a family against the viewer URL passes or fails on
the abstract, not the contents, and the abstract of a survey lists what it
*excludes* as readily as what it holds. This one does: it names bipartite
Ramsey numbers as not covered, which killed a proposal.

What it should say: a word-presence check is only as strong as the name is
specific and the fetch is readable. When the distinguishing part of a family's
name is one proper noun, or the page uses that noun for a different object of
the same person's, the check proves nothing and you must read the page. When
the source is a PDF, no automatic check applies; read it, and cite the table
and page you read. The tell for the first case is the count: one
distinguishing word, or a name whose words are all common in the subject,
means read it; two or more unusual words together is a real check.

Evidence: 2026-09-23 ideas run.
`source_names_it("Folkman numbers", "https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folkman%27s_theorem")`
returns `None` (passes); the MathWorld page it was also run against,
`https://mathworld.wolfram.com/FolkmanNumber.html`, returns
`source answered 404`, so the family has no MathWorld article at all.
`source_names_it("Graph Ramsey numbers $R(G,H)$",
"https://mathworld.wolfram.com/RamseyNumber.html")` returns `None` while the
page defines $R(m_1,\dots,m_k;n)$ over $n$-element subsets.
`source_names_it("Bipartite Ramsey numbers $b(s,t)$",
"https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramsey%27s_theorem")` returns `None`, and the
only occurrence of "bipartite" on that page is inside the title of a cited
paper.
`source_names_it(..., "https://www.combinatorics.org/ojs/index.php/eljc/article/download/DS1/pdf/")`
-> `the source does not mention ramsey, complete, graphs`, against a 149-page
document whose title is *Small Ramsey Numbers*.

## The whole corpus can be listed, without a key, from `/tables?page=N`

What happened: the run wanted to know which subjects the corpus does not
cover, which needs the list of every table. The skill says there is no such
call and that you walk the T-numbers; `/api/tables` exists but is rate limited
to 60 requests an hour without a key and answered `429` on the first try. The
ordinary HTML listing at `https://numberdb.org/tables?page=N` is paginated at
50 titles a page, is not rate limited in the same way, and nine requests
returned all 408 titles with their T-numbers, parsed out of
`href="/T123">title</a>` in six lines. That is what made "one table out of 408
in this subject" a statement rather than a guess.

Note the count: the skill's own census says 107 tables and the ideation prompt
says 126. Both are stale by a factor of three, and a proposal that trusts
either will mis-state how well covered a subject is.

What the skill says now: "There is **no call that lists the corpus**; to see
everything you walk the T-numbers." And, in the census, "107 tables -- 65
real, 16 integer, ...".

What it should say: the corpus listing is at `/tables`, paginated fifty to a
page, and reading it is the first thing to do before claiming a subject is
uncovered -- `search_text` answers a name, and cannot answer "what is not
here". And the census should be a pointer to that page rather than a number in
the text, because a number in the text goes stale silently and reads as
current.

Evidence: 2026-09-23 ideas run.
`curl -s "https://numberdb.org/api/tables?per_page=300"` ->
`{"error": "Rate limit exceeded (60 requests per 60 minutes) ...",
"retry_after": 1201}`;
`for p in 1..9; do curl -s "https://numberdb.org/tables?page=$p"; done` ->
408 distinct `T<number> <title>` pairs, T0 to T411, in nine requests.
