## A normalisation identity that holds by integration by parts is not a check

What happened: a proposal for the bulk level-spacing laws of random matrix
theory named $\int_0^\infty E(0;s)\,ds = 1$ as its check, on the grounds that
the process is normalised to mean spacing $1$. It is not an identity. The
integral is the expected distance from a fixed location to the next point of
the process, which is $1$ for a Poisson process and smaller for a rigid one;
the sine-kernel determinant gave $0.58999693884592\ldots$, and the check
"failed" while the values were right.

Looking for the identity that was meant found two, and only one of them is a
check. $\int_0^\infty p(s)\,ds = 1$ is the statement $E'(0) = -1$ and does test
the values. $\int_0^\infty s\,p(s)\,ds = 1$ is $\int_0^\infty s\,E''(s)\,ds$,
which integrates by parts to $E(0) - E(\infty) = 1 - 0$ for *any* monotone $E$
with those endpoints: it cannot fail, so it confirms nothing, and reporting it
as agreement would have been a false confirmation of the kind the skill already
warns about for enclosures that contain everything.

What the skill says now: "'There is a known closed form' is not a check; it is
a hope", and the list of what a real check looks like; and, under checking the
work, that an enclosure containing everything agrees with everything, and that
a measurement needs a control returning a known answer.

What it should say: add the same caution one level up, to the identity itself.
Before quoting a normalisation identity as a check, derive it and ask what
would have to be wrong for it to fail. An identity forced by the endpoints or
by integration by parts holds for any function of the right shape and tests
nothing; an identity that constrains the interior is the one to run. A check
that cannot fail is the enclosure-that-contains-everything in another form.

Evidence: 2026-09-22 ideas run, `/tmp/nd/rmt.py`. `int_0^12 E_2(0;s) ds`
returned `0.5899969388459202583145` against an asserted `1.0`, while in the
same run the Tracy-Widom mean computed from the same machinery reproduced every
published digit of $-1.771086807411$.

## Check an asymptotic law as a ratio at several arguments: a constant limit that is not 1 has found a missing factor

What happened: the same run checked the sine-kernel gap probability against
Dyson's large-$s$ asymptotic, written as
$E_2(0;s)\sim 2^{1/3}e^{3\zeta'(-1)}s^{-1/4}e^{-\pi^2s^2/8}$, by printing the
ratio of the computed determinant to that expression at $s = 3, 4, 6, 8, 10$:

    0.752261409655   0.751742120365   0.75139377189
    0.751275413187   0.751221172532

The ratio is not going to $1$ and it is not drifting: it is converging, and to
$\pi^{-1/4} = 0.7511255\ldots$. The asymptotic as written was missing a factor
$\pi^{-1/4}$, because the constant is quoted for a different normalisation of
the interval than the one the determinant used. With $(\pi s)^{-1/4}$ in place
of $s^{-1/4}$ the two agree across thirty-five orders of magnitude, and the
check has then confirmed the determinant, the constant $2^{1/3}e^{3\zeta'(-1)}$
and the convention all three at once.

Had the ratio been printed at one argument only, it would have read as a 25 %
disagreement and the check would have been abandoned or the values doubted.

What the skill says now: names "a specialisation to a sequence with an OEIS
number", "a value in a published table, quoted", and "an identity relating it
to a table this database already holds" as checks. Nothing about asymptotic
laws, which are the only published check available for many transcendentally
defined families.

What it should say: an asymptotic is a usable check if it is run as a *ratio at
several arguments* and the ratio is watched. Converging to $1$ confirms the
values. Converging to a constant other than $1$ locates a missing constant
factor, which is almost always a normalisation of the variable, and is the more
informative outcome. Only a ratio that fails to converge is evidence against
the values. Quote the ratios in `rigour details`, not the single worst one.

Evidence: 2026-09-22 ideas run, `/tmp/nd/rmt2.py`, the five ratios above at
`mp.dps = 30` with 80 Gauss-Legendre nodes.

## A constant defined by shooting along a separatrix loses digits at a rate you can predict, and the run reports success

What happened: the Hastings-McLeod solution of Painleve II, $q''=sq+2q^3$ with
$q(s)\sim\operatorname{Ai}(s)$ as $s\to+\infty$, was integrated with
`mpmath.odefun` at 30 digits, started from $q(8)=\operatorname{Ai}(8)$ and run
down to $s=-10$. Nothing complained. `odefun`'s own tolerance was met, the
solution was smooth, and $q(0) = 0.3670615515480783754726$ reproduced all
sixteen published digits. At the other end,

    q(-10)                                = 2.235562042664078474338
    sqrt(-x/2)(1 + x^-3/8 - 73 x^-6/128 + 10657 x^-9/1024) = 2.235787170473817753857
    ratio                                 = 0.9998993071376773

— four correct digits out of thirty, against an asymptotic series whose own
truncation error there is about $10^{-11}$. The Hastings-McLeod solution is the
single trajectory that neither blows up nor turns over, so integrating along it
amplifies every error, including the one in the starting value: linearising
gives $\delta''=(s+6q^2)\delta$, which for $s<0$ is $\delta''\approx2|s|\delta$,
and the amplification from $s=8$ to $s=-10$ is of order $10^{19.5}$. That
mechanism explains the loss and *understates* it -- the loss measured was
twenty-six digits. Which is the point: the estimate tells you to expect a loss,
only the measurement tells you how much.

This is a whole class of constants, not one: Painleve transcendents, the
Thomas-Fermi initial slope, the Blasius constant, the Emden-Chandrasekhar
functions, anything defined as "the unique solution that does not blow up".
Every one of them is computed by shooting, and every one loses digits silently.

What the skill says now: the rigour levels, and that `proven` is refused for a
value carrying no error of its own. An ODE solver's own tolerance *is* an error
of its own, so a value from one can pass every refusal the package makes while
being wrong in its ninth digit.

What it should say: for a value defined as the solution of a boundary-value
problem, the solver's tolerance is not the error. Measure the loss by comparing
against the asymptotic at the far end of the range, quote the measured number
of correct digits in `rigour details`, and set the working precision from it
rather than from the digits wanted. Where the family runs in both directions,
integrate from the end where the instability decays.

Evidence: 2026-09-22 ideas run, `/tmp/nd/rmt2.py`, at `mp.dps = 30`.
$q(6)/\operatorname{Ai}(6) = 1.000000000003844$ shows the start of the
integration was right, which is what makes the left-hand loss attributable to
the separatrix and not to the initial condition.

## Fredholm determinants: the Nystrom convergence is not the error, the working precision is

What happened: the Tracy-Widom law and the sine-kernel gap probability were
computed by the standard Nystrom recipe -- Gauss-Legendre nodes $x_i$ with
weights $w_i$, the matrix $\delta_{ij}-\sqrt{w_iw_j}K(x_i,x_j)$, and its
determinant. The question was how many nodes a 25-digit table needs. The answer
is: far fewer than expected, because the convergence in the node count is
faster than exponential for an analytic kernel.

    F_2(-8), 50 nodes   1.9859004257636490035e-19
    F_2(-8), 70 nodes   1.9859004257636574793e-19
    difference          8.5e-34

Thirty-three agreeing digits from fifty nodes. The cost, at 30 decimal digits
of working precision, is `1.4 s` at 40 nodes, `6.9 s` at 80 and `10.5 s` at
100, so the node count is nearly free and the precision is what to spend on.
Two independent routes to the same number then agreed to seventeen digits:
$\det(I-A_0) = 0.9693728283552626683499$ against
$\exp(-\int_0^\infty xq(x)^2dx) = 0.9693728283552626765202$ from the Painleve
solution.

Everything needed is in mpmath, which Sage carries: `mp.airyai(x)`,
`mp.airyai(x, 1)`, `mp.matrix`, `mp.det`, `mp.odefun`. No Sage ring is
involved, so none of the import order the skill describes applies -- but
equally, nothing here carries an error of its own, so the result can be
`heuristic (agreement-checked)` and cannot be `proven`.

What the skill says now: nothing about Fredholm determinants, which are how a
large family of constants in random matrix theory, integrable probability and
statistical mechanics is defined.

What it should say: a value defined as a Fredholm determinant is computed by
the Nystrom method, and the agreement check to run is two node counts and two
working precisions -- in that order of cheapness, since doubling the nodes
costs little and buys many digits while the precision costs everything. Say in
`rigour details` which pair was used and what they agreed to.

Evidence: 2026-09-22 ideas run, `/tmp/nd/rmt.py` and `/tmp/nd/rmt2.py`, Sage's
mpmath, all timings on the container `agents/sage.sh` runs.
