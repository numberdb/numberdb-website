## A source can name a family and still define a different one

What happened: proposal 4 of the simplex batch is the Cantor polynomial of
$\mathbb{N}^d$, and the obvious source is Wikipedia's *Pairing function*. The
screen passed it: the page contains "Cantor", which is the only
distinguishing word in "Cantor polynomials". Reading the page showed it gives
*two* generalisations to $d$ variables and calls the wrong one Cantor's -- the
nested $\pi(\pi(x_1,x_2),x_3)$, whose degree doubles with each variable --
while the degree-$d$ layer enumeration that actually generalises the picture
in the request is attributed there to the combinatorial number system. The
page that ties the name to the degree-$d$ polynomial is *Fueter-Pólya
theorem*, in its "Higher dimensions" section, and it also says there are at
least $(d-1)!$ inequivalent packing polynomials in dimension $d$ -- which is a
decision about what the table holds, and would have been missed entirely by a
proposal that cited the first page and stopped.

What the skill says now: cite a source for the family, because it is the only
check that the family is real and is called what you say.

What it should say: the same, plus that the check is on the *name*, not on the
definition. Read the section the source defines the family in, and quote the
formula into the proposal or the table. A page can mention a name while
defining something else under it, and a family with more than one published
generalisation is exactly where that happens.

Evidence: 2026-09-21, simplex batch. `source_names_it('Cantor polynomials',
'https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pairing_function')` returns `None`, as does
the same call against
`https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fueter–Pólya_theorem`; the two pages give
different degree-$d$ polynomials.

## The polynomial key matches renamed variables, never rescaled ones

What happened: the Lagrange basis of the discrete simplex exists in two
conventions -- nodes at the integer points $\{\alpha : |\alpha| \le k\}$, and
nodes at $\alpha/k$ on the unit simplex -- related by $x \mapsto kx$. T112
stores the first for $d = 1$: $\ell_{2,0} = \tfrac12 x^2 - \tfrac32 x + 1$.
The unit-simplex form of the same function is $2x^2 - 3x + 1$. A reader
holding one of them and searching by polynomial finds nothing of the other,
because the key that matches polynomials is invariant under renaming variables
and not under scaling them. In the other direction the invariance is a gift:
the two Cantor polynomials of the Fueter-Pólya theorem differ by swapping $x$
and $y$, so storing one finds both, and only one needs a row.

What the skill says now: it explains that a value has to be *findable*, not
merely derivable, and that a family reachable only by guessing an unguessable
multiple is not covered. It says of polynomials only that the search key
matches families differing in variable names.

What it should say: for a polynomial family, the same findability question is
answered by the key. Permutations and renamings of the variables are one
entry; a rescaled, shifted or otherwise substituted variant is a different
entry that no search reaches from the first, so a table that picks one
convention must say which, and should name the table holding the other. The
choice between two conventions of a polynomial family is therefore a choice
about who can find it, not a matter of taste.

Evidence: 2026-09-21. `numberdb.table('T112')['Numbers']['2']['0']` is
`'1/2*x^2 - 3/2*x + 1'`; the unit-simplex basis computed today for $d=1$,
$k=2$, $\alpha=(2,0)$ is `2*x^2 - 3*x + 1`, and each is the other under
$x \mapsto 2x$.

## How long a polynomial basis in two or three variables prints

What happened: the batch needed ranges for four families of multivariate
polynomials, and the skill's measured table of entry lengths is for
one-variable families indexed by degree. Measured today, printed characters
of the expanded polynomial on the unit simplex:

    Lagrange   d=2 k= 6:  28 entries, longest  353, cumulative   8.8 KB
    Lagrange   d=2 k= 9:  55 entries, longest 1052, cumulative  64.8 KB
    Lagrange   d=2 k=12:  91 entries, longest 1929, cumulative 271.2 KB
    Lagrange   d=3 k= 6:  84 entries, longest 1167, cumulative  37.7 KB
    Lagrange   d=3 k= 9: 220 entries, longest 4718, cumulative 466.9 KB
    Bernstein  d=2 k= 9:  55 entries, longest  622, cumulative  23.4 KB
    Bernstein  d=3 k= 6:  84 entries, longest  951, cumulative  19.7 KB
    Koornwinder degree 12: 13 entries, longest 1485, cumulative  44.4 KB

Two things a one-variable measurement does not show. The number of entries at
degree $k$ is $\binom{k+d}{d}$, so the table grows in two directions at once
and a dimension costs more than a degree: the Lagrange table reaches the soft
block limit at $k=12$ on the triangle and at $k=9$ on the tetrahedron. And two
bases of the *same* space are not the same size -- the Lagrange basis prints
about three times longer than the Bernstein basis at every $(d,k)$, because
its coefficients are dense rationals where Bernstein's are a single
multinomial coefficient times a monomial.

What the skill says now: a house range of $n$ up to about 100 for polynomial
families indexed by degree, with 69 to 164 KB measured for Chebyshev, Hermite
and Legendre.

What it should say: that range is for one variable. In $d$ variables, measure
before choosing, because entries per degree grow like $k^d$ and the printed
length of each grows too; a degree in the twenties is already out of reach on
a triangle. And where a batch holds two bases of one space, choose the grid
for the more expensive one and carry the cheaper one no further, so a reader
can convert between them at every entry.

Evidence: 2026-09-21, run through `agents/sage.sh` over
`QQ['x,y']` and `QQ['x,y,z']`, measuring `len(str(p))` of each expanded basis
polynomial.
