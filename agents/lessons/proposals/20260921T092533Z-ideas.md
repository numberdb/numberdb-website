# Lessons from the ideas run of 2026-09-21T0925Z (zeros of zeta functions)

## `source_names_it` passes on a page that is about something else

What happened: the proposal for *Zeros of the derivative of the Riemann zeta
function* cited OEIS **A074760** and `source_names_it` answered OK. A074760 is
not zeros of $\zeta'$. It is the decimal expansion of $\lambda(1)$ in Li's
criterion -- which is, incidentally, already the first entry of T31. The check
passed because it asks only whether the distinguishing words appear anywhere on
the page, and a page about $\lambda_1$ names Riemann, zeta and the derivative
in its comments. The real sequences are **A356216** (real part) and **A356092**
(imaginary part) of the first non-real zero of $\zeta'$, and they were found
only by searching OEIS by phrase and *reading* what came back.

What the skill says now: it names `source_names_it` as "the only check that a
family is real and is called what you say".

What it should say: `source_names_it` is a spell-check on the name, not a
check on the source. It catches a family nobody calls that; it cannot catch a
page that uses all the same words about a different object. Read the page. The
failure mode is invisible -- a wrong citation that passes the screen reads
exactly like a right one, which is the thing the screen exists to prevent.

Evidence: `source_names_it('Zeros of the derivative of the Riemann zeta
function', 'https://oeis.org/A074760')` returns `None`; the sequence's name is
"Decimal expansion of lambda(1) in Li's criterion".

## Search OEIS for the *first member* of a family of reals, not for the family

What happened: the family proposed was the non-real zeros of $\zeta'$, a
sequence of complex numbers, so OEIS looked like the wrong place. It holds the
first zero as **two** separate decimal expansions, A356216 and A356092, and
nothing after it -- exactly the shape the skill describes OEIS as bad at. But
those two entries carried 60 digits of an independent computation, the
statement of Speiser's theorem, the Levinson-Montgomery reference, and the
location of a trivial real zero at $-2.717262829204574\ldots$. All of that went
into the proposal and none of it would have been found by searching for the
family.

What the skill says now: OEIS is named as a link target, and a proposed lesson
already says to search it for a *polynomial* family as a coefficient triangle.

What it should say: search OEIS for the first, best-known member of a family of
real or complex numbers, as a "decimal expansion of". One member is usually
there with full digits and the definitive comment, and it is the cheapest
independent check a build will get.

Evidence: A356216, A356092 (zeros of $\zeta'$); A158241 (the
Davenport-Heilbronn $\kappa$, below).

## A nested surd in the literature has two readings and both are numbers

What happened: the Davenport-Heilbronn function needs a constant written in
several sources as $\kappa=(\sqrt{10-2\sqrt5}-2)/(\sqrt5-1)$. Read as
$(\sqrt{10}-2-\sqrt5)/(\sqrt5-1)$ -- which is how it renders when the vinculum
is lost, and how it appears in more than one secondary source -- it is
$-0.8687\ldots$; read correctly it is $+0.2840790438\ldots$. Both are perfectly
plausible constants and the function built from either satisfies *a* functional
equation, so nothing downstream would have complained. OEIS A158241 stores
$\arctan\kappa=0.27678717944852262575\ldots$ and settles it in one fetch.

What the skill says now: "Decide exactness from the definition, never from the
digits", and a good deal about conventions, but nothing about a formula that
can be *parsed* two ways.

What it should say: when a defining constant is written with a nested radical,
a continued fraction, or any structure that loses information in plain text,
evaluate every reading and pin the right one against an independent numerical
record before writing it into a definition. A table that is wrong this way is
self-consistent and unfalsifiable from inside.

Evidence: $(\sqrt{10-2\sqrt5}-2)/(\sqrt5-1)=0.28407904384041229602829183\ldots$
computed in `RealBallField(160)`; $\tan(0.2767871794485226\ldots)$ from OEIS
A158241 agrees.

## "Could a reader find it already" is a check, not an argument

What happened: the batch considered a table of the zeros of Dedekind zeta
functions of quadratic fields, and the objection is the usual one --
$\zeta_K=\zeta\cdot L(s,\chi_D)$, so every zero is already in T3 or T4. That is
normally where the reasoning stops, as prose. It does not have to. The zeros
were computed (`lfunzeros(lfuncreate(bnfinit(x^2+1,1)), 16)`) and matched
against the actual stored strings of T4 through the client, and all of them
matched to every digit T4 carries.

The same check then *saved* a different table. For cyclic cubic fields the same
factorisation holds with cubic characters, and six of the six lowest zeros of
$\zeta_K$ for the conductor-7 field turned out to be in T4 -- split between two
Conrey labels, `7,2` and `7,4`, three each. That is coverage a reader could
never reconstruct from the number in their hand, so the cubic table survives
while the quadratic one does not, and the proposal can say which rows repeat
what.

What the skill says now: a family whose values are a small guessable multiple
of a stored one is arguably covered; being derivable is not being findable.

What it should say: where the family factors through something the corpus
holds, *run the comparison* -- compute a dozen values and match them against
the stored strings. It takes a few minutes, it turns a judgement into a fact,
and it tells you which rows to mark `repeats` rather than whether to abandon
the table.

Evidence: T4 groups `7,2` and `7,4` contain `4.35640162473628422727957479051`,
`8.78555471449907536558015746317`, `10.73611998749339311587424153504` and
`6.20123004275588129466099054628`, `7.92743089809203774838798659746`,
`11.010444862072490422393627410948`; PARI returns exactly those six as the
first zeros of $\zeta_K$ for $x^3-x^2-2x+1$.

## arb spells the Hurwitz zeta and the zeta derivative differently from mpmath

What happened: two Sage runs were lost to guessing the names. On a Sage
`ComplexBall`, the Hurwitz zeta is `z.zeta(a)` -- the parameter is a positional
argument to `zeta`, not a method of its own. `z.hurwitz_zeta(a)` raises
`AttributeError`, and `z.zeta(derivative=1)` raises `TypeError`; the derivative
is `z.zetaderiv(k)`. Both exist and both return balls, so both support
`proven`.

What the skill says now: it lists `zeta`, `zetaderiv` and "Hurwitz
`zeta(s, a)`" among the arb functions, which is right but reads as three
functions rather than two names and an argument.

What it should say: `RealBall`/`ComplexBall.zeta(a=None)` is the Hurwitz zeta
with `a` given and the Riemann zeta without; `zetaderiv(k)` is the $k$-th
derivative of the Riemann zeta. There is no `hurwitz_zeta` method and no
`derivative=` keyword.

Evidence: `CBF(3/2 + 7/5*I).zeta(CBF(1/2))` and `(CBF(2)**s-1)*s.zeta()` agree
to 59 digits with overlapping balls, which is the identity
$\zeta(s,\tfrac12)=(2^s-1)\zeta(s)$; `CBF(...).hurwitz_zeta(...)` raises
`AttributeError`, `CBF(...).zeta(derivative=1)` raises `TypeError`.

## A grid scan says where a family's interesting values are *not*

What happened: two of the proposed tables are worth making because the function
has zeros off the critical line. The obvious question -- at what height -- was
answered by a coarse grid of $|f|$ rather than by the literature, and the
answer changed the proposal. For the Davenport-Heilbronn function every small
value of $|f|$ on $0.5\leq\sigma\leq1.3$, $0<t\leq120$ sat at $\sigma=0.5$; for
$\zeta(s,\tfrac13)$ the smallest $|\zeta|$ anywhere in $1\leq\sigma\leq1.2$,
$0<t\leq120$ was $1.709$. So the zeros that make both families interesting are
above the range a first build would naturally pick, and a table stopping at
$t=120$ would hold only the ordinary ones and imply, by omission, that there
are no others.

What the skill says now: "Hold the numbers that turn up", and let the arguments
choose the grid rather than the count.

What it should say: where a family is worth a table *because* of an exceptional
subset, find out at what parameter the exceptional subset starts before
choosing the range, and put that height in the `complete-note`. A scan of the
modulus on a coarse grid costs minutes and is the difference between a table
that demonstrates the phenomenon and one that hides it.

Evidence: 2026-09-21 ideas run, `probe2.py`; smallest $|f|$ on the grid
$0.0416$ at $\sigma=0.50$, $t=72.5$, and the next nine all at $\sigma=0.50$.
