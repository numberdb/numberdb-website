# Proposed lessons from the T18 growth critique, 2026-09-21

Two. Both would be true for somebody with Python and Sage on their own laptop:
one is about what a table has to store for a search to answer, and one is about
what a definition has to say for a table to be extensible at all. The
deployment-only findings from the same reading are in
`docs/agent-environment.md` instead.

---

## Where the literature prints a magnitude and your source certifies a sign, store both as rows

What happened: T18 holds the second Feigenbaum constant as
$\alpha = -2.502907875\ldots$. That sign is correct and is the one that makes
the table's own functional equation $g(\alpha x)/\alpha = g(g(x))$ true; it is
the sign Broadhurst's letter, the table's source, writes. But almost nobody
else writes it. Wikipedia prints $\alpha = 2.502907875\ldots$, MathWorld prints
it positive, and **both OEIS sequences the table links** print it positive:
A006891 is "Decimal expansion of Feigenbaum reduction parameter",
$2.5029\ldots$.

So a reader who has just read any of those three pages, or who has computed a
tine ratio and taken its width, types the number they are holding into the
search box and is told:

    ?q=2.5029078750958928     ->  "No match in database"
    ?q=-2.5029078750958928    ->  1 result: Feigenbaum constants #alpha

The search matches the number as stored and does not try the negation, which is
right -- $-x$ and $x$ are different numbers and a database of numbers should
not pretend otherwise. The consequence is that one of the two best-known
constants in bifurcation theory is unfindable in the table that holds it, from
the form in which it is usually written.

What the skill says now: two rules that meet here and do not cover the case.
"Do not discard what the source certified" (the T174 story) says to keep the
sign, and adds: "Where the literature quotes a magnitude, as it does for the
Gauss-Kuzmin-Wirsing constant $|\lambda_2|$, that convention is about one
value; say so in that entry's comment and store the rest as they are." And
"Where two entries are one object in two conventions, one table" gives
$\beta$ and $e^\beta$ for Levy's constant as two rows of one table.

The first of those is advice to write a comment, and **a comment answers no
search** -- the skill says so itself two sections earlier, about entry comments
being in none of the four index weights. Where the magnitude convention is not
one table's quirk but the form the whole literature prints, the comment is the
wrong instrument.

What it should say: a sign convention is a convention like any other, so the
Levy rule governs it. When the sign your source certifies is not the sign the
standard references print, store both, as two rows of the same table with the
symbols $\alpha$ and $|\alpha|$ -- the reader holding either lands in the same
place, which is the whole test for one table in two conventions -- and say in
a comment which convention makes which sign. One sentence, beside the T174
paragraph, and worth an `audit_table` check: a table whose Links name an OEIS
sequence, all of whose stored values are negative, is worth asking about,
because OEIS decimal expansions are unsigned by construction.

Not every derived form deserves a row: $1/\alpha$ and $\alpha^2$ are arithmetic
a reader can guess, and the skill's "a reader tries a half, a two, a pi"
covers them. A sign is not a guess a search box makes for you.

Evidence: 2026-09-21, T18 critique. The searches above are
`https://numberdb.org/?q=...`, the reader-facing box, and `/api/lookup` agrees
(`text=2.5029078750958928` returns zero results, `text=-2.5029...` returns
`T18[alpha]`). Source: `Links[OEIS_alpha]` is A006891, whose b-file begins
`1 2`, `2 5`, `3 0` -- unsigned. The negative sign is Broadhurst,
`http://www.plouffe.fr/simon/constants/feigenbaum.txt`.

## A definition that names a person's list instead of the objects cannot be extended by anybody but that person

What happened: T18's definition reads, in full:

    $\delta$ and $\alpha$ are the first and second Feigenbaum constants
    CITE{Wiki} CITE{MathWorld}. $b$, $c$, $d$, $\kappa$ are additional
    Feigenbaum constants considered by Broadhurst CITE{Broadhurst}.

Every word of that is true, and four of the six entries are undefined by it. A
reader holding $1.8312589849\ldots$ learns that it is a constant somebody
considered. The table has no `Comments` and no `Formulas`, so nothing else on
the page says more.

The growth consequence is the sharp one, and it is why this is worth a rule
rather than a note. The boundary of the family is a bibliographic fact -- what
was in one letter of 22 March 1999 -- so the question "is the table complete?"
cannot be answered from the mathematics at all, by the author or by anybody
after them. It happens that T18 *is* complete, because the six constants are
the complete analytic description of one function (the scaling constant, the
eigenvalue, the nearest real zero of $g$, its nearest complex singularity, and
the order of that singularity), and Broadhurst's letter states all of that in
five lines. Those five lines are what the definition should have carried. With
them the table can be seen to be finished; without them it can only be seen to
match a file.

What the skill says now: "**Definition** holds one or two sentences saying what
the object *is*", and that a definition should be "precise enough that two
people would build the same table". It warns about definitions that hold too
much -- history, caveats, conventions -- and not about one that holds a name
where the object should be.

What it should say: a definition that identifies its objects by who studied
them ("the constants considered by X", "the cases treated in [3]") has not
defined them. State the equation or the property each entry satisfies, and let
the citation support it rather than replace it. The test is the completeness
note: if you cannot say what the next entry would be without opening the
source, the definition is a bibliography. One sentence, in the section on
where each thing goes, beside the two-sentence rule.

Evidence: 2026-09-21, T18 critique. `Definition` as quoted, `Comments` and
`Formulas` both `{}`, and `Data properties` carries no `complete` key at all.
The five lines that define the six constants are in the cited source: with $g$,
$f$ even and $g(0)=f(0)=1$, $g(\alpha x)/\alpha = g(g(x))$ and
$\delta f(\alpha x)/\alpha = g'(g(x))f(x)+f(g(x))$ with $\delta$ as large as
possible; $b$, $c$, $d$ positive with $g(b)=0=1/g(c+id)$ and $b$, $c^2+d^2$ as
small as possible; $1/g(c+id+z) = O(z^\kappa)$. The digit lengths the table
stores, 1018 for $\alpha$, $\delta$, $b$ and 868 for $c$, $d$, $\kappa$, follow
from two further sentences of the same letter and are unexplained on the page.
