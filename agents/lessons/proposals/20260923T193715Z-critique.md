# Lessons proposed by the T449 critique, 2026-09-23

Format as `agents/lessons/PROPOSALS.md`. Nothing here is in force.

---

## A per-row credit is a claim about a paper's range, and the review states the range

What happened: T449 carries "Optimality is proven by CITE{Melissen}" on all
twenty-eight of its entries, copied row by row from the sibling table T394,
which does the same. The reference is Melissen's 1993 *Monthly* paper, the one
the source article cites under its table of side lengths. The zbMATH review of
that paper (Zbl 0814.52006) says what is actually in it: the author "lists such
configurations for $n = 2,3,4,\dots,10,12$ and for $n = k(k+1)/2$, $k \geq 2$
… The case $n = 11$ is announced." So the credit is false on three of the
fourteen values of $n$ the table holds: $n=11$ is announced rather than proved
there (the proof is Melissen 1994, Acta Math. Hungar. 65, 389-393), $n=13$ is
absent from it, and $n=14$ is the Erdős-Oler case that the table's *other*
reference is about. Six entries assert something no paper the table cites
supports.

Nothing catches this. The audit does not read references, `verify` recomputes
values and says nothing about prose, and the sentence is grammatical, plausible
and in the right section. It arrived by copying a published table's shape into a
family with a different literature: T394 divides its per-row credits correctly
across five papers, T449 flattened them to one.

The cheap check is a bibliographic database rather than the paper. A zbMATH
review (open, no login, one `curl`) states the range in its first sentence more
often than not, and a survey that footnotes a *single* row separately -- the
source article here attaches its own reference to thirteen points -- is the tell
that the range is split over several papers. Both took under a minute.

What the skill says now: "Verify a claim before writing it into a table,
including one somebody suggested. A suggestion is a hypothesis." And, of
comments, "A comment states a fact about the mathematics." Neither warns that
an attribution is such a claim, and neither says where to check one.

What it should say: a sentence naming who proved a value is a claim about a
paper, not about the value, and it is wrong far more easily than the value is --
especially where it was copied onto every row at once. Check the range the paper
actually covers before writing its name on a row: the zbMATH review usually
gives it in one line, and where a survey cites a different paper for one case,
the credit is split. A table whose rows were proved by different people should
either say so once in `rigour details`, naming each range, or carry the right
paper on each row. One paper's name on every row of a family with a hundred
years of literature is a claim nobody made.

Evidence: 2026-09-23, T449 critique. `GET /api/table?id=T449` (all 28 entry
comments end "Optimality is proven by CITE{Melissen}");
`https://zbmath.org/?q=Melissen+Densest+packings+of+congruent+circles+in+an+equilateral+triangle`
-> Zbl 0814.52006, review by B. Kind, the sentence quoted above;
`https://zbmath.org/?q=Melissen+eleven+equal+circles+equilateral+triangle` ->
Zbl 0807.52014, the 1994 paper that proves $n=11$; the cited Wikipedia article's
reference list, which footnotes Tedeschi 2021 for thirteen points and Payan 1997
for Erdős-Oler up to 15. T394's `rigour details`, which divides the same kind
of claim correctly, is the model.

## A `Programs` snippet has to print, and has to compute something the table does not store

What happened: T449's `Programs` is Sage that builds $S_8$ from the radical
already written in row 8's comment, derives $d_8$ from it, and ends with the
bare expression

    d8, S8

which prints the pair inside the Sage REPL and prints nothing at all when run
the way the skill's own generator instructions run a file, `sage -python
generate.py`. The published T394 ends `d3, R3` for the same reason, so the shape
propagates by copying. Neither snippet answers the question `Programs` exists
for: a reader who wants one more value cannot get $S_{16}$ from either, because
the code is a transcription of one stored row rather than a computation of an
arbitrary one.

What the skill says now: "`Programs` is the standard incantation in Sage, PARI
or mpmath for a reader who wants one more value", and separately, of generators,
that a file is opened with the commands to run it. Nothing says the snippet is
run rather than read, and the worked example in section 6 is a generator with a
`__main__` that prints, so the omission is invisible from the skill.

What it should say: a `Programs` snippet is pasted into a file as often as into
a prompt, and a trailing bare expression prints only in the REPL. End it with
`print(...)`. And it earns its place by computing a value the table does not
hold: a snippet that re-evaluates a closed form the entry comment already gives
is a copy of the table, not a way past its last row. Where the family has no
algorithm and the values come from the literature, the incantation that does
generalise is the one for the sub-family that has a formula -- here the
triangular $n$, where $S_n=2(k-1)+2\sqrt3$ gives every $k$.

Evidence: 2026-09-23, T449 critique. `GET /api/table?id=T449`, `Programs`
program-sage, last line `d8, S8`; the same table's formula (2) gives every
triangular $n$ and is not what the snippet computes; T394's `program-sage`,
published, last line `d3, R3`.
