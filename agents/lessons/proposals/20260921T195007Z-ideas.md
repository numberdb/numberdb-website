# Lessons from the 2026-09-21T19:50Z ideation run

The batch is *the limit laws of random matrix theory*: Tracy--Widom at the
edge, the sine kernel in the bulk, Painlevé II behind both. Four lessons, none
about this deployment.

## A normalisation check the algebra makes automatic is not a check

What happened: to test that a proposed table of level-spacing densities would
be correctly unfolded -- mean spacing one -- this run computed the gap
probability $E(0;s)$ by Fredholm determinant and set out to confirm
$\int_0^\infty s\,p(s)\,ds = 1$, where $p = E''$. It is a textbook
normalisation and it looked like the obvious control.

It is not a control. Integrating by parts twice,

    int_0^inf s E''(s) ds = [s E'(s)] - int_0^inf E'(s) ds = 0 + E(0) = 1

for *every* $E$ with $E(0) = 1$ that decays, whatever the kernel, whatever the
scaling, whatever the mistake. The same is true of $\int p = -E'(0)$ only if
$E'(0)$ is itself checked. A run that had mis-scaled the kernel by $\pi$ -- the
commonest error in this subject, because both $\sin(x-y)/(x-y)$ and
$\sin\pi(x-y)/\pi(x-y)$ are in print -- would have passed this check and
published a table whose every value was the right function of the wrong
argument.

The variant that does bite is the one that cannot be satisfied by an identity:
$E'(0) = -1$, measured directly from a short interval, and the parity
factorisation $E_+(s)E_-(s) = E_2(0;s)$, which relates two separately computed
determinants. Both caught nothing here because nothing was wrong, which is what
a control looks like when it works.

Related to this: the first thing I tried was $\int_0^\infty E(0;s)\,ds = 1$,
which is not an identity at all -- it came out 0.590 for $\beta=2$ -- and for a
minute the correct computation looked broken. A check has to be an identity
before its failure means anything.

What the skill says now: "A measurement needs a control that returns a known
answer", with the Simpson's-rule example where the control silently returned
zero. That is about a control that is broken. This is about a control that
works perfectly and tests nothing.

What it should say: before trusting a normalisation check, derive it. If it
follows from the definition by algebra alone -- a moment identity that integration
by parts gives for any function in the class -- it will hold for a wrong
computation too. Prefer a check that relates two independently computed things
(a factorisation, a second method, a published value) over one that relates a
computation to itself.

Evidence: 2026-09-21, `/tmp/w3b/tw_check4.py` through `agents/sage.sh`. The
integrals of $E_2$, $E_+$ and $E_-$ over $(0,7)$ printed 0.590, 0.643 and
1.565; $E'(0)$ from a $10^{-3}$ interval printed $-0.999999999726$;
$E_+E_-/E_2 - 1$ printed $2.2\cdot10^{-28}$, $1.1\cdot10^{-26}$,
$2.6\cdot10^{-25}$ at $s = 1, 2, 3$.

## Where a family has two conventions in print, a published moment settles it and the formula in your head does not

What happened: the Tracy--Widom law for $\beta = 4$ is written both as
$F_4(s)$ and as $F_4(s/\sqrt2)$, by authors who each think their version is
the obvious one, and the two differ by a factor that no property of the
resulting numbers betrays: both are increasing, both go from 0 to 1, both have
the right shape, and the digits of one are as plausible as the digits of the
other. The same trap is in the bulk, where the sine kernel is written with and
without the $\pi$.

What settled it in half a minute of arithmetic was a *moment*: the published
means of the three laws are $-1.206533574$, $-1.771086807411601$ and
$-2.306884893$, and computing the mean of what the determinants produce says at
once which convention they are in. This run's quadrature returned $-1.20653362$
for $\det(I - A_s)$, so that determinant is $F_1$ with no rescaling; and
$-3.2624279$ for $\tfrac12[\det(I-A_s)+\det(I+A_s)]$, which is
$\sqrt2 \times (-2.306884893)$ and not $-2.306884893$, so that determinant is
$F_4$ of a rescaled argument.

A moment is the right discriminator because it is one number, it is published
for almost any distribution anybody cares about, it is insensitive to the
details of the method, and a scale error moves it. A value of the function at a
point is worse: fewer are published, and an argument convention shifts which
point you are at.

What the skill says now: "Normalisation, branch, indexing" must be written down
before computing, with the `elliptic_k(m)` versus modulus $k$ example. It says
to decide, and not how to find out which you decided.

What it should say: when the literature has two scalings, do not decide from
memory of a formula. Compute a published moment -- a mean, a variance, a total
mass -- and read the convention off it, and put that comparison in
`rigour details` where the next person can see which convention the table is
in without re-deriving it.

Evidence: 2026-09-21, `/tmp/w3b/tw_check.py` and `tw_check3.py` through
`agents/sage.sh`. Means by Gauss--Legendre quadrature over the determinants:
$-1.771086807411606$ (published $-1.771086807411601$), variance
$0.8131947928328999$ (published $0.813194792832984$), $-1.20653362$ (GOE) and
$-3.2624279$ (GSE, scaled).

## The title that passes `source_names_it` is usually the title a reader types

What happened: the batch wanted a table of the cumulants of the Tracy--Widom
laws. `source_names_it("Cumulants of the Tracy-Widom distributions",
"https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tracy–Widom_distribution")` complains that the
source does not mention "cumulants" -- and it is right: that article gives the
mean, the variance, the skewness and the excess kurtosis, and never uses the
word. "Cumulant" is correct mathematics and is what a probabilist would write.
It is not what the reader of this database has in their hand, and it is not
what they will type.

Retitling to *Mean and variance of the Tracy--Widom distributions* and
*Skewness and kurtosis of the Tracy--Widom distributions* passes the screen and
also puts the two search terms in the title, which is the field the index
weighs highest. The screen, meant as a check against inventing a family, turned
out to be a check on whether the title is written in the reader's vocabulary.

The trap is treating the complaint as a formality to be worked around by
finding some page that does print the word. The complaint is information.

What the skill says now: "A title is what a search reaches", with the
Feigenbaum and Khinchin examples, and the instruction to put what a reader
would type in `Keywords` if it is not in the title.

What it should say: the same rule with a test attached. If the encyclopedia
article about your family never uses the word you put in the title, the word is
probably a specialist's name for it, and the article's own vocabulary is the
better title. `source_names_it` already tells you which words those are.

Evidence: 2026-09-21. The Wikipedia article contains "skewness", "kurtosis",
"critical", "empty", "interval", "spacing", "density" and not "cumulant",
"moment", "quantile" or "percentage"; all seven titles in the batch were chosen
from words the cited page actually prints, and all seven pass.

## An arXiv abstract page does not contain the names the paper is about

What happened: to cite the standard numerical reference for these
distributions, this run screened the family against
`https://arxiv.org/abs/0904.1581`, which is Bornemann's "On the Numerical
Evaluation of Distributions in Random Matrix Theory: A Review" -- exactly the
right paper. `source_names_it("Tracy-Widom distribution", ...)` reports that the
page mentions neither "tracy" nor "widom", and a word-by-word check of the
fetched text confirms it: 4956 characters of title, abstract, subject class and
site furniture, in which the distributions the paper is entirely about are
never named. The abstract speaks of "distributions in random matrix theory".

So a screen against an abstract page can fail for a perfectly good source, and
the failure is indistinguishable from citing a paper that has nothing to do
with the family. The same is true of most abstracts: they are a paragraph, and
the family's name is often only in the body.

What the skill says now: it names "a paper" as one of the durable link targets,
beside Wikipedia, LMFDB, OEIS and MathWorld, and says nothing about what an
abstract page contains.

What it should say: screen the *name* against a reference work -- Wikipedia,
MathWorld, DLMF -- and cite the paper separately for the method or the values.
DLMF is the strongest of the three for this purpose: §32.11 named Hastings,
McLeod, Painlevé, Tracy and Widom on one page, which is four families at once.

Evidence: 2026-09-21. `urllib` with a User-Agent fetched
`https://arxiv.org/abs/0904.1581` successfully (4956 characters, correct
title); a search of the extracted text for each of `tracy`, `widom`,
`cumulant`, `skewness`, `kurtosis`, `moment`, `quantile`, `percentage` and
`gap` found only `probabilit`.

## Walking the corpus by T-number spends the search rate limit, and the refusal reads like an empty corpus

What happened: the skill says there is no call that lists the corpus and that
to see everything you walk T-numbers, so this run read `numberdb.table('T1')`
through `T400` to get the titles. 388 answered; the rest raised
`RateLimitError`. The next thing the run did was `already_here` on sixteen
candidate names, and every one of the sixteen came back

    (could not ask the corpus: RateLimitError: too many requests; retry in 236s)

which `screen.py` prints, honestly, in the same shape as a list of matches. The
rate limit is per account and shared between the walk and the searches, so the
survey of the corpus is exactly what stops you from screening against it, and
the retry window is about four minutes.

The screen's own docstring already warns that a failed question and an empty
answer must not look the same, and its wording saved this run. A contributor who
writes their own loop will not have that.

What the skill says now: "There is no call that lists the corpus; to see
everything, walk T-numbers." It does not say what that costs.

What it should say: the walk is a few hundred requests and will exhaust the
search rate limit for several minutes; do it once, cache the titles in a file,
and put a retry with a sleep around anything that searches afterwards. And
never let an exception from a search be rendered as an empty result.

Evidence: 2026-09-21. `numberdb.table('T%d')` for 1..400 returned 388 tables,
one `NumberDBError` and eleven `RateLimitError`; sixteen immediately following
`numberdb.search_text` calls all raised `RateLimitError` with "retry in 236s"
counting down; a version that sleeps 70 s and retries returned the real matches.
