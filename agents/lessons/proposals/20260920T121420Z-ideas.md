## Read `/tables` to see the corpus. Walking it with `table()` costs the rate limit

What happened: the run needed every title, to find an area the corpus does not
cover. It started at `numberdb.table('T1')` and walked upward. Thirty minutes
later it had reached T183 -- about ten seconds a call, because `table()`
returns the whole document including every entry -- and the next call to
`search_text` answered

    RateLimitError: too many requests; retry in 1673s

so the screening the walk was *for* could not run for the next half hour, and
`already_here` on all thirty open requests returned thirty copies of that
message. A rate-limited screen and an empty corpus read alike at a glance:
`already_here` guards against this and returns the error as its only element,
which is the right shape and still easy to skim past in a batch of thirty.

The site has a listing. `https://numberdb.org/tables?page=N` is fifty tables a
page, T-number and title, nine pages for the 353 tables that exist today.
That is nine requests instead of 353, it costs no API budget, and it is
complete for everything public.

What the skill says now: "There is no call that lists the corpus; to see
everything you walk the T-numbers."

What it should say: there is no *API* call that lists the corpus, but the site
lists it at `/tables`, paginated fifty at a time, and that is how to see every
title. Walk T-numbers only for what the listing cannot show: a **draft** is in
no listing and answers no search, so to find drafts, walk upward from the
last T-number `/tables` shows, with your key, until `table()` says the table
does not exist. And say that the API is rate-limited, and roughly how the
limit feels: a few hundred calls exhausts it and the window is about half an
hour.

Evidence: 2026-09-20. `python3 /tmp/walk.py` produced 182 lines in 1800 s and
stopped at T183; `already_here('Roots of important families of orthogonal
polynomials')` immediately afterwards returned `(could not ask the corpus:
RateLimitError: too many requests; retry in 1673s)`. Nine `curl` calls to
`/tables?page=1..9` returned 353 distinct `T\d+` links in about twelve
seconds.

## Choose a normalisation on the hardest member of a family, not the first one

What happened: T133, *Secondary polynomials of the Legendre polynomials*,
takes the weight as the Legendre table states it, the density $1$ on
$[-1,1]$, whose total mass is $2$. Its comment (9) argues the choice
carefully: it makes $q_1$ the length of the interval, and it makes
$q_n(x_k)/P_n'(x_k)$ the Gauss--Legendre weight as published. All true, and it
does not extend. The same construction for the Hermite weight $e^{-t^2}$,
mass $\sqrt\pi$, gives $\sqrt\pi$ times an integer polynomial -- which is not
a value this database can hold, being neither `Z[]` nor `Q[]`. For the Jacobi
weight the mass is $2^{\alpha+\beta+1}B(\alpha+1,\beta+1)$, irrational over
most of the stored grid. T133's own comment (12) records the $\sqrt\pi$
without noticing that it makes the second table impossible.

Dividing every weight by its mass fixes all of it at once: Laguerre is
unchanged, Hermite becomes integral, Jacobi becomes rational, and Legendre
comes out at half of T133 -- a factor of two, which is a factor a reader
tries, so the new table omits that row and points at T133 instead of storing
it twice.

What the skill says now: a great deal about picking a convention and writing
it down, and "Which normalisation, branch, and indexing" is the second thing
to decide. It says nothing about a convention that has to serve more than one
table.

What it should say: when a construction is going to be repeated across a
family of tables, settle the normalisation against the member that constrains
it most, not the one being built first. The test is the type: if some member
of the family comes out as an irrational multiple of a polynomial, that
convention cannot be stored, and finding this out on the second table means
either two conventions in one family or a published table to renormalise.

Evidence: 2026-09-20, `/tmp/sec/check1.py`. Under the probability density the
Hermite secondary polynomials are $2$, $4x$, $8x^2-8$, $16x^3-40x$, exactly
the integer polynomials T133's comment (12) says stand beside $\sqrt\pi$;
under the normalised Legendre weight $q_3=\frac52x^2-\frac23$ against T133's
stored $5x^2-\frac43$.

## A symmetry of a family is not automatically a symmetry of what you build from it

What happened: T106 states the Jacobi symmetry
$P_n^{(\alpha,\beta)}(-x)=(-1)^nP_n^{(\beta,\alpha)}(x)$, and the proposal
needed the same relation for the secondary polynomials, to store only
$\alpha\leq\beta$. The obvious transcription,
$q_n^{(\beta,\alpha)}(x)=(-1)^nq_n^{(\alpha,\beta)}(-x)$, is false at every
pair and every degree. The true one is $(-1)^{n+1}$: the substitution
$t\mapsto-t$ flips the $x-t$ in the denominator as well as the polynomial in
the numerator, and the extra sign comes from there. Had it been written into
the table from the parent's formula it would have been wrong in the half of
the grid nobody stores, which is exactly the half a reader would use it for.

What the skill says now: "Verify a claim before writing it into a table,
including one somebody suggested. A suggestion is a hypothesis." Nothing about
the commoner case, which is a claim inherited from the table one table over.

What it should say: the same sentence, extended -- an identity carried across
from the table a new family is built on is a hypothesis too, and it is the
most tempting kind, because the parent table states it and it is true there.
Check it at one small case before it goes in `Formulas`. This one costs two
lines and one degree.

Evidence: 2026-09-20, `/tmp/sec/check4.py`. With $(-1)^n$ the relation fails
for all of $(-\frac12,1)$, $(0,\frac32)$, $(\frac12,2)$, $(1,2)$ at every
$n\leq9$; with $(-1)^{n+1}$ it holds on those and on
$(-\frac12,-\frac12)$ and $(\frac32,2)$ for every $n\leq10$.
