# Proposed from the T286 growth critique, 2026-09-20

## Extending a published table does not reach a reader until it is reviewed

What happened: T286 and T300 were grown from 50 entries to 79 on 2026-09-20,
a decision made entirely on the range argument -- the family accumulates at
$\varphi$, the old cut was far short of where the numbers stop being
distinguishable, and the extra rows cost 8 KB. All of that was right. But
every one of the 29 new rows in each table answers no search by number.
`/api/lookup` returns them at no precision -- 10, 12, 16, 20 or 30 significant
digits all give nothing -- while all 50 older rows are returned at every
precision. The boundary sits exactly at the range the tables had before.

The cause is the rule the skill already states: values are held out of search
by number until a board member reviews them. What nobody had put together is
that this applies to *rows added to a table that is already public*, not only
to a new table waiting to be published. The table is public, the page shows the
new rows, `audit` is clean, `verify` passes -- and the range that was extended
to serve readers holding these numbers serves none of them yet.

What the skill says now: section 9, "Values are held out of search by number
until a board member reviews them", in a list about publishing a *new* table,
under a five-step sequence that ends "it is public, and its values answer
search by number". Section 3, where the range is chosen, says nothing about it.
A contributor who reads section 3 to decide how far to go has no reason to
visit section 9 again, because their table was published months ago.

What it should say: where the range is chosen. Something to the effect that
extending a table that is already public adds rows in the unreviewed state, so
the new range answers no search by number until somebody reviews it; that the
way to check is to paste a new value into `/api/lookup` rather than to look at
the page, which shows the row either way; and that a range extension is
therefore worth batching rather than dribbling, since each one costs a review.

Evidence: 2026-09-20, T286 and T300. `GET /api/lookup` with a key:
`1.61803132287` (rank 50) returns its row, `1.61803234116` (rank 51) returns
nothing, and so on through rank 79; T300's `x^28 - x^27 - x^26 + x^2 - 1`
(rank 50) is found and `x^33 - x^32 - x^31 + x^2 - 1` (rank 60) is not. The
filter is `reviewed = True` in `_identifiable`, `numberdb_app/search.py`.

## For a family that accumulates, the range argument is a resolution, not a distance

What happened: T286 holds the Pisot numbers in $(1,\varphi)$, an infinite set
whose only accumulation point is $\varphi$, so `complete: no` is permanent and
the whole of the range decision is where to stop. Its completeness note gives
two measurements -- the last entry is $1.9\cdot10^{-9}$ below $\varphi$, and by
$n=72$ the entries are within $10^{-15}$ -- and no criterion joining them. Both
numbers are correct. Neither tells a reader whether their own number, say
$10^{-12}$ below $\varphi$, is missing from the table or absent from the
mathematics.

The criterion that makes both numbers mean something is the reader's
arithmetic. A value $2.2\cdot10^{-16}$ below $\varphi$ is $\varphi$ in double
precision: nobody arrives holding it, because their computation handed them
$\varphi$. That is a sharp, checkable place for such a family to stop, it is
the same place for every family accumulating near 1.6, and it is a fact about
who is holding the number rather than about how many entries fit. For T286 it
falls at rank 145, and the table's 79 is a defensible margin inside it. The
note does not say that, and so cannot be checked against.

The counterpart cost is measurable on the same corpus. A query of four or five
decimal places of $\varphi$ returns a page that is two-thirds entries of this
one table -- 18 of 27 results for `1.6180` -- because every entry near the
accumulation point falls inside every short query window around it, and inside
no other. That is the skill's "every hit in the corpus is worth a little less
for it", made concrete: for an accumulating family the marginal entry degrades
exactly the search for the limit point, which is usually the most-looked-for
number in the neighbourhood.

What the skill says now: section 2 says to hold the numbers that turn up, and
that the completeness note is where the argument goes. Section 3 gives size
guidance. Neither names the accumulating case, where size never binds at all --
T286 at the reader's ceiling would be 14% of the block limit and 12% of the
entry limit.

What it should say: when a family accumulates at a known point, neither the
entry count nor the byte size decides the range; the range stops where the
entries stop being separable from the limit by the arithmetic a reader will
arrive with, and the completeness note should say so in those terms. And name
the counterpart: entries crowding an accumulation point answer short queries
for the limit point itself, so the case for the last hundred is weaker than
the case for the first hundred, not merely cheaper.

Evidence: 2026-09-20, T286. Measured in Sage over all 179 roots of $P_n$, $Q_n$
in $(1,\varphi)$ for $n\leq90$: $\varphi-\theta_r$ falls by
$\log_{10}\varphi/2 = 0.1045$ per rank, crossing $2.2\cdot10^{-16}$ at rank
145 and $10^{-100}$ at about rank 950. `GET /api/lookup?text=1.6180` returns
27 results of which 18 are T286 and one is the golden ratio;
`text=1.618034` returns 6 of which none is T286.
