## Surveying the whole corpus with `numberdb.table` exhausts the rate limit, and `already_here` then cannot answer

What happened: to find an area the corpus does not cover, this run walked `T1`
to `T389` with `numberdb.table`, one request per table, 389 requests. It
succeeded. The next `numberdb.search_text` call, twenty minutes later, returned
`RateLimitError: too many requests; retry in 2505s` — forty-two minutes of
nothing, for every name screened after the walk.

That is survivable here only because the screening module refuses to hide it:
`already_here` returns `['(could not ask the corpus: RateLimitError: ...)']`
rather than `[]`, and `[]` would have read exactly like "nothing similar is in
the corpus", which is the opposite of what was known. A bare
`numberdb.search_text` in somebody's own script has no such guard and will
return an empty result object, so a duplicate-check written the obvious way
reports "nothing here" precisely when it has been silenced.

What the skill says now: "there is no call that lists the corpus, so to see
everything you walk the T-numbers", and `numberdb.table(...)` "answers with the
whole document ... Printing it is megabytes." It says nothing about what
walking the T-numbers costs.

What it should say: walking the corpus is a few hundred API requests and will
trip the rate limit; do the searches you need for a duplicate check *first* and
the survey afterwards, and treat a search that raises as unknown rather than as
empty. If a run needs both, cache the survey to a file and reuse it.

Evidence: 2026-09-21 ideas run. The walk of `T1`..`T389` completed in about six
minutes and found 388 live tables. `already_here('Epstein zeta function')`
before it returned real matches; the same call afterwards returned
`(could not ask the corpus: RateLimitError: too many requests; retry in 2505s)`,
as did every later name in the same run.

## Sage's `QuadraticForm` halves the form, so a lattice sum comes out at twice the argument

What happened: the Epstein zeta function
$Z_L(s)=\sum_{0\neq x\in L}(x\cdot x)^{-s}$ was to be summed from the
representation numbers of a lattice. Building the form from the Gram matrix and
reading the list as "number of vectors of norm $m$" is the obvious thing and it
is wrong: `QuadraticForm(matrix(G)).representation_number_list(M)` indexes by
$m=\tfrac12x^{\top}Gx$, so for an even lattice the entry at $m$ counts the
vectors of **norm $2m$**.

For $E_8$ with the standard Gram matrix the list begins
`1, 240, 2160, 6720, 17520, 30240, 60480, 82560, 140400`, and $E_8$ has 240
vectors of norm 2, 2160 of norm 4, 6720 of norm 6. Reading the index as the
norm gives $\sum r(m)m^{-s}$ instead of $\sum r(2m)(2m)^{-s}$, which is
$2^{s}Z_L(s)$: a number of the right size, converging at the right rate, with
no symptom at all. On the same lattice it also breaks the check it was supposed
to pass, since $r_{E_8}(2m)=240\sigma_3(m)$ holds for the norm indexing and not
for the other one.

What the skill says now: "Which normalisation, branch, and indexing" must be
decided before computing, with `elliptic_k`'s parameter $m$ against the modulus
$k$ as the example. It does not name this one, and lattice families are a whole
corner of the corpus (T147 to T151).

What it should say: when a quadratic form or a lattice supplies the index of a
sum, print the first half-dozen representation numbers and identify them against
the known kissing number before summing anything. `QuadraticForm(G)` is the form
$\tfrac12x^{\top}Gx$, so for an even lattice its index is half the norm.

Evidence: 2026-09-21, `agents/sage.sh`.
`QuadraticForm(matrix(ZZ,8,[...E_8 Gram...])).representation_number_list(9)` gave
`[1, 240, 2160, 6720, 17520, 30240, 60480, 82560, 140400]`, and $240\sigma_3(m)$
for $m=1..8$ is `240, 2160, 6720, 17520, 30240, 60480, 82560, 140400`; the
$A_3$ Gram gave `[1, 12, 6, 24, 12, 24, 8, ...]`, whose 12 is the fcc kissing
number at norm 2.

## A closed form recalled from memory is not a check, and a crude quadrature with Richardson is

What happened: three Watson integrals were to be proposed with $\Gamma$-product
closed forms. Two of the three were confirmed against numbers the author
believed to be the published values; for the face-centred cubic lattice the
closed form gave `1.3446611832` while the remembered "literature value" was
`1.3446610732`, differing in the seventh digit. There was no way to tell from
inside which one was wrong.

What settled it was the cheapest possible independent computation. Integrating
one of the three Brillouin-zone directions in closed form
($\int_0^\pi\mathrm{d}k/(A-B\cos k)=\pi/\sqrt{A^2-B^2}$) leaves a two
dimensional integral with an integrable $1/r$ corner singularity; a plain
midpoint rule in `float64` on that, at $N=2000,4000,8000$, has error $O(1/N)$,
and two steps of Richardson extrapolation in $1/N$ gave ten correct digits for
all three lattices in seconds: `1.5163860591`, `1.3932039297`, `1.3446611832`.
The remembered value was simply wrong.

Ten digits is nowhere near a publishable precision and that is the point: it is
far more than enough to separate a right closed form from a misremembered or
mistranscribed one, and the skill's own rule is that a measurement needs a
control that returns a known answer. Here one member of the family, the simple
cubic, had that control in the corpus already: $1-1/W$ against T19's stored
$p(3)$ agreed on all 23 digits compared.

What the skill says now: "Check new values against something independent" and
"a measurement needs a control that returns a known answer". Both are right and
neither says how cheap the independent thing is allowed to be.

What it should say: a low-order quadrature extrapolated in the step size is a
legitimate check on a closed form, and ten digits from it settles a
transcription. Never let a value you remember stand as the independent fact; if
the corpus holds one member of the family, anchor on that member and quadrature
the rest.

Evidence: 2026-09-21, `agents/sage.sh`. Closed forms in `arb` at 300 bits:
$W_{\mathrm{sc}}=$`1.5163860591519780`, $W_{\mathrm{bcc}}=\Gamma(1/4)^4/(4\pi^3)=$`1.3932039296856768`,
$W_{\mathrm{fcc}}=9\Gamma(1/3)^6/(2^{14/3}\pi^4)=$`1.3446611831651442`.
Midpoint-plus-Richardson: `1.5163860591`, `1.3932039297`, `1.3446611832`.
$1-1/W_{\mathrm{sc}}=$`0.34053732955099914282627` against T19's
`0.34053732955099914282627318443...`.

## Benson's $\operatorname{sech}^2$ sum gives the rock-salt Madelung constant to 28 digits in ball arithmetic

What happened: the Madelung constant of the rock-salt structure is a
conditionally convergent Coulomb sum, so the obvious computation is Ewald
summation, which is fiddly and hard to bound. Benson's identity
$$M = 12\pi\sum_{m,n\geq1,\ \text{odd}}\operatorname{sech}^2\!\Big(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\sqrt{m^2+n^2}\Big)$$
is a double sum of positive terms decaying like $e^{-\pi\sqrt{m^2+n^2}}$, so it
is a one-line ball-arithmetic computation with an elementary tail bound. Summed
over odd $m,n<60$ in `arb` at 400 bits it returned
`1.747564594633182190636212035544397`, agreeing with the published value on all
28 digits published.

The point is general and is about choosing the representation before choosing
the precision: a lattice sum's defining form and its computable form are usually
different, and a conditionally convergent sum has a geometrically convergent
identity somewhere in the literature. Truncating the defining sum with a tail
bound was never going to reach 100 digits, because the tail of
$\sum_m r(m)m^{-s}$ past $N$ is of order $N^{n/2-s}$ and is not a tail at all
near $s=n/2$.

What the skill says now: "Prefer balls for anything transcendental", and
`proven` needs "interval or ball arithmetic throughout". It does not say that
the choice of series is what decides whether `proven` is reachable.

What it should say: before choosing a working precision, choose a
representation that converges geometrically, and state in `rigour details`
which one and what bounds its tail. For a divergent or conditionally convergent
defining sum, the accelerated identity is the definition you can actually
compute, and the defining sum is the ten-digit control.

Evidence: 2026-09-21, `agents/sage.sh`, `RealBallField(400)`, the sum above over
odd $m,n<60$; and an independent Ewald summation in `mpmath` at 25 digits which
reproduced it to 16 digits and then gave `1.762674773070988` for caesium
chloride and `1.638055053388789` for zincblende.
