## A boundary-value problem with a scaling invariance does not need shooting, and the invariance is a free independent check

What happened: the previous run's lesson (`20260922T080710Z-ideas.md`) records
that a constant defined by shooting along a separatrix loses digits silently,
and names the Blasius constant as one of the class. For the Blasius constant
specifically there is a way out that costs nothing, and it generalises to any
autonomous boundary-value problem with a one-parameter scaling symmetry.

The Blasius problem is $2f'''+ff''=0$, $f(0)=f'(0)=0$, $f'(\infty)=1$. The
equation is invariant under $f(\eta)\mapsto a f(a\eta)$, which moves $f''(0)$
and $f'(\infty)$ together. So integrate the *initial* value problem from
$F(0)=F'(0)=0$, $F''(0)=1$ instead, read $L=F'(\infty)$ off the end, and
recover the boundary-value solution by rescaling with $a=L^{-1/2}$:

    f''(0)   = L^{-3/2}
    delta_1  = L^{-1/2} * lim (L*xi - F(xi))

There is no free parameter, no root-find and no separatrix: the trajectory
being integrated is an ordinary one. Measured under `agents/sage.sh`, at 30
working digits and $\eta_{\max}=30$:

    f''(0)  = 0.3320573362151962989371801      3.9 s, one integration
    delta_1 = 1.720787657520502819605438

against about 20 s for the same quantity by shooting at 30 working digits and
$\eta_{\max}=18$, which returned 22 digits. The two agree to every digit the
shot value carries, and they share no code path: one solves an initial value
problem, the other runs a secant iteration on a two-point problem. That is an
independent check of exactly the kind the skill asks for, obtained by looking
for a symmetry rather than for a second library.

The transformation is Töpfer's (1912) and the same trick works whenever the
equation is invariant and the missing condition is at infinity; it does not
work for the Falkner–Skan family with $\beta\neq 0$, because the $\beta(1-f'^2)$
term breaks the invariance. So it is worth asking of every such family, and it
will often answer for one member — which is then the row of the table that is
known best, and that is worth a sentence in `rigour details`.

What the skill says now: "Check new values against something independent. Not
the code that produced them", with a family's identities as the example; and,
in the proposed lesson above, that shooting loses digits.

What it should say: before shooting a boundary-value problem, look for a
scaling invariance that turns it into an initial value problem. Where one
exists it is faster, it has no separatrix to amplify errors, and running both
gives an independent check of the shooting for the rest of the family.

Evidence: 2026-09-22 ideas run, `/tmp/w/fs.py` and `/tmp/w/fs2.py`. Shot value
at $\beta=0$: `0.3320573362151962989372`; scaled value:
`0.3320573362151962989371801`.

## A source names a family; it does not certify the digits, and one article can state two different values

What happened: the Wikipedia article *Blasius boundary layer* was cited as the
source for a proposal and passed `source_names_it`. It states

    f''(0) = 0.332043934904293          (section "Blasius equation")
    alpha  = 0.332057336215196          (two paragraphs later)

for the same constant. They differ in the fifth significant figure and only
the second is right; the first is what shooting to a short $\eta_{\max}$
returns. The same run found the article *Von Kármán swirling flow* giving the
axial inflow as $0.886$ where the accurate value is $0.884474$.

Neither is a reason not to cite those pages: they are the pages that establish
what the family is called, which is what the source check is for. The error is
to carry a number *out* of them.

What the skill says now: "Cite a source for every proposal and run
`source_names_it` on it ... it is the only check that a family is real and is
called what you say." True, and easy to read as though a passing source also
underwrites the values.

What it should say: a source check answers "is this family called that" and
nothing else. Any digit quoted in a proposal or written into a table needs its
own computation or a second source, and where two sources disagree the
proposal should say so — a family whose published values contradict each other
is a better candidate for a table, not a worse one, because a table with a
rigour level and an attached generator is what settles it.

Evidence: 2026-09-22 ideas run. The two values above are both in the article as
fetched on 2026-09-22; the run's own computation gives
`0.3320573362151962989371801`.

## Two normalisations whose ratio depends on the index are not covered by storing one of them

What happened: the Falkner–Skan wall shear is tabulated in two scalings of the
wall-normal coordinate, both standard. Hartree's and Schlichting's tables give
$0.4696$ for the flat plate; White's and most solvers give $0.33206$. The two
are related by

    f''_hartree(0) = sqrt(2/(m+1)) * f''_wedge(0)

where $m$ is the index of the family. At $m=0$ the factor is $\sqrt2$; at
$m=1$ it is $1$; at $m=3$ it is $1/\sqrt2$.

The skill's findability rule is that a reader will try a half, a two, a pi and
then stop, so a family whose values are a small guessable multiple of a stored
one is arguably covered. That rule is about a *constant* factor. A factor that
is a function of the index fails it for a different reason: there is no single
number to try, and a reader holding $0.4696$ cannot get to a table of
$0.33206$ by any fixed guess, even knowing both conventions exist.

What the skill says now: the "one number in two conventions" allowance, tested
by invertibility, with $E_1$ and $\operatorname{Ei}$ and "a constant and its
logarithm" as the examples — all constant conversions.

What it should say: when the conversion between two published normalisations
depends on the parameter, that is the strongest case for the allowance rather
than a marginal one, and the reason to state in `Comments` is findability
rather than tidiness. Give the conversion in `Formulas` as a formula in the
parameter, not as a number.

Evidence: 2026-09-22 ideas run, `agents/table-ideas/BATCH-2026-09-22T0903.md`
§1. Computed at $m=0$: wedge $0.3320573362151962989$, Hartree
$0.3320573362151962989\times\sqrt2 = 0.4695999883$; at $m=1$ the two coincide
at $1.23258765682028102$.

## A famous constant may be a fitted correlation coefficient rather than the value of the quantity it stands for

What happened: the laminar flat-plate heat-transfer law is printed everywhere
as $\mathrm{Nu}_x = 0.332\,\mathrm{Re}_x^{1/2}\mathrm{Pr}^{1/3}$, and the
proposal was about to say that $0.332$ is the value of
$-\theta'(0)/\mathrm{Pr}^{1/3}$. Computing it says otherwise:

    Pr      -theta'(0)          -theta'(0) / Pr^(1/3)
    0.7     0.29268022262401    0.32963048
    1       0.3320573362152     0.33205734
    10      0.72814130546758    0.33797326
    100     1.5718317531344     0.33864089

The ratio is not constant. It equals $0.332$ only at $\mathrm{Pr} = 1$, where
it also equals the wall shear $f''(0)$ exactly by Reynolds's analogy, and the
textbook has taken that one value as the coefficient of a $\mathrm{Pr}^{1/3}$
law that is really only asymptotic (the limit is $0.339$). So $0.332$,
$0.3296$ and $0.339$ are three different numbers printed as one, and somebody
who computes $0.3296$ where the book says $0.332$ cannot tell whether they are
wrong.

The general shape: in applied subjects a "known constant" is often the
coefficient of a fitted or asymptotic law, and the exact value of the
quantity varies over the range the law is used on. Storing the correlation
coefficient as though it were the quantity puts a number in the database that
is right nowhere.

What the skill says now: "Decide exactness from the definition, never from the
digits", which is about integers and rationals. Nothing about a quoted
constant that is exact for no argument at all.

What it should say: before storing a constant that a literature quotes without
a derivation, compute the quantity it is supposed to be and check that the two
agree at more than one point. Where they do not, the table holds the computed
quantity, indexed, and the `Comments` say which widely-quoted number is the
correlation and where it comes from. That disagreement is a reason to build
the table, not a reason to doubt the computation.

Evidence: 2026-09-22 ideas run, `/tmp/w/fs4.py`, RK4 at 25 digits. The
$\mathrm{Pr} = 1$ row reproduces $f''(0) = 0.3320573362152$ to all thirteen
digits printed, so the quadrature is right where the identity can test it.
