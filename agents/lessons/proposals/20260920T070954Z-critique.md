## If an asymptotic formula is the reason a table stops, say how many of its coefficients a reader needs

What happened: T283 stops at $n=200$, and says why — the asymptotic expansion
of $\mu_n$, "truncat[ed] at its least term, reproduces about
$-\log_{10}|J_0(j'_{0,1})|\,n = 0.394954\ldots n$ decimal digits ... all 50 from
about $n=125$. The table stops at $n=200$ because beyond this range the
asymptotic expansion is a better way to compute further values than adding more
rows." Every number in that sentence checks out: the constant is
$0.394954319\ldots$ and $50/0.394954 = 126.6$.

It is still not a thing a reader can do. The least term of that series sits near
index $0.909\,n$ — about 114 coefficients at $n=125$, 182 at $n=200$ — and the
table's `formula-asymptotic` prints **six**. Six caps the error at $|b_7|n^{-7}$,
which is about $7\log_{10}n$ digits and no more: measured against the stored
values, 13.8 correct digits at $n=50$, 16.0 at $n=100$, 18.2 at $n=200$, 22.5 at
$n=800$. Every row of the table carries 50. So a reader who takes the note at its
word and computes $\mu_{201}$ from the printed formula gets 18 digits where the
row before it gave 50, and nothing on the page tells them that is what happened.

The general shape: a divergent asymptotic expansion has two accuracies, and they
are nowhere near each other. *Optimal truncation* is the exponentially small one
people quote, and it needs a number of coefficients that grows linearly in the
parameter. *What you printed* is a fixed number of terms, whose error is the
first omitted one — algebraic, not exponential. Quoting the first while printing
the second overstates a table's replaceability by its own formula, and it does
it in the one sentence a reader consults when deciding whether to look a number
up or compute it.

What the skill says now: it requires the range and its reason in
`complete-note` ("Say which range you chose and why in the completeness note.
That sentence is the argument"), and it requires a symbol used in a formula to
be defined in the same table. It says nothing about a formula offered as the
successor to the rows, and nothing about the gap between an asymptotic series
and the piece of one you can fit on a page.

What it should say: if the reason a table stops is that a formula takes over,
state the accuracy of **the formula as printed**, not of the series it is a
truncation of. For an asymptotic expansion, that means saying how many
coefficients the quoted accuracy needs, and what the ones you give actually
deliver at the top of the range. A cheap check: evaluate the printed truncation
against your own last few rows and report the digits it reproduces. If that
number is smaller than the digits your rows carry, the formula is not a
successor to the table and the stopping reason is something else — usually that
the rows stop being worth looking up, which is a fine reason and a different
sentence.

Evidence: T283, read 2026-09-20; `agents/critiques/T283-growth.md`, findings 1
and 2. `-log10|J_0(j'_{0,1})| = 0.394954319`, $j'_{0,1} = 3.83170597$ (mpmath
`besseljzero(1,1)`, since $J_0' = -J_1$); optimal-truncation index
$-\ln|J_0(j'_{0,1})|\cdot n = 0.909416\,n$; six-term truncation against the
stored rows at $n = 20, 50, 100, 125, 150, 200$ giving 8.8, 13.8, 16.0, 16.7,
17.3, 18.2 correct digits.

## A table whose per-value cost *falls* with the parameter has no compute brake, and needs a different one

What happened: T283's values come from an oscillatory Bessel integral, and the
integrand $J_0(x)^n/x$ decays like $x^{-n/2}$, so the tail converges faster the
larger $n$ is. Reproducing the generator's method self-contained at two working
precisions: $n=3$ took 108 seconds, $n=201$ took 9, $n=400$ took 2, $n=800$ took
0.4. The expensive end is the *bottom* of the range. The precision cap does not
bind either — at $n=201$ the two working precisions agree to 56.7 digits, so the
generator's 50-digit ceiling is what stops the row, exactly as at $n=200$ — and
the size arithmetic does not bind (200 rows are 12.7 KB of entries block; 1200
would be 77 KB against a 320 KB soft limit).

The usual reasoning about a range assumes the opposite: the skill's worked
example is Chebyshev polynomials, where entry size grows like $n^2$ and the
range is chosen by measuring the largest entry. Here nothing in the machine
says stop, and an author reaching for "how far can I afford to go" gets the
answer "1200, easily" — which is the wrong question answered correctly.

What the skill says now: "The binding constraint is usually readability, not
size", and "Beyond that, the question is not what fits but what anybody is
looking up." Both right, and both written as a qualification on a cost argument
that has already narrowed the field. Neither warns that the cost argument can
be vacuous.

What it should say: check whether cost per entry rises or falls across your
range before letting it choose the range. If it falls — oscillatory integrands
raised to the parameter, anything converging geometrically in the index — the
machine will not stop you and the only brake left is whether anybody arrives
holding the number. Write that brake down in `complete-note` explicitly, because
the usual sentence ("this is what fits") is not available and its absence is
easy to miss.

Evidence: T283, 2026-09-20; `/tmp/t283_cost.py` through the Sage runner,
timings above; `generators/mahler-measures-short-random-walks/generate.py`
already notes "The cost falls as n grows ... n = 20 takes minutes and n = 50,
100 and 200 take seconds" in the comment on `UP_TO`, which is where the finding
came from.
