# Lessons proposed by the T283 growth critique, 2026-09-20

Nothing here is in force. Format as in `agents/lessons/PROPOSALS.md`.

## The cost of a value does not always grow with the index, so measure the top of the range and past it

What happened: T283 stores $\mu_n = m(1+x_1+\cdots+x_{n-1})$ from the integral
$\mu_n=\log2-\gamma-\int_0^1(J_0(x)^n-1)/x\,dx-\int_1^\infty J_0(x)^n/x\,dx$,
and its `complete-note` gives as a reason for stopping at $n=200$ that "the
Bessel integral is cheap and the rows remain short there" — the ordinary
assumption that a bigger index costs more. It is backwards here. $J_0(x)^n$
decays like $x^{-n/2}$, so the oscillating tail needs *fewer* intervals between
zeros of $J_0$ as $n$ grows. Measured at the table's own two working
precisions, both per row, on one box:

    n = 200    12.3 s      n = 500     2.9 s
    n = 300    12.6 s      n = 1000    0.4 s

A factor of thirty cheaper at five times the index, with no loss of precision:
all four agreed to about 71 digits, and $n=200$ reproduced the stored value to
all 50 of its digits. A range bound chosen because "it gets expensive" would
have been chosen for a reason that is not true, and a contributor extrapolating
from $n=7$ (the slowest row in the table) would conclude the opposite of the
truth about $n=1000$.

Families where this happens are not exotic: anything whose integrand or summand
is raised to the $n$th power and is bounded by 1 away from a small set gets
cheaper with $n$ — powers of an oscillating special function, tails of a
probability that concentrates, series whose terms are $r^n$ for $r<1$.

What the skill says now: §3 says "measure the largest entry before choosing the
range", which is about the *size* of a stored value, and everything around it
assumes cost rises — "Values that grow", the Chebyshev $O(n^3)$ table. Nothing
says the cost of *computing* a value can fall.

What it should say: in §3, beside "measure the largest entry before choosing
the range", something like — and measure the *time* at the top of the range and
at twice it, rather than extrapolating from the first entries. For some
families the cost falls with the index, so "it gets expensive" may be a reason
that is not true, and a bound resting on it will be wrong in both directions:
too low where computing is cheap, and stated in the table as a fact a reader
can check and find false.

Evidence: T283 as of 2026-09-20; `/tmp/t283_growth.py` on this box, the four
timings above; `generators/mahler-measures-short-random-walks/generate.py`,
whose docstring already records the same thing ("measured at working precision
40 and 48, n = 20 takes minutes and n = 50, 100 and 200 take seconds") while
the table it fills says the opposite.

## Where a family is infinite, stop the range where the table's own closed form overtakes it

What happened: T283 holds 200 of infinitely many $\mu_n$, and nothing bounds
it: the method is cheaper at $n=1000$ than at $n=200$, 1000 entries is exactly
the recommended count, and the entries block would be about 58 KB against a
320 KB limit. So the bound had to be argued rather than found, and the argument
that works is one the table already contains without using it. Its `Formulas`
carry the asymptotic expansion with six coefficients. Checked in 70-digit
decimal arithmetic against the stored values, those six give:

    n = 100    15 correct digits        n = 500     21
    n = 200    18                       n = 1000    23

Every row carries 50 digits. So a reader arriving with fewer than ~18 digits —
which is everyone whose number came from a simulation or a plot — is already
answered by a formula printed on the page, and a reader with more than that
computed it from the integral and knows what it is. The band a new row would
serve is empty, and it stays empty however far the range runs, because the
digits the formula gives *grow* with $n$ while the digits a row holds stay at
50. That is a stopping rule with a number in it, and it is checkable by anyone
in twenty lines.

The rule generalises to any family whose values approach a closed form: the
range should stop around where the closed form starts answering the reader to
the precision they plausibly hold. It also gives the `complete-note` a sentence
that is an argument instead of a size.

What the skill says now: §2 has the principle — "the question a table has to
answer about its range is not how many entries that makes, nor how simply they
can be described, but whether anybody will arrive holding one of them", and
"$\operatorname{erf}(17/18)$ does not, and a bound on the denominator that
admits it is counting rather than choosing". That is about which *arguments* to
pick from a grid. It says nothing about where to stop a range running to
infinity, and the worked examples — polynomial degree, denominator bounds — all
stop because of cost or size, which is the case where the answer is easy.

What it should say: in §3, after "include what is common, and stop" — where the
family is infinite and the values converge to a closed form the table states,
the range can stop where that form answers a reader to the precision the rows
carry. Compute how many digits the stated form gives at the top of the proposed
range; if it is already more than a reader is likely to hold, further rows
serve nobody, and the count only rises as the range does. Put that number in
`complete-note`: "it stops at $n=200$, where the expansion in (4) is already
good to 18 digits" is an argument a reader can check, and "it stops at $n=200$
because that is where the computation gets slow" is one that may not even be
true.

Evidence: T283 formula `formula-asymptotic` ($b_1=-1/8$ … $b_6=-153841/12192768$)
against `Numbers` at $n=100,200,300,500,1000$, Python `decimal` at 70 places;
the table's own comment (11) makes the $n=100$ and $n=200$ claim and my count
confirms it (15 and 18 against its "about 16" and "18"). Written up in
`agents/critiques/T283-growth.md` §3.
