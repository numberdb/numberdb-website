## Some ranges are not chosen: say so, and say what closes them

What happened: T280 holds 23 solutions of $a_1^k+\cdots+a_{k-1}^k=b^k$ in 96
entries — 8% of the entry limit and 0.6% of the block limit — and the growth
review asked whether it had been stopped early. It had not. Its four axes
close for four different reasons: $k=3$ is Fermat's last theorem at $n=3$ and
is empty forever; $k=4$ holds the complete published list (OEIS A003828,
complete to $b\leq1986560000$); $k=5$ holds all four solutions anybody knows;
$k\geq6$ is an open problem with nothing found. Not one of those is a bound
somebody picked. But the page could not say so, because every sentence the
skill offers for a range is about a bound the author chose — "every $a/b$ with
$b\leq18$", "$D\leq1000$" — and a reader landing on 23 entries in a corpus
whose house range is a thousand reasonably wonders what was left out.

What the skill says now: "Say which range you chose and why in the
completeness note. That sentence is the argument." And: `complete: no`, with
`complete-note` saying which part is finished. Both assume the author drew a
line. There is no guidance for the table where the line is drawn by the state
of knowledge, and no encouragement to say *why the next row does not exist*,
which is the fact a reader of a small table actually needs.

What it should say: some families have no range to choose — every object that
exists is already in the table. A named constant with one entry, the known
solutions of a Diophantine equation, the sporadic groups. For those, the
completeness note should name what closes the range rather than where it was
cut: "it holds every solution known; $k=3$ has none by Fermat's last theorem
at $n=3$, and none is known for $k\geq6$". A table that is small because the
subject is small is finished, and saying which theorem or which search makes
it finished is worth more than any bound. It also tells the next person what
would have to happen for the table to grow, which is the question they are
actually asking when they open it.

Evidence: T280 (`agents/critiques/T280-growth.md`), the OEIS A003828 b-file
(19 terms), `http://euler.free.fr/database.txt` (one known $(5,2,3)$ solution,
already stored), arXiv 2603.05549.

## A parametric family above an exhaustive search bound is not a range extension

What happened: T280's fourth-power rows are complete to $b\leq1986560000$.
Elkies proved there are infinitely many, and the slice of his construction
that Wikipedia quotes is an elliptic curve you can put in Sage in twenty
lines. Doing that (rank 3 over $\mathbb{Q}$, torsion $\mathbb{Z}/2$; map 1372
points of the Mordell–Weil group back through the parametrisation) produces
nine further primitive solutions below $10^{30}$, the smallest being
$664793200^4+2448718655^4+3134081336^4=3393603777^4$ — only $1.71\times$ the
search bound. Adding them costs 36 entries and 4 KB. It is cheap, it is exact,
and it is checkable, so it looks like free growth.

It is not, and the reason is the skill's own test. Nobody arrives holding
$3393603777$: to have it you must have run the parametrisation, and then you
already know what it is. The stored solutions below the bound reach readers
because a search finds them. And the combined range — *complete* below
$2\times10^9$, then nine scattered members of one thin slice (only 3 of the 19
stored solutions lie on that curve at all), with everything between unsearched
— cannot be said in one clause, which is the symptom the skill already names
for a range that has gone wrong.

What the skill says now: "Hold the numbers that turn up", with the
$\operatorname{erf}(17/18)$ and Weil-polynomial examples; and a ragged range is
discussed only as a sign of two tables wearing one title.

What it should say: when a family is exhaustively known below a bound and
infinitely many members are *constructible* above it, the constructible ones
are usually not a range extension. The bound is where readers' numbers stop
coming from; beyond it the only person who can hold one of your values is
somebody who ran your own construction. Record the construction in `Comments`
and `Formulas` — a reader with a twelve-digit solution needs to be told that
infinitely many exist and that the table holds the searched range, or they
will read the table's silence as a denial — and leave the values out. A
generator that can produce a value is not an argument for storing it.

Evidence: T280; Elkies, Math. Comp. 51 (1988) 825–835; the quartic
$u^2=22030+28849v-56158v^2+36941v^3-31790v^4$ with $v=-31/467$; full
computation and table of the nine new solutions in
`agents/critiques/T280-growth.md` §3.
