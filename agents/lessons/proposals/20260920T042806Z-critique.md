## A source's printed decimals are truncated or rounded, and one author can do both

What happened: reading T284 ("Salem numbers less than 1.3") for whether it
could grow, I checked the 25 rows a later paper lists between 1.3 and 49/37
using the same test T284's generator applies to its own source —
`check_source_truncation()`, which asserts that the isolated root ball lies
inside `[printed, printed + 10^-k)`. **Eleven of the 25 failed.** Nothing was
wrong with them: the paper rounds at the twelfth decimal, and the test allows
only truncation. `1.3064735375326…` is printed as `1.306473537533`.

The trap is that both conventions are in play in the same body of work, and
each is documented only in its own footer. Mossinghoff's `SalemList.html`
ends "Note: Salem numbers are truncated, not rounded". The format note for
his other tables on the same site says the Mahler measure is "rounded (not
truncated) at the last digit shown". The paper says it follows Mossinghoff's
presentation, and on this point it does not follow the list it names.

Guessing costs a run rather than a wrong value — the check is there to fail —
but eleven simultaneous failures on verified-correct rows read like the
rebuilt polynomials are wrong, which is where the time goes.

What the skill says now: it insists on checking new values against something
independent, and on recomputing rather than transcribing printed digits. It
says nothing about which way a printed digit was cut.

What it should say: when a source's decimals are used as a check, decide
which convention it uses before the check fails, and write the answer into
the generator as a named choice rather than an assumption. Truncation and
rounding differ by one unit in the last place, so a truncation test rejects
about half of a correctly rebuilt table, and a rounding test accepts both — if
the source does not say, test both and record which one held for every row. A
source that says "truncated, not rounded" in its footer is telling you
something it expects to be read.

Evidence: 2026-09-20, T284 growth reading. arXiv:2409.11159 Table 1, 25 rows;
`/tmp/check_extension.py` under `agents/sage.sh` reported 14 `truncated` and
11 `ROUNDED`, 0 failures, after the check was widened. Mossinghoff's
`SalemList.html` footer and his `misc/tableformat.html` state the two
conventions.

## To check a Salem or Pisot root pattern, use the trace polynomial, not the algebraic closure

What happened: verifying that 25 candidate rows really were Salem numbers
meant testing, for each reciprocal $f$ of even degree $d$, that exactly one
conjugate lies outside the unit circle, one inside, and $d-2$ on it. The
obvious spelling is `for root, _ in f.roots(QQbar): compare abs(root) to 1`.
That is exact and it is also the wrong tool: comparing `abs(root)` to 1 in
`QQbar` forces an exactification through a square root, which is slow on
degree 44 and, in the Sage of this image, raises
`NameError: name 'RR_1_10' is not defined` out of `sage/rings/qqbar.py`
before it finishes.

The whole test is a real-root count in disguise. A reciprocal $f$ of even
degree $d$ factors as $f(x) = x^{d/2} g(x + 1/x)$ with $g \in \mathbb{Z}[y]$
of degree $d/2$; the unit circle becomes $[-2, 2]$, and a real root of $f$
above 1 becomes a real root of $g$ above 2. So "Salem" is exactly: $g$ has
$d/2$ real roots, one of them above 2 and the other $d/2 - 1$ inside
$(-2, 2)$, with neither endpoint a root. Build $g$ from the half-coefficients
with $p_0 = 2$, $p_1 = y$, $p_k = y\,p_{k-1} - p_{k-2}$ (so $p_k = x^k +
x^{-k}$), then count with `number_of_roots_in_interval`, which is Sturm
sequences over $\mathbb{Q}$ — exact, no algebraic numbers constructed, and
instant at degree 44. Pisot is the same test with a different count.

What the skill says now: nothing about root patterns. It says to isolate
values in interval arithmetic and to check whatever the definition asserts,
which is right and leaves the reader to reach for `QQbar`.

What it should say: when a property is "how many conjugates lie inside,
outside or on the unit circle", do not enumerate conjugates. Map the circle
to a real interval with $y = x + 1/x$ and count real roots of the trace
polynomial exactly. It is the difference between a test that is exact and
fast and one that is exact and may not return.

Evidence: 2026-09-20, T284 growth reading. `/tmp/check_extension.py` under
`agents/sage.sh`: the `QQbar` version died with `NameError: RR_1_10` inside
`qqbar.py`'s `sqrt`; the trace-polynomial version checked all 25 rows, degrees
12 to 44, in one run with no numerical decision anywhere.
