# Lessons proposed by the T282 growth critique, 2026-09-20

Nothing here is in force. Format follows `agents/lessons/PROPOSALS.md`.

## A completeness note must say who arrives holding the values, not who can compute them

What happened: T282 holds $m_k$ for $0\leq k\leq100$ out of an infinite family,
and its `complete-note` ends "for $k>100$, CITE{formula-large-k} gives $m_k$
from a few terms". That is true -- the series needs 34 terms at 100 digits for
$k=101$ -- and it is the wrong direction. NumberDB answers the reader who *has*
a number and wants to know what it is. That reader cannot apply the formula,
because applying it needs $k$, which is what they came to find out. Forward
cheapness is a property of nearly every real-valued table in the corpus and has
never been a reason to stop one.

The sentence also points the wrong way on its own terms. The series in that
table is *cheapest* where the note uses it to justify an absence: 35 terms at
$k=100$ against 502 at $k=5$, for the table's own precision. Read literally it
argues for deleting the rows the table has at least as strongly as for
declining the rows it does not.

What the skill says now: "Say which range you chose and why in the completeness
note. That sentence is the argument, and it is what a reader checking whether
their own number belongs here actually reads." It asks for an argument and does
not say what makes one valid, so "the missing values are easy to compute" reads
as an acceptable answer. It is the first one that comes to mind, and a second
table in the corpus (T283) uses the same shape.

What it should say: a completeness note argues from who arrives holding a
value, never from how cheaply the absent ones could be computed. Everything in
this database is cheap to compute forwards from its own definition; that is
what makes the backwards question worth answering. If the reason a range stops
where it does is that the values beyond it are not ones anybody meets, say
that, and say what they *do* meet -- the named cases, the arguments a
calculation actually hands back. If instead the reason is cost, say what was
measured.

Evidence: T282 `complete-note` as served 2026-09-20; term counts computed from
the geometric tail bound the table's own generator uses, 502 terms at $k=5$ and
35 at $k=100$ for 100 digits. Extending T282 from $k=101$ to the corpus entry
limit at $k=1199$ was measured at 8 seconds of values plus 38 seconds of
independent checks, so cost is not what stopped it and the note does not claim
it was.

## Checking a stored value against a ball needs the last place widened first

What happened: recomputing T282's $k=100$ entry in `RealBallField` and asking
`recomputed.overlaps(R(stored_string))` answered **False** on a table that is
correct. `R(stored_string)` parses the decimal as a *point*, while a stored
NumberDB real is an interval of one unit in its last place. The recomputed ball
has radius about $10^{-119}$; the stored decimal is a 99-place rounding, so it
misses that ball by up to $10^{-99}$, and the two do not overlap even though
each contains the true value. Widening the stored value first --
`R(stored).add_error(R(10)**-99).overlaps(recomputed)` -- answers True.

What the skill says now: the convention is stated -- "A real is stored as an
interval that contains it", "`3.14` is the interval [2.13, 3.15] -- the last
digit may be off by one" -- but only as a rule for *writing* values. Nothing
says what it means for reading one back, and the natural check is the wrong
one. The package's own `verify()` does this correctly, so a contributor who
uses it never meets the trap; a contributor checking one entry by hand meets it
immediately, and what it tells them is that the table is wrong.

What it should say: beside the table of real formats, a sentence on reading one
back. A stored decimal is an interval of one unit in its last place, so
comparing it with a freshly computed ball means widening it by that unit first.
A bare `overlaps` between a narrow ball and a stored decimal parsed as a point
reports a disagreement on every correct entry in the corpus. Use
`generator.verify()`, which does this; where you must do it by hand, add the
error of the last place to the stored value and not to the recomputation.

Evidence: 2026-09-20, T282 $k=100$. `R(stored).overlaps(recomputed)` False;
`R(stored).add_error(10**-99).overlaps(recomputed)` True, `R` being
`RealBallField(numberdb.bits(100, losing=96))` and the stored value carrying 99
decimal places.
