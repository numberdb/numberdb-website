## The article about the object often does not name the constant; the OEIS entry for its sequence does

What happened: three of eleven citations were rejected by `source_names_it`,
and none of the three was a case of the family not existing.

    Otter's tree enumeration constants
      https://mathworld.wolfram.com/OttersTreeEnumerationConstants.html
      -> source answered 404

    Klarner's constant
      https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyomino
      -> the source does not mention klarner's

    Growth constant of planar graphs
      https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0501269
      -> the source does not mention growth

All three pass once the citation or the name is changed:

    Otter's tree enumeration constants   https://oeis.org/A051491          OK
    Klarner's constant                   https://oeis.org/A001168          OK
    Asymptotic enumeration of planar graphs
                                  https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0501269       OK

The pattern is worth naming. A constant of this kind is attached to a
*counting sequence*, and the sequence has an OEIS entry whose name and comments
carry the constant's human name, its digits and its attribution -- A001168 says
"Klarner's constant" where Wikipedia's polyomino article, which is about the
objects, never mentions Klarner at all. The encyclopedia article is about the
thing being counted; the OEIS entry is about the counting, which is where the
constant lives.

The third rejection is a different animal and just as useful: the family is
real and the paper is the right one, but its own words are "asymptotic
enumeration", not "growth constant". The screen was telling me the *name* was
wrong for that page, not that the family was invented. The name the source
uses is also the name that belongs in the table's `Keywords`, so the screen
was doing a second job.

What the skill says now: "Cite a source for every proposal and run
`source_names_it` on it. Wikipedia, MathWorld, DLMF, OEIS or a paper." OEIS is
in the list, but as one option among five.

What it should say: for a *constant* -- as opposed to a function or a family of
polynomials -- try the OEIS entry for the sequence it is the growth rate,
density or amplitude of, before the encyclopedia article about the objects.
And when `source_names_it` complains that a real source does not mention a
word of the name, read it as a claim about the name rather than about the
family: the page's own wording is usually the one the literature uses, and it
is the one to put in the title or `Keywords`.

Evidence: the eleven checks above, run 2026-09-23 through
`agents/table-ideas/screen.py`.

## Run the ratio test before proposing a constant defined as a limit; the digits it reaches are the digits a builder can check

What happened: this batch proposes six tables of constants
$\gamma=\lim_n c_n^{1/n}$. Two of them look identical on paper and are not
remotely alike to check.

Rooted unlabelled trees, from the recurrence
$(n-1)t_n=\sum_{k<n}\sigma(k)t_{n-k}$ with $\sigma(k)=\sum_{d\mid k}d\,t_d$:

    t_50/t_49      2.8673928
    t_200/t_199    2.9336170
    t_1000/t_999   2.9513324
    t_4000/t_3999  2.9546569
    one Richardson step at n=4000:   2.955765235
    published:                       2.955765285651995

Fixed polyominoes, brute-forced by adding one cell at a time:

    a_11/a_10      3.7115
    published:     4.0625696

Both sequences are right -- they are A000081 and A001168 term for term. The
difference is the size of the $1/n$ correction: $r_n=\gamma(1-\tfrac{3}{2n}
+O(n^{-2}))$ in both cases, but the trees can be pushed to $n=4000$ in under a
minute of integer arithmetic while the polyominoes stop dead at $n=11$ because
the enumeration is exponential. So the tree constant is checkable to eight
digits this afternoon, and the polyomino constant is not checkable at all
without Jensen's published series to $n \approx 56$.

That is not a detail about two sequences. **It decides the rigour level, it
decides how many digits the table may honestly hold, and it decides the build
order**, and all three are decided before any table is built, by a script that
takes a minute. In this batch it moved the tree proposal to first and the
polyomino proposal to last, and it turned "there is a known value" -- which is
a hope -- into a number with an error next to it.

The same test also checks the *exponent*: the approach to $12$ from below at
$11.925$ for rooted planar maps is what an $n^{-5/2}$ correction looks like,
and an approach from the wrong side would mean the asymptotic form in the
definition is wrong.

What the skill says now: under "What a good check looks like" it asks for a
named independent fact and lists "a specialisation to a sequence with an OEIS
number" and "a count of something small, done by brute force". Both apply
here, but neither says what to do with the answer.

What it should say: for a constant defined as a limit of a sequence, run the
ratio test on as many terms as are cheap, with one Richardson step, **before**
writing the proposal, and put the number it reached in the proposal. If it
reaches the published digits, the check is a computation and the entry can be
`proven` or better. If it does not -- because the sequence is exponentially
expensive, or the correction is large -- then the check is a citation, the
entry is `heuristic`, and the proposal should say so and rank lower. The two
cases are indistinguishable from the definition alone.

Evidence: `/tmp/checks.py`, `/tmp/checks2.py`, `/tmp/checks4.py`, run
2026-09-23; sequences verified against A000081, A000055, A000168, A000260,
A006156, A001168.

## Reads and searches share one hourly allowance, and a walk of the corpus is most of it

What happened: after a walk of 450 `numberdb.table(...)` calls followed by
roughly ninety `search_text(...)` calls, the client began refusing:

    numberdb.search_text('Tracy-Widom')
    -> RateLimitError: too many requests; retry in 984s

with `numberdb.configure(api_key=...)` set from a valid key. The first reading
of that is that searching is throttled and reading is not, because the walk
went through untouched and the searches did not. **That reading is wrong**, and
it matters. The allowance is **1000 requests per 60 minutes per key**, and a
read and a search each cost one; the walk did not fail because it went first
and spent about 450 of them, and the searches failed because they arrived at
the end of the same bucket. A triage run against the same site minutes later
got the number in full:

    429 {"error": "Rate limit exceeded (1000 requests per 60 minutes)",
         "retry_after": 970}

Two consequences for anybody planning a session.

**A walk of the corpus is a large purchase.** Reading every table from T1
upward is the only complete duplicate check -- the search does not reach
drafts, see below -- but at 444 tables it is nearly half the hour's allowance,
and it leaves too little for the screening that comes after. Walk once, keep
the titles, and do not walk again to re-check something.

**`already_here` costs several queries per name, not one.** It searches the
*distinguishing words of a name separately* -- by design, and the reason is in
its docstring -- so "Growth constants of the classes of planar maps" is four
queries and a batch of eleven names is forty. Screen the whole batch in one
pass at the end; do not re-run it to see a result again.

The failure is at least loud: `RateLimitError` names its own retry delay, so it
cannot be mistaken for an empty corpus the way an unrouted proxy once was.

What the skill says now: it tells the reader to search the corpus first, and
warns that `search_text` returns an object with a `.tables` list, that it
stems, and that no call lists the corpus -- "walk the T-numbers". Nothing says
the walk has a price, or that it comes out of the same purse as the searching
the skill also asks for.

What it should say: reads and searches share one allowance of 1000 requests an
hour on a key. Walking the T-numbers is the complete check and costs one
request per table, so do it once and keep what it returns; screen names in a
single pass, remembering that each name costs one query per distinguishing
word.

Evidence: the session of 2026-09-23T0921 -- 450 `numberdb.table` calls, then
roughly ninety `search_text` calls, then `retry in 984s`; and the 429 body
quoted above, recorded independently the same hour in
`docs/agent-environment.md` under "The failure loop has spent the key's hourly
allowance".

## OEIS serves `urllib` and refuses some other fetchers, so a passing `source_names_it` does not mean the page opens everywhere

What happened: `source_names_it` fetched `https://oeis.org/A051491`,
`A001168`, `A000168`, `A000260`, `A000055` and `A006156` and returned 200 for
every one -- it uses `urllib` with `User-Agent: numberdb-proposal-screen`. A
separate web-fetching tool asked for `https://oeis.org/A006156` in the same
session got `403 Forbidden` three times, including on the `fmt=text` and
`/internal` variants.

So the screen's verdict on an OEIS citation is about the screen's own client,
not about the page's general reachability. This did not cause a wrong
proposal, and it is not a reason to avoid citing OEIS -- the citation is for a
human reader with a browser, who is not blocked. It is a reason not to be
surprised, and not to conclude the page has gone away, when a different tool
cannot read a URL the screen just passed.

What the skill says now: nothing about fetchers, which is right for its
audience.

What it should say: probably nothing either -- this is a note for whoever
maintains the screening step rather than for a contributor. Recorded here so
the next run does not spend a turn deciding OEIS is down.

Evidence: 2026-09-23, six OEIS URLs at 200 through `screen.source_names_it`,
the same host at 403 through a browser-like fetcher minutes apart.
