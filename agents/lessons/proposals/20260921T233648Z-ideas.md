## arb's rigorous integration is on `ComplexBallField`, not on `RealBallField`

What happened: this batch needed to know whether a quantity defined by an
integral can reach `rigour: proven`. The integrands here are real, so the
first thing tried was `RealBallField.integral`, which does not exist:

    RealBallField has .integral: False
    ComplexBallField has .integral: True
    CBF.integral(lambda x, _: x.sin(), 0, 1)
        = [0.45969769413186028259906339255702339626768957938207 +/- 6.8e-60]
    1 - CBF(1).cos()
        = [0.459697694131860282599063392557023396267689579382077 +/- 7.7e-61]

`hasattr(RBF(1), ...)` on every plausible name finds nothing either. So a
contributor with a real-valued integrand, following the skill's advice to
"check with arb/ball arithmetic (`ComplexBallField`, `RealBallField`)", looks
for the method on the field the quantity lives in, does not find it, and
concludes -- wrongly -- that the family cannot be done rigorously. That
conclusion decided the ranking of one proposal in this batch before it was
checked.

What the skill says now: section 7 lists what arb implements -- "`elliptic_k`,
`barnes_g`, `zeta`, `zetaderiv`, `agm`, `airy_ai`, `bessel_J`, Hurwitz
`zeta(s, a)`, `sin_integral`, ..." -- and names both ball fields as the thing
to check with. It says nothing about integration.

What it should say: a quantity defined by an integral can be `proven` in one
call, through `ComplexBallField.integral(f, a, b)`, which implements arb's
rigorous integration; there is no `RealBallField.integral`, so a real
integrand is integrated in the complex field and the imaginary part asserted
to contain zero, exactly as the skill's own `elliptic_k` example does. Worth a
line in the section 5 table beside the `ComplexBallField` row.

Evidence: 2026-09-21, the deployed image, via `agents/sage.sh`.

## Every classical CDF of statistics is in arb's reach, but not by the route the libraries use

What happened: the batch needed `proven` quantiles for four distributions, and
whether that is possible turns entirely on what arb has.

    RealBall.erf              present  -> the normal CDF
    RealBall.gamma_inc_lower  present  -> the chi-squared CDF, as
                                          gamma_inc_lower(k/2, x/2)/gamma(k/2)
    RealBall.beta_lower       ABSENT
    RealBall.beta_inc         ABSENT   -> no incomplete beta at all

Every numerical library computes the Student $t$ and $F$ CDFs from the
regularized incomplete beta, and arb does not expose one. The route that does
work is the one the libraries abandoned: for **integer** degrees of freedom
both CDFs are *finite elementary expressions*. The Student $t$ CDF is the
classical $\theta$-expansion in `arctan`, `sin` and `cos`; the $F$ CDF is
$I_x(a,b)$ with half-integer $a, b$, which reduces to `arcsin` and a finite
sum. Written in `RealBallField(200)` and evaluated at independently computed
mpmath quantiles:

    CDF(2.228138851986274748395490663201806717856, nu=10)
        = [0.97499999999999999999999999999999999999999917915 +/- 7.06e-60]
    CDF(12.70620473617470464602167997884208746767,  nu=1)
        = [0.97500000000000000000000000000000000000000247219 +/- 3.99e-60]

and bisection on them returns an enclosure whose two ends bracket $p$:

    t_0.975(10)   = [2.2281388519862747483954906632018067178560193664 +/- 7.2e-60]
    chi2_0.95(10) = [18.30703805327514687180303099695903319758448841 +/- 4.0e-59]
    z_0.975       = [1.9599639845400542355245944305205515279555500778 +/- 4.3e-60]
    F_0.95(3,10)  = [3.7082648190468444853881904385736184477371153284 +/- 4.2e-60]

The trap in between is worth naming. A Numerical-Recipes continued fraction
for $I_x(a,b)$, written in balls, reproduces all of these to 55 digits and
*looks* proven -- the output is a ball with a small radius. It is not: the
balls carry the rounding error of each step and say nothing about the error of
**truncating the continued fraction**, which is outside the arithmetic
entirely. An enclosure that omits the truncation term is a fixed-precision
result wearing an interval, which is the same mistake as
`RealIntervalField(prec)(some_float_function(x))` in a shape the refusal
cannot catch.

What the skill says now: section 7 warns about wrapping a fixed-precision
result in an interval field, and section 10 warns that a ball wide enough
contains everything. Both are about the value being wrong. Neither covers a
ball that is correctly computed and answers a different question than the one
asked.

What it should say: ball arithmetic bounds the arithmetic, not the
mathematics. Any *truncation* -- a series cut off, a continued fraction
stopped, a matrix truncated -- needs its own bound added to the ball by hand,
and `rigour: proven` is a claim that this was done. Where the truncation is an
alternating series with decreasing terms, as for the Kolmogorov distribution
function $1 - 2\sum(-1)^{k-1}e^{-2k^2x^2}$, the bound is the next term and
costs nothing; where it is a continued fraction it is real work, and the
shorter road is usually a closed form the parameters happen to admit.

Evidence: 2026-09-21, three scripts through `agents/sage.sh`; every value
above cross-checked against an independent mpmath computation at 40 digits,
and against `qt`, `qchisq`, `qf`, `qnorm` and Abramowitz--Stegun Tables
26.1, 26.8 and 26.10.

## `source_names_it` matches words literally; the corpus index stems. A plural in a title fails the screen

What happened: this batch proposes five tables whose titles begin "Quantiles
of ...". Screened against the Wikipedia article that defines each family, four
of the five fail:

    "Quantiles of the chi-squared distribution"
        -> the source does not mention quantiles
    "Quantile function of the chi-squared distribution"
        -> passes, same URL

The only missing word is the plural. The page writes "quantile function" and
never "quantiles"; `_distinguishing` does no stemming, so the two forms are
different words to the screen. The corpus's own text index *does* stem -- the
skill says so, with "regulator also matches regular" -- so the two forms are
the same word to the thing the title actually has to satisfy.

This matters because the last run's lesson, correctly, says the screen's
complaint is information and not a formality: if the article never uses the
word in your title, the word is a specialist's name and the article's
vocabulary is the better title. That lesson is right and this case is its
exception, and the two are indistinguishable from the output. Here the article
*does* name the family in the reader's vocabulary, in the singular. One of the
five is a genuine failure of the kind the earlier lesson describes -- the
Kolmogorov--Smirnov article never says "quantile" in any form, and that table
was retitled "Critical values of the Kolmogorov distribution" in response.

What the skill says now: nothing about the screen's matching, and "a title is
what a search reaches" with the instruction to use `Keywords` for what the
title does not contain.

What it should say, and it is mostly a change to `screen.py` rather than to
the skill: when `source_names_it` complains about a word, try its singular and
its plural before believing the complaint, and report which form the page
uses. A one-word difference in number is a stemming artifact; a word the page
does not contain in any form is the real finding. Until the screen does that,
a proposal should say which forms it tried.

Evidence: 2026-09-21. Eleven (name, URL) pairs screened; the plural form
failed for the normal, Student $t$, chi-squared and $F$ articles and the
singular passed on each of the same four URLs; both forms failed for the
Kolmogorov--Smirnov article, where a word-by-word check confirms "quantile"
does not appear.

## A table of arguments and a table of values of the same function do not overlap in anything findable

What happened: the corpus already holds the functions this batch inverts --
T197 `erf`, T352 the lower incomplete gamma, T353 the incomplete beta -- and
T352 and T353 even carry "chi-squared distribution" and "Student t
distribution" in their `Keywords`, with T353's `complete-note` saying its grid
was chosen to include "all two-sided Student $t$ tail arguments
$\nu/(\nu+t^2)$". Step 3 of the ideation prompt asks what a family is a
specialisation of and whether a reader could get there through what exists, so
this looked, at first, like the strongest possible reason to drop the batch.

It is the opposite. A quantile is the *argument* solving $F(x) = p$; those
tables store $F$'s *values*, which are areas under a density. A reader holding
`3.841458820694126` and searching by number is searching T352's value column,
which holds numbers like $\gamma(1/2, 1/10)$; a collision would be a
coincidence, not a route. The argument 3.84 appears nowhere in T352 and could
not, because T352's grid is $x = j/10$. Two families that are inverse to each
other overlap in **no findable value at all** -- more completely than two
unrelated families, which at least might collide.

And the relation is then worth more as a *check* than it would have been as an
objection: T352 holds $\gamma(a,x)$ at exactly the $a = k/2$ that are the
chi-squared degrees of freedom $k \le 5$, so a generator's CDF can be verified
against stored corpus values before a single quantile is inverted.

What the skill says now: section 1 asks whether a reader could get from their
number to a stored one by trying "a half, a two, a pi", and gives the
elaborate-multiple case as the one that earns a table.

What it should say: add the inverse case, which is cleaner than the multiple
case and comes up often (erf and probit, $\Gamma$ and its zeros, a CDF and its
quantiles, a function and the nodes where it vanishes). If the proposed family
is the inverse of a stored one, it is never findable through it, because one
stores arguments and the other stores values. Propose it, link the two with
the relation named, and use the stored table as the independent check.

Evidence: 2026-09-21. `numberdb.table('T352')` and `('T353')` read directly;
`search_text` on `probit`, `percentile`, `confidence interval` and `hypothesis
test` returns nothing, while `quantile` returns seven tables of which every
one is a stemming coincidence.
