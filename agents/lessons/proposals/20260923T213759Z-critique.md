# Lessons proposed by the T453 critique, 2026-09-23

Format as `agents/lessons/PROPOSALS.md` describes. Nothing here is in force.

## A completeness note that describes the grid by a rule has to be checked against the keys

What happened: T453 stores chi-squared quantiles on fourteen probabilities, and
`comment-grid` describes them by a rule rather than by a list: "one-sided
upper-tail levels $0.10,0.05,0.025,0.01,0.005,0.001$, their lower-tail
complements, and the central reference probabilities $1/4,1/2,3/4$." Six levels
and their complements is fifteen values. Fourteen are stored. $999/1000$ is
there and $1/1000$ is not, so the grid is closed under $p\mapsto 1-p$ at
thirteen of its fourteen points and open at the fourteenth.

Nothing catches this. The generator holds the grid as a literal tuple, the
prose holds it as a rule, and they were written at different moments by
different hands; `verify` recomputes the values that are present and says
nothing about the one that is absent, and `audit_table` reads the grid's
denominators but not the sentence describing it. The reader is the only checker
there is, and the sentence is precisely what a reader checking whether their own
number belongs here reads: the NIST/SEMATECH lower-tail chi-square table has a
0.001 column, so somebody arriving from it is told their number is here and
finds it is not.

The shape is general and not about this family. Any grid stated as "every
$p/q$ with $q\leq8$", "the complements of these", "both halves", "$n$ and $-n$
for each $n$" is an assertion with a truth value, and the enumeration in the
generator is a separate object that can fail it. A missing member is invisible
in a table of 476 rows.

What the skill says now: section 3 says "Say which range you chose and why in
the completeness note. That sentence is the argument, and it is what a reader
checking whether their own number belongs here actually reads", and "Density is
not completeness". Both are about choosing the right sentence. Neither says the
sentence is a claim about the stored keys that can be false.

What it should say: where `complete-note` or a grid comment describes the
arguments by a rule rather than by listing them, make the generator check the
rule rather than restating it in prose -- derive the enumeration from the rule
if you can, and otherwise assert it: if the note says "and their complements",
`assert set(P) == {1 - p for p in P}` before the run starts. One line, and it
fails on the machine of whoever next edits either half. The cheap manual form,
for a table already built, is to read the stored keys back out of
`numberdb.table(tid)['Numbers']` and check the sentence against them, not
against the tuple you think you wrote.

Evidence: 2026-09-23, T453 critique. Stored grid from `GET
/api/table?id=T453`: `1/200 1/100 1/40 1/20 1/10 1/4 1/2 3/4 9/10 19/20 39/40
99/100 199/200 999/1000`; `PROBABILITIES` in
`generators/quantiles-chi-squared-distribution/generate.py` is the same
fourteen and contains no `QQ(1)/QQ(1000)`; `comment-grid` names six upper
levels and "their lower-tail complements"; `GET /api/table/T453/audit`
reported only the decimal-grid finding.

## A grid defended as "the conventional one" needs the source named, and the source usually disagrees a little

What happened: T453's two range comments assert "the conventional significance
levels at which a reader most often meets chi-squared critical values" and "the
consecutive rows $\nu=1,\ldots,30$ used in many printed chi-squared tables,
followed by the common larger rows $\nu=40,50,60,100$", and cite nothing. Both
assertions are close to true and neither is quite true of any one source. The
NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook table, which is the commonest one online, runs $\nu$
consecutively to 100 -- it has no 40/50/60 tail at all -- and its upper-tail
columns are 0.90, 0.95, 0.975, 0.99, 0.999, with no 0.995. The classic textbook
appendix does carry 0.995 and does stop the tail short, but it continues 70, 80,
90 before 100. So the stored grid is a blend of two conventions, and the
prose's claim of *the* convention is what makes that invisible.

Checking the source costs one fetch and changes the table: it settles which
columns belong (the 0.001 complement above), it settles the $\nu$ tail, and it
turns the audit's standing decimal-grid objection into an answered one, because
the skill already admits a fine grid that a named source publishes.

What the skill says now: "If a fine grid is genuinely right, it is because a
named source publishes that grid. Cite it, and check it rather than assuming:
the handbooks are decimal because they were built for hand interpolation, which
is a claim about a book and needs to be true of the book you cite."

What it should say: the same, with the consequence added -- fetch the source
and read its column headings and its index column before writing the grid, not
after, because the usual outcome is not "the source agrees" but "the source has
one column this grid lacks and lacks one this grid has", and that difference is
the decision the completeness note is supposed to record. A grid defended as
conventional without a citation is an author's memory of a table, and memories
of tables lose a column.

Evidence: 2026-09-23, T453 critique.
<https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda3674.htm> fetched
with `curl`: upper-tail columns 0.90 0.95 0.975 0.99 0.999, lower-tail columns
0.10 0.05 0.025 0.01 0.001, $\nu$ consecutive through the nineties. T453 stores
0.995/0.005 as $199/200$ and $1/200$, omits $1/1000$, and takes $\nu \in
\{40,50,60,100\}$ past 30.
