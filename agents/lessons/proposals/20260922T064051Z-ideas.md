# Lessons from the ideation run of 2026-09-22T06:40Z

The batch is `agents/table-ideas/BATCH-2026-09-22T0640.md` -- the Struve,
modified Struve, Scorer, Weber and Anger functions, the particular solutions of
the inhomogeneous Bessel and Airy equations, of which the corpus holds none.
Every value in it was computed before it was proposed, and all three lessons
come from getting a check wrong first.

## `regularized=True` is refused for ${}_1F_2$, so a DLMF series with $\Gamma$ in the denominator has to be written out

What happened: most of the classical special functions the corpus does not yet
hold are a single ${}_1F_2$ or a pair of ${}_0F_1$'s, and Sage's
`ComplexBall.hypergeometric(a, b)` is arb's `acb_hypgeom_pfq`, so they look
like one line each. But DLMF writes these series with gamma functions in the
denominator rather than Pochhammer symbols -- DLMF 11.2.1 for Struve,
11.10.10 and 11.10.11 for Anger-Weber -- and that is exactly what arb calls
the *regularized* hypergeometric. Asking for it:

    CBF(z).hypergeometric([CBF(1)], [CBF(3/2), nu + CBF(3/2)], regularized=True)
    NotImplementedError: regularized=True not yet supported in this case

The docstring does say regularization is "only supported for some $p$ and $q$",
which is easy to read as a footnote and turns out to exclude the case every one
of these functions needs. What works is to pull the $k=0$ gammas out as
reciprocal gammas and multiply by the ordinary ${}_1F_2$:

    (z/2)**(nu+1) * CBF(3/2).rgamma() * (nu + CBF(3/2)).rgamma() \
        * (-(z**2)/4).hypergeometric([CBF(1)], [CBF(3/2), nu + CBF(3/2)])

`rgamma` and not `1/gamma`: the reciprocal gamma is zero at a pole, which is
the behaviour the series has, where dividing by a ball containing a pole is
not. That form reproduced DLMF 11.4.5, 11.4.9, 11.4.3 and the recurrence
11.4.23 at 32 of 32 order-argument pairs.

The rewriting is not free, though, and that is the next lesson.

What the skill says now: section 5 names the arb functions available
(`elliptic_k`, `barnes_g`, `zeta`, `airy_ai`, `bessel_J`, `sin_integral`) and
says "arb implements a great deal". `hypergeometric` is not among them, and
nothing says that the functions arb does *not* implement are mostly one pFq
away.

What it should say: a named special function arb has no method for is usually a
generalized hypergeometric, and `ComplexBall.hypergeometric(a, b)` evaluates
one in ball arithmetic, so the whole of DLMF chapters 9 to 13 is reachable at
`proven` rigour. Two practical notes belong with it: the DLMF form with
$\Gamma$'s in the denominator is arb's *regularized* pFq and
`regularized=True` raises `NotImplementedError` for ${}_1F_2$, so write the
prefactor with `rgamma`; and check the resulting ball's radius, because the
prefactor form has poles the regularized form does not.

Evidence: 2026-09-22, `agents/sage.sh`, SageMath in the deployed image,
`ComplexBallField(400)`; `/tmp/probe5.py` raised the `NotImplementedError` and
`/tmp/probe6.py` passed every identity with the `rgamma` form.

## A ball of *infinite* radius overlaps everything, and `rigour: proven` does not catch it

What happened: the `rgamma` form above is $0\cdot\infty$ wherever a lower
parameter of the ${}_1F_2$ is a nonpositive integer. For the Struve function
that is $\nu=-\tfrac32,-\tfrac52,\ldots$; for the Anger and Weber series it is
every integer $\nu\geq2$. The ball that comes back is not an error and not a
wide interval -- it has radius $\infty$, or a `nan` real part -- and it
therefore agrees with every comparison offered to it. My first run reported

    H_{-3/2}(1)   = -J_{3/2}(1)   overlap=True   rad=inf
    H_{-3/2}(9/4) = -J_{3/2}(9/4) overlap=True   rad=inf
    H_{-3/2}(5)   = -J_{3/2}(5)   overlap=True   rad=inf

three confirmations of an identity it had not tested, and separately eight
"confirmations" that the Anger function equals the Bessel function at
$\nu=0,1,2,3$, of which only the first two were real: at $\nu=2$ and $3$ the
value was `nan`.

Two things make this worse than the wide-ball case the skill already warns
about. First, **it is parameter-specific**: the same code is correct at
$\nu=\tfrac13,\tfrac12,\tfrac23,\tfrac32$ and junk at $\nu=2$, so a spot check
at three sensible parameters passes and a full grid contains a handful of
poisoned rows. Second, **`rigour: proven` does not protect against it**. The
package refuses a value that carries *no* error of its own, which is the
opposite mistake; a value whose error is infinite satisfies that test.

So the radius check belongs in `value()`, not only in a check script:

    assert not v.real().is_NaN() and v.rad() < 2.0**(-wanted_bits)

What the skill says now: "An enclosure that contains everything agrees with
everything", with the examples of a `nan` ball from `psi(-1/2)` and a ball of
radius 1.4e18. Both are about a comparison being believed; and section 8 says
`proven` is enforced "in one direction", against a value with no error.

What it should say: the same sentence, extended in two ways. That the
enforcement of `proven` is only against a zero-width value, so a generator
should assert an *upper* bound on the radius itself before returning. And that
when a family is computed from a closed form with poles in its parameters, the
blow-up is at isolated parameter values, so a sample of the grid is not
evidence: check the radius on every entry, or enumerate the exceptional
parameters and route them to a different formula. (Here: DLMF 11.4.3 for
Struve at negative half-integer order, DLMF 11.10.29 and 11.10.22 for Anger
and Weber at integer order.)

Evidence: 2026-09-22, `/tmp/probe4.py` and `/tmp/probe6.py`, `ComplexBallField(400)`;
`rad=inf` with `overlaps=True` at $\nu=-\tfrac32$ and `is_NaN()` true at
$\nu=2,3,4$.

## Do not check an identity against a value you defined with that identity

What happened: the two Scorer functions satisfy
$\operatorname{Gi}+\operatorname{Hi}=\operatorname{Bi}$ (DLMF 9.12.11), and the
corpus holds $\operatorname{Bi}$ in T202, so that identity is the obvious
independent check on a pair of new tables. I computed $\operatorname{Hi}$ from
its series and then, because it was one line shorter,
$\operatorname{Gi}=\operatorname{Bi}-\operatorname{Hi}$. The check then passed
at every argument, and had tested nothing whatever about $\operatorname{Gi}$:
it was $\operatorname{Bi}-\operatorname{Hi}+\operatorname{Hi}=\operatorname{Bi}$.
What it did test, weakly, was that both balls were finite.

Written properly -- both series out in full, DLMF 9.12.15 for
$\operatorname{Gi}$ and 9.12.17 for $\operatorname{Hi}$ -- the same identity
becomes a strong check, and the reason is structural: splitting each series by
$k \bmod 3$ gives three blocks, and the two functions differ only in the
coefficients they put on them, $(1,1,1)$ against
$(\tfrac12,\tfrac12,-1)$. Their sum therefore has to cancel the third block
exactly and leave $\tfrac32$ of the first two, which is arb's independent
$\operatorname{Bi}$. That passed at 367 of 367 arguments, with the residual at
$x=-2.3$ enclosed in $-5.8\cdot10^{-121}\pm6.2\cdot10^{-118}$: an enclosure of
zero narrow enough to mean something.

The general shape is not about Scorer functions. When a family of $n$ related
quantities satisfies $n-1$ identities, computing $n-1$ of them and deriving the
last makes every one of those identities vacuous, and the derived table then
has no check at all while appearing to have the best one in the batch. The cost
of computing each member from its own definition is one more series; the cost
of not doing it is a table nobody has verified.

What the skill says now: "Check new values against something independent. Not
the code that produced them", with the Fibonacci polynomials checked against
`fibonacci()` and against identities. The failure here is subtler than checking
against your own code: the code is independent, and the *identity* is the thing
that is not.

What it should say: an identity is only a check on the quantities it was not
used to define. Where a relation ties several members of a family together,
compute each member from its own definition, and say in `rigour details` which
values were computed independently -- otherwise a reader cannot tell a verified
table from a rearranged one. The cheaper derived form belongs in `Formulas`,
where it tells a reader how to get one from the other, not in the generator.

Evidence: 2026-09-22, `/tmp/probe6.py` (Gi as a difference: check vacuous) against
`/tmp/probe7.py` (both series independent: 367 of 367 arguments,
$|$residual$|<6.2\cdot10^{-118}$).

## Search OEIS by the digits of a real family's simplest member, not by its name

What happened: I wrote into this batch, as a named weakness, that no
third-party decimal expansion existed for any of these six functions -- nothing
to quote the way T187 quotes OEIS A334380 for $J_0(1)$ -- and that the
identities would have to carry the whole weight. That was wrong, and it was
wrong for a reason worth naming: I had searched OEIS the way you search it for
a *sequence*, by the family's name, and these are families of reals.

Searching instead by the first fourteen decimal digits of each family's
simplest member, comma-separated, found three:

    5,6,8,6,5,6,6,2,7,0,4,8,2,8  -> A397738  Struve H_0 at 1, 100 digits
    7,1,0,2,4,3,1,8,5,9,3,7,8,9  -> A197037  modified Struve L_0 at 1, 107
    2,0,4,9,7,5,5,4,2,4,8,2,0,0  -> A358564  Scorer Gi(0), 87 digits

all three agreeing digit for digit with a computation independent of the rest of
the run -- plain Python `decimal` at 120 places, summing DLMF 11.2.3 and 11.2.4,
no Sage and no arb. A397738 matched on all 100 of its digits. So four of the six
tables gained a published anchor, since $\operatorname{Hi}(0)=2\operatorname{Gi}(0)$
puts A358564 behind $\operatorname{Hi}$ as well.

The name search does not find these, and looking at why is the useful part.
`"Anger function"` returns nothing; `"Weber function"` returns seven entries,
every one the modular or partition-theoretic Weber function rather than
$\mathbf{E}_\nu$; `"Struve function"` does return A197037 but among ten hits,
most of them integer sequences that merely appear in a Struve expansion. A
constant's OEIS entry is named for whatever its submitter called it, and
`%N A397738 Decimal expansion of the Struve H-function of order 0 at 1` is not a
phrase this batch's own prose contains. The digits are the one search key that
does not depend on agreeing about a name -- which is the argument for NumberDB
itself, applied to the search for NumberDB's own sources.

Two practical notes. The query form `q=5,6,8,6,...&fmt=text` works and needs no
key; fourteen digits was enough to make every hit unique here, and OEIS strips
the decimal point, so lead with the first significant digit and not with a zero.
And the *absence* of a hit is a result to write down: I confirmed by digits that
$\mathbf{E}_{1/2}(1)$ and $\mathbf{J}_{1/2}(1)$ are in no OEIS entry, which is
why the two Anger-Weber proposals are ranked last in that batch.

What the skill says now: OEIS "holds a real number only one sequence at a time,
as the digits of that one constant, which is cumbersome for a *family* of
reals". That sentence is about what OEIS is bad at, and reads as a reason not
to go there. It is also a description of exactly where to look for a check.

What it should say: the same observation, turned round. Because OEIS stores a
real as the digits of one constant, a proposed family of reals should be
searched there by the digits of its simplest member -- $x=0$, $x=1$, $\nu=0$ --
before concluding that no independent published value exists. Search by digits
and not by name: the entry is named for whatever its submitter called the
function, and a family with a contested name will be missed. A search that
returns nothing is itself worth recording, as evidence for how well a table can
be checked.

Evidence: 2026-09-22, `urllib` against `https://oeis.org/search?q=...&fmt=text`
from the runner; A397738 (100 digits, all matching), A197037 (107 digits, first
101 matching, the 101 being my own precision), A358564 (87 digits); and no hit
for the digits of $\mathbf{E}_{1/2}(1)=0.0995075426400431653\ldots$ or
$\mathbf{J}_{1/2}(1)=0.8551653096792622201\ldots$.
