## A table's own comments say where it stops, and the open request is usually one step past that

What happened: this run was looking for a family in an area the corpus does
not cover, with an open `table wanted` issue behind it. Searching for names
found nothing useful, because the family has no table and therefore no name in
the corpus to match. What found it was reading the *comments* of the nearest
table that does exist. T144, *Kloosterman sums modulo a prime*, says in its
comment (15): "The twisted Kloosterman sum $K(a,b,\chi;p)=\sum_x \chi(x)
e^{2\pi i(ax+b\bar x)/p}$ is $K(a,b;p)$ for the trivial character ... The
entries here are the sums for the trivial character." That sentence is
numberdb-data#13, *Kloosterman sums of Dirichlet characters*, open since 2021,
written out with its definition and its boundary, by somebody who had already
decided not to build it. Two more of the same table's comments, (12) and (14),
draw two further boundaries: prime moduli only, because prime powers have a
closed form, and the Kloosterman angle, defined but not stored.

The same reading answered three questions a proposal has to answer anyway --
what convention to use, what the neighbour already holds, and what argument
the corpus has already made against a nearby family -- from a page that was
written to be read.

What the skill says now: search the corpus for the family, and for what the
family is a specialisation of, by name. It says a table's comments hold
"conventions, caveats, meaning at special points, alternative indexing",
which is advice to the writer of a table, not to somebody looking for a gap.

What it should say: when proposing in an area the corpus already touches, read
the **comments** of the two or three nearest tables before searching for
anything. A well-written table states what it excludes and why, so its
comments are a list of the families next to it -- and the ones excluded for a
bad reason, or for a reason that has since stopped holding, are the proposals
with the strongest case. A family named in a neighbour's comment also arrives
with its notation and its normalisation already fixed, which is most of the
"what has to be decided" section written for you.

Evidence: 2026-09-20. `numberdb.table('T144')`, comments (12), (14), (15),
against `agents/table-ideas/screen.py requests`, where #13 is listed with no
body at all. `already_asked('Twisted Kloosterman sums')` returned nothing,
because #13 is titled *Kloosterman sums of Dirichlet characters*.

## A 404 page can contain the word you are grepping for

What happened: a proposal was going to cite
`https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salié_sum` as the source naming the Salié sums.
Fetching that URL by hand and grepping the body for "salié" found it ten
times, so the check passed. It passed on a **404 page**: there is no Wikipedia
article of that name, and what was being grepped was Wikipedia's "the page
does not exist" page, which repeats the requested title and lists search
suggestions containing it. `screen.source_names_it` refused the same URL --
`source answered 404` -- because it looks at the status before it looks at the
text.

The general shape: for a guessed URL the failure mode is not an empty page but
a page *about* the words you guessed, which is exactly what a text search
rewards.

What the skill says now: cite Wikipedia, MathWorld, DLMF, OEIS or a paper, and
link outward only to durable sources. Nothing about checking that the page is
there.

What it should say: a citation is checked by status **and** text, in that
order, and a guessed encyclopedia URL that answers 404 means the family is not
called that -- which is worth knowing before the table is titled, not after.
The same holds for a link inside a finished table: a Wikipedia article can be
renamed, and the redirect that keeps the old title working is not guaranteed.

Evidence: 2026-09-20. `curl -s -o /dev/null -w '%{http_code}'
'https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sali%C3%A9_sum'` -> `404`, body 13 KB
containing "Salié" ten times;
`source_names_it('Salie sums', 'https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sali%C3%A9_sum')`
-> `source answered 404`. The family is real; its citation is Salié's 1932
paper in *Mathematische Zeitschrift*, which T144 already lists as its
reference [3].

## Walking the T-numbers through the API runs into the rate limiter, and a refusal is not an empty corpus

What happened: a walk over `numberdb.table('T1')` ... `numberdb.table('T367')`
to see what the corpus covers got through about 140 tables and then answered
`RateLimitError: too many requests; retry in 375s` for every call after it.
The same limiter then refused `already_here` for five names in a row during
the screen, which is the call whose answer decides whether a family is a
duplicate. `screen.already_here` returns the refusal as its one result --
`(could not ask the corpus: RateLimitError: ...)` -- rather than an empty
list, so it reads as a failed question and not as "nothing similar is here",
which is the distinction that matters.

The paginated listing at `https://numberdb.org/tables?page=N` is not limited
the same way: eight requests give every title.

What the skill says now: nothing about a rate limit. It warns that a failed
search and an empty answer must not look the same, in the context of the
proxy.

What it should say: the API rate-limits a burst of reads and says how long to
wait; a contributor walking the corpus, or screening several names at once,
should sleep between calls and re-ask on refusal, and must not treat a refusal
as an answer. Where only titles are wanted, read the listing pages instead.

Evidence: 2026-09-20. `RateLimitError: too many requests; retry in 375s` from
`numberdb.table('T280')` and from `already_here('Twisted Kloosterman sums')`;
the same names screened cleanly after a 400-second wait.
