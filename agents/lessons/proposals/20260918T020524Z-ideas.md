## `import numberdb.sage` leaves `sage.rings.qqbar` half built: call `_init_qqbar()` before using `AA`

What happened: a proposal for the classical test matrices rests on computing
eigenvalues and condition numbers as exact algebraic numbers, so every check
went through `AA`. Root isolation itself works. Two ordinary things next to it
do not:

    from sage.rings.qqbar import AA, QQbar
    hash(AA(2).sqrt())      # NameError: name 'AA_hash_offset' is not defined
    QQbar.zeta(12).real()   # NameError: name 'QQbar_I' is not defined

The first bites through `set()` and `dict`, which is how it was met -- a
`sorted(set([...AA elements...]))` to deduplicate interval endpoints. Neither
error mentions the module that is not initialised, and both look like a broken
Sage rather than a missing call.

`sage/rings/qqbar.py` defines `AA_hash_offset`, `QQbar_I` and the rest inside
`_init_qqbar()`, which `sage.all` calls on import. A file that follows the
skill's advice and imports rings by name never calls it, so those globals stay
unset until something reaches for them, which may be deep inside an
exactification. The fix is one line after the `numberdb.sage` import:

    import numberdb.sage as numberdb
    from sage.rings.qqbar import _init_qqbar
    _init_qqbar()

Confirmed by elimination: `hash(AA(2).sqrt())` raises before the call,
returns `1188143105183040880` after it, and returns the same value after
`import sage.all` instead.

What the skill says now: "Name the rings you use rather than importing
`sage.all`", and lists what named imports do not bring -- root finding,
`RealBall.str`, `QQ('1.25')`.

What it should say: add `AA` and `QQbar` to that list, with the remedy.
Importing `sage.rings.qqbar` gives you the field, but not the module globals
that `sage.all` would have initialised, so hashing an algebraic number or
building a root of unity raises `NameError` on a name you never wrote. Call
`sage.rings.qqbar._init_qqbar()` once after importing `numberdb.sage`.

Evidence: 2026-09-18, `/tmp/check5.py`, `/tmp/check6.py`, `/tmp/check7.py`,
run through `agents/sage.sh`. Sage 10.x in the builder image.

## `sage.functions.*` cannot be imported by name at all: it drags in the symbolic ring and breaks

What happened: `from sage.functions.other import binomial` and
`from sage.functions.trig import sin`, written in exactly the style the skill
recommends, both raise

    AttributeError: cannot access submodule 'function' of module
    'sage.symbolic' (most likely due to a circular import)

from `sage/symbolic/expression.pyx`. It is not about which function is being
imported: every module under `sage.functions` imports `sage.symbolic.function`
at module level, so the first one to be imported fails.

The replacements are elsewhere and are better anyway, because they are not
symbolic: `math.comb`, `math.sin` and `math.pi` for numerics,
`sage.arith.misc.binomial` for exact integers, and a three-line recurrence for
a classical orthogonal polynomial (`chebyshev_T` lives in
`sage.functions.orthogonal_polys`, which is unreachable for the same reason;
$T_0=1$, $T_1=x$, $T_{k+1}=2xT_k-T_{k-1}$ over `QQ` costs two lines and gives
an exact polynomial, whose roots in `AA` are the Chebyshev nodes).

What the skill says now: it lists three things named imports do not bring, all
of them modules that are simply absent.

What it should say: this is a fourth and different case -- `sage.functions` is
present and raises when imported. Do not reach for it; a symbolic function is
the wrong tool in a generator in any case, because what a table stores is a
number or an exact polynomial and neither wants the symbolic ring in the way.

Evidence: 2026-09-18, `/tmp/check1.py` and `/tmp/check2.py` through
`agents/sage.sh`; both failed on the import line before any arithmetic, and
both ran after substituting `math.comb`.

## An algebraic value can be `proven` without ball arithmetic, and it is often cheaper

What happened: every value proposed in the test-matrix batch -- eigenvalues,
singular values, condition numbers, and the Lebesgue constants of
interpolation nodes -- is an algebraic number, because it comes from a matrix
with rational entries or from maximising a polynomial with rational
coefficients. That means there is a second route to `proven` besides arb:
build the object over `QQ`, take the characteristic polynomial or the
derivative exactly, and isolate the roots in `AA`, which carries a certified
isolating interval and will refine to any precision asked of it.

It is not a slower route. Measured in the builder image: the whole Hilbert
family to $n=20$, characteristic polynomial and condition number, in under two
seconds; $\kappa_2(H_{20})=2.452157\times10^{28}$ in under one. The Lebesgue
constant of $21$ equally spaced nodes, which is the maximum over $22$
subintervals of a polynomial of degree $20$, in $0.9$ s. Where it does get
expensive is when the *inputs* are algebraic rather than rational: the same
computation at Chebyshev nodes, which are roots of $T_{n+1}$, costs $0.2$ s at
$n=4$, $0.7$ s at $n=8$ and $5.8$ s at $n=12$, so a table over algebraic nodes
needs a ball-arithmetic fallback at moderate $n$ and an exactly computed
prefix to check it against.

What the skill says now: the rigour table maps `proven` to "interval or ball
arithmetic throughout", and the advice is to prefer `RealBallField` and
`ComplexBallField` for anything transcendental.

What it should say: "anything transcendental" is the right scope, and the
sentence beside it should say what to do when the value is algebraic. An
eigenvalue, a singular value, a root of an exact polynomial or a maximum of
one is `proven` from exact arithmetic plus certified root isolation in `AA`,
and the interval `AA` hands back is an enclosure in the same sense arb's is.
The trap to name is the opposite one: isolating the roots of a polynomial
whose coefficients were computed in floating point proves nothing about the
quantity the polynomial was supposed to have.

Evidence: 2026-09-18, `/tmp/check3.py` (timings, Hilbert and Frank),
`/tmp/check4.py` (equally spaced Lebesgue constants: $\Lambda_4=2.2078243973$,
$\Lambda_{10}=29.8999554833$, $\Lambda_{20}=10986.7058926728$, against
$2.20782$, $29.89996$ from a $200001$-point scan), `/tmp/check6.py`
(Chebyshev nodes and the timings above).

## A condition number quoted with its constant may not be a check: test the growth rate first

What happened: the proposal for condition numbers of the classical test
matrices wanted an independent check on $\kappa_2(H_n)$, and the obvious
candidate is the asymptotic usually quoted for it,
$\kappa_2(H_n)\sim(1+\sqrt2)^{4n+4}/\!\left(2^{15/4}\sqrt{\pi n}\right)$. It
overshoots the exactly computed values by a factor of about $55$: the ratio of
computed to asymptotic is $0.0174$ at $n=10$, $0.0184$ at $n=20$ and $0.0190$
at $n=30$ -- drifting so slowly that no amount of further $n$ would rescue it,
which is the tell that the discrepancy is a constant and not a convergence.
The growth rate in the same formula is right: the measured ratios
$\kappa_2(H_{n+1})/\kappa_2(H_n)$ run $32.32, 32.50, \dots, 33.25$ for
$n=8,\dots,20$, approaching $(1+\sqrt2)^4=33.9705\ldots$ from below exactly as
the $\sqrt n$ factor predicts.

Had the asymptotic been used as the check, it would have rejected values that
are exact.

What the skill says now: "There is a known closed form is not a check; it is a
hope", and it asks for a specialisation, a published value, an identity or a
brute-force count.

What it should say: an asymptotic is a check on the *rate*, not on the value,
unless its constant has been verified independently -- and a constant
transcribed from a summary is exactly the kind of thing that carries a
normalisation from some other scaling of the object. Check the rate, which is
usually robust, and treat a constant factor that does not move as evidence
about the formula rather than about the values.

Evidence: 2026-09-18, `/tmp/check8.py` and `/tmp/check9.py` through
`agents/sage.sh`. Exact values from characteristic polynomials over `QQ` with
roots isolated in `AA`: $\kappa_2(H_{10})=1.602629\times10^{13}$,
$\kappa_2(H_{20})=2.452157\times10^{28}$,
$\kappa_2(H_{30})=4.227806\times10^{43}$. Wikipedia's *Hilbert matrix* article
gives "about $4.8\times10^5$" for $n=5$, against the computed
$4.766073\times10^5$, and that one does check out.
