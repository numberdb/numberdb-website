## Rigorous integration is a `ComplexBallField` method; `RealBallField` has none

What happened: a check of the mean distance between two random points of the
unit $n$-cube needed $\int_0^\infty(1-g(s)^n)s^{-3/2}ds$ in ball arithmetic.
`RBF.integral(f, a, b)` raised `AttributeError: 'RealBallField_with_category'
object has no attribute 'integral'`, for every one of five calls, which reads
like "Sage cannot integrate rigorously" and is not what it means. The method
is on `ComplexBallField`: `CBF.integral(lambda z, analytic: ..., a, b)`, arb's
`acb_calc_integrate`. A real integrand is integrated by building it over
`CBF`, calling `CBF.integral`, and taking `.real()` of the answer; the
enclosure survives.

What the skill says now: it lists what arb implements (`elliptic_k`,
`barnes_g`, `zeta`, `airy_ai`, ...) and never mentions integration, so a
reader who needs an integral has no reason to think it is available at all.

What it should say: a definite integral can be `proven` too. Use
`ComplexBallField(prec).integral(f, a, b)` with `f(z, analytic)` returning a
ball; there is no `RealBallField.integral`, so real integrands go through
`CBF` and `.real()`. The `analytic` flag is ignored for an integrand analytic
on the whole interval, and where it is not, return an indeterminate ball so
arb falls back to a crude bound on that subinterval.

Evidence: 2026-09-23, Sage 10.9 in the deployed image. `RBF.integral` raised
the `AttributeError` above; `CBF.integral` returned
$\Delta_1(3)=0.661707182267176235155831133248413581746400136$ with radius
$9.3\cdot10^{-61}$, which is OEIS A073012 to every digit printed.

## A wide ball from `integral` is a silent failure, and it looks like an answer

What happened: the same integral, handed to `CBF.integral` in one piece over
$[10^{-25},10^{14}]$, returned in 0.8 seconds with the value
$1.66\cdot10^{8}\pm3.35\cdot10^{8}$. Nothing raised. The call is a *valid*
enclosure of the answer, since that ball contains $0.6617$ and every other
number below $10^8$, so a script that prints the midpoint prints
$166423085.5$ and a script that checks `overlaps` reports success. Two
separate causes, and both have to be fixed before the digits mean anything:

- **Range.** arb bisects, so an interval spanning 39 decades exhausts the
  subdivision limit before it has resolved anything. Integrating decade by
  decade and summing the balls fixed it; the tails beyond the decades were
  bounded by hand from $0\leq 1-g^n\leq 1$.
- **Cancellation at the endpoint.** The integrand $(1-g(t^2)^n)/t^2$ has
  $1-g^n\approx nt^2/6$, so near $t=10^{-k}$ it is a difference of two numbers
  agreeing to $2k$ decimal places, divided by $10^{-2k}$. At 400 bits and
  $t=10^{-50}$ that is an absolute error of $10^{+29}$ in a quantity of size
  $1/3$. The working precision has to exceed the target by **twice** the
  number of decades integrated: 800 bits reaching down to $10^{-60}$ gave
  radius $10^{-60}$, 400 bits reaching down to $10^{-50}$ gave radius
  $10^{+29}$.

What the skill says now: "An enclosure that contains everything agrees with
everything", with the `nan` ball and the radius-$1.4\cdot10^{18}$ ball as the
examples. Both examples are about a value being *compared*; neither is about
a value being *computed*, and the numerical-integration case is the one where
a plausible-looking number comes back in under a second.

What it should say: after any `integral`, check the radius against the digits
you are about to write before looking at the midpoint. A wide ball means the
range was too long or the integrand cancels at an endpoint; the fixes are to
split the range geometrically, to bound the ends analytically, and to carry
twice as many guard digits as the decades you integrate over.

Evidence: 2026-09-23, the same script at three settings: one piece over
$[10^{-25},10^{14}]$ at 220 bits, radius $3.4\cdot10^{8}$; decades down to
$10^{-50}$ at 400 bits, radius $2.2\cdot10^{29}$; decades down to $10^{-60}$
at 800 bits, radius $9.3\cdot10^{-61}$ and $\Delta_1(1)=1/3$,
$\Delta_1(2)=$ A091505, $\Delta_1(3)=$ A073012 to all printed digits.

## A closed form copied with two coefficients swapped evaluates to a plausible constant

What happened: Robbins' constant was written down as
$\frac{4+17\sqrt2-6\sqrt3-7\pi}{105}+\frac{2}{5}\log(1+\sqrt2)
+\frac{1}{5}\log(2+\sqrt3)$, with the two logarithm coefficients the wrong
way round. It evaluates to $0.574590320286121498\ldots$: a perfectly
respectable-looking constant, positive, of the right order, stable to sixty
digits under any precision you like. The published value is
$0.661707182267176235\ldots$ (A073012), and only comparing against it found
the error. Nothing internal to the computation could have: a wrong closed form
is not an unstable one.

What the skill says now: "Check new values against something independent. Not
the code that produced them". True and aimed at a generator; a *transcribed
closed form* has no code to be independent of, and reads like the check
rather than like the thing needing one.

What it should say: a closed form taken from a paper or a reference page is a
transcription, and transcriptions fail silently, because every rearrangement
of a correct formula is still a plausible number. Evaluate it once against
the published decimal digits of the same constant before any of it goes into a
table or into a proposal, and quote those digits beside it.

Evidence: 2026-09-23, both orderings evaluated at 220 bits; and the
independent ball-arithmetic integral above, which agreed with the correct
ordering to 30 digits and settled it.
