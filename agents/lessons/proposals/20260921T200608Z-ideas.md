## A stored value that is the *inverse* of a function is made `proven` by two evaluations of the function, not by a root finder

What happened: a batch of quantile tables was screened -- the $z$, $t$,
$\chi^2$, $F$ and Kolmogorov critical values, each of which is defined as the
solution $x$ of $F(x)=p$ rather than as the value of anything. The obvious
worry was that no such table could carry `rigour: proven`, because arb has no
inverse error function, no inverse incomplete gamma, and no root finder that
returns a ball.

It does not need one. The distribution function is strictly increasing, so an
approximation from any source becomes a proof in two evaluations:

```python
from sage.rings.real_arb import RealBallField
R = RealBallField(200)

def certify(cdf, guess, p, delta):
    """A proven enclosure of the quantile, from monotonicity alone."""
    lo, hi = R(guess) - R(delta), R(guess) + R(delta)
    if cdf(lo).upper() < p and cdf(hi).lower() > p:
        return lo.union(hi)          # the quantile is inside, proven
    raise ValueError('the guess is not good to %s' % delta)
```

The approximation can come from mpmath, from SciPy, from a published table or
from a guess: nothing about its provenance enters the claim, because what is
proven is the bracket. With $\delta = 10^{-40}$ at 200 bits this certified
$\Phi^{-1}(0.975)$ and $\chi^2_{\nu,p}$ for $\nu = 1, 10$ and
$p = 0.05, 0.95$ on the first attempt.

Two conditions the code must state rather than assume: that the function is
monotone on the interval searched (otherwise the bracket encloses *a*
solution, not *the* one the definition names), and that the comparison uses
`upper()` on one side and `lower()` on the other. Writing
`cdf(lo) < p` compares balls and is not the question being asked.

What the skill says now: that a real is stored as an interval containing it,
that balls carry the error through every step, and that wrapping a
fixed-precision result in `RealIntervalField` claims a width of zero and is
refused. Nothing about a value that is defined implicitly.

What it should say: where a table's value is the solution of $f(x)=y$ for a
monotone $f$ the library can evaluate in ball arithmetic, compute the root any
way at all and then *prove* the enclosure by evaluating $f$ in ball arithmetic
at both ends of a narrow interval around it. The rigour of such a table comes
from the two evaluations, not from the solver, so a table of zeros, quantiles,
or any other implicitly defined quantity can be `proven` even where the
library offers no inverse function.

Evidence: 2026-09-21, ideation run `20260921T200608Z`, `/tmp/w2-ideas/feasible.py`
through `agents/sage.sh`: `ball certification of a 1e-40 box: True` for
$\Phi^{-1}(0.975) = 1.959963984540054235524594$ and for three $\chi^2$
quantiles, at `RealBallField(200)`.

## What arb offers a statistics table: `gamma_inc_lower` on a real ball, but no incomplete beta and no `hypergeometric`

What happened: the same batch needed four distribution functions in ball
arithmetic. Introspecting a `RealBall` for the relevant methods gives

    ['beta', 'erf', 'erfi', 'exp', 'expm1', 'gamma', 'gamma_inc',
     'gamma_inc_lower', 'log_gamma', 'rgamma']

so the normal ($\operatorname{erf}$) and $\chi^2$
($\gamma(a,x)/\Gamma(a)$, from `gamma_inc_lower` and `gamma`) are immediate,
and the Kolmogorov distribution function is an alternating series whose
truncation error is its next term, closed with `add_error`.

The Student $t$ and $F$ distribution functions are the regularised incomplete
*beta*, and there is no `beta_lower`, no `beta_inc` and no `hypergeometric`
on a real ball at all. `ComplexBall.hypergeometric` exists, so ${}_2F_1$ is
reachable through the complex field at the cost of having to check that the
result contains no imaginary part.

The better route is arithmetic rather than special: for integer degrees of
freedom the parameters of $I_x(a,b)$ are integers and half-integers, so the
recurrence $I_x(a+1,b) = I_x(a,b) - x^a(1-x)^b/(a\,B(a,b))$ reaches every row
from $I_x(\frac12,\frac12) = \frac2\pi\arcsin\sqrt x$ and $I_x(1,1) = x$, in
real ball arithmetic, with the precision loss visible in the radius rather
than hidden. Student's $t$ on integer $\nu$ has an elementary distribution
function outright.

What the skill says now: that arb implements a great deal, and names
`elliptic_k`, `barnes_g`, `zeta`, `zetaderiv`, `agm`, `airy_ai`, `bessel_J`,
Hurwitz `zeta(s,a)` and `sin_integral`. A reader takes from that list that the
incomplete functions are there too, and half of them are.

What it should say: check the method exists on the ball class you mean before
planning a table around it -- `dir(RealBallField(53)(1))` is the whole of the
check -- and expect the incomplete beta to be missing. Where a special
function is absent, a recurrence in exact or ball arithmetic from a base case
the library does have is usually shorter than reaching for the complex field,
and it keeps the precision loss on the page.

Evidence: 2026-09-21, `/tmp/w2-ideas/feasible.py` through `agents/sage.sh`:
the method list above; `beta_lower False`, `beta_inc False`,
`hypergeometric False` on a `RealBall`, `ComplexBall hypergeometric: True`.

## Name a table in the words its source uses, plural included: the screen tests them literally, and so does a reader's search

What happened: six statistics tables were proposed as "Quantiles of the ...
distribution", which is what the objects are in probability. Three of the six
failed `source_names_it` against correct, well-chosen pages -- Wikipedia's
*Chi-squared distribution*, its *Chi-squared test*, the NIST/SEMATECH
handbook's own table of $\chi^2$ critical values, Wikipedia's *F-test* and its
*Kolmogorov--Smirnov test*:

    the source does not mention quantiles -- either the name is not what this
    family is called, or the wrong page was cited

Every one of those pages discusses exactly these numbers. What they do not
contain is the string `quantiles`: they say *quantile* in the singular, and
where they name the family they say **critical values** or **percentage
points**. The screen is strict about the plural because it tests substrings,
and being strict was right here: "critical values of the $F$ distribution" is
both what the literature calls them and what a reader types. The batch was
renamed accordingly, and the one table that really is called by the other word
-- the standard normal, whose quantiles are quantiles -- kept it.

What the skill says now: that the title is what a search reaches, that the
test is what somebody holding one of these numbers would type, and that a word
the title lacks belongs in `Keywords`. It does not say where to find that
word.

What it should say: take the title's nouns from the source you are citing. If
the page that documents the family never uses your phrase, a reader searching
for the family will not use it either, and the disagreement is worth resolving
before the table is created, since the title is fixed by then. The plural is
part of this: a corpus search stems and a source page does not, so
"quantiles" and "quantile" find different things outside the database.

Evidence: 2026-09-21, ideation run `20260921T200608Z`, `/tmp/w2-ideas/src.py`
and `/tmp/w2-ideas/src2.py`: eight failures on `quantiles`, `squared` and
`critical` across five pages, and `OK` for every renamed title.
