# Lessons proposed by the T6 growth critique, 2026-09-21

Three, all met while reading T6 (*Diagonal Ramsey numbers*) against
<https://numberdb.org/skill>. All three are reachable by a contributor with a
laptop, Python and a browser; none of them is about this deployment.

---

## Cite a dynamic survey by its revision, because its DOI always resolves to the newest one

What happened: T6 declares `sources: CITE{Rad}, CITE{Wiki}` and cites
Radziszowski's *Small Ramsey Numbers* as "Dynamic Surveys, Electronic Journal
of Combinatorics, (2011)", DOI `10.37236/21`. That survey is *dynamic*: the
DOI resolves to whatever the current revision is, which today is **DS1
revision #18, 24 April 2026**. Six of T6's ten values — every upper bound from
$R(5,5)$ to $R(10,10)$ — are the numbers that survey carried before 2019, and
all six are now superseded in it. The table looks correctly sourced. A reader
who follows the reference to check lands on the current revision, sees
different numbers, and has nothing on the page to tell them which of the two
is out of date or by how long.

The same shape holds for every live source a contributor is likely to reach
for: OEIS b-files, the LMFDB, MathWorld, a Wikipedia table of bounds. All of
them are cited by an address that means "the newest version", and a table
transcribed from one of them silently stops agreeing with its own citation.

What the skill says now: §4 tells you what `References` holds — "`bib`, and
the identifiers beside it: `arxiv`, `doi`, `zbl`, `mr`" — and §2 says "Link
outward only to sources that will still exist: Wikipedia, LMFDB, OEIS,
MathWorld, mpmath, or a paper". Both are about the source *existing*. Neither
is about the source *changing*, and the list of recommended sources is almost
exactly the list of sources that do.

What it should say: a source that updates in place is cited by its version —
the revision number and date for a dynamic survey, the access date for a
Wikipedia table or an LMFDB query, the b-file's own date for OEIS. Write it
into the `bib` text, where a reader sees it beside the numbers. For a table
whose values are transcribed rather than computed, that line is doing the job
`generate.py` does elsewhere: it is the only way a later reader can tell
whether the table still agrees with where it came from.

Evidence: 2026-09-21. `curl https://numberdb.org/api/table?id=T6` gives
`"bib": "Stanisław Radziszowski, \"Small Ramsey Numbers\", Dynamic Surveys,
Electronic Journal of Combinatorics, (2011)."` and
`"Numbers": {..., "7": "[205, 540]", "10": "[798, 23556]"}`.
`https://www.combinatorics.org/ojs/index.php/eljc/article/view/DS1` says
"This Version DS1: Apr 24, 2026"; its Table Ib gives $R(7,7)\leq 492$ and
$R(10,10)\leq 16064$. Wikipedia's *Ramsey's theorem*, the table's other
declared source, gives `205–492` and `798–16064` too. Full working in
`agents/critiques/T6-growth.md` §4.

## `rigour: exact` describes the endpoints, not the number, when the entries are bounds

What happened: T6 is `type: Z`, `rigour: exact`, and six of its ten entries
are intervals like `[43, 48]` — not measurements with an error bar but the gap
between the best lower bound and the best upper bound anybody has proved.
The page reads:

> How well the digits are known: exact
>
> How they were obtained: An exact value -- an integer, a rational or a
> polynomial -- so there is no precision to choose and nothing to be wrong
> about.

There was a great deal to be wrong about: all six upper ends are stale, and
$R(10,10)$ is pinned only to within a factor of 20. `exact` is true of 43 and
48 and false of $R(5,5)$.

The corpus already contains the sentence that is true. T5 holds the single
entry `[2, 2.3728596]` and says: "Not a computed value: the entry is an
interval between two theorems, the trivial lower bound of 2 and the best upper
bound published when the entry was written. The true exponent is not known,
and the width of the interval is ignorance about mathematics rather than error
in a computation."

What the skill says now: §7's table gives `exact` as "an integer, rational or
polynomial. No precision to choose." and `measured` as "not computed at all —
an experimental value. Not on the scale." Neither covers an interval between
two theorems, which is a third thing: the endpoints are exact, the value is
unknown, and the width is nobody's error.

What it should say: where an entry is a bound rather than a value, `rigour`
describes the endpoints and `rigour details` has to say so — that the entry is
an interval between two published results, which ones, and that its width is
ignorance rather than error. The stock "exact, so there is nothing to be wrong
about" sentence is the one thing that must not be there, because the thing
that can be wrong is which theorem is the best one. Two tables here hold such
entries (T5, T6) and only one says it.

Evidence: 2026-09-21, `curl https://numberdb.org/api/table?id=T5` and
`...?id=T6`, quoted above. `/revisions/T6` shows the sentence arriving in a
migration on 2026-08-14, written from `rigour: exact` and `type: Z` without
reference to the entries.

## An entry wider than $10^{-5}$ relative is not searchable, and that decides whether a bounds table earns rows

What happened: reading T6 rendered rather than stored, six of its ten rows
carry a dagger:

    <a class="not-findable-mark" href="/help#search-precision"
       title="This number will not be found in the search. Click for why.">&dagger;</a>

`/help#search-precision` gives the rule: "The cutoff is a relative precision
of 1e-05: a value counts as identifiable when it is known to roughly that
fraction of its own size, which is about five significant digits." It names
T6's $R(5,5)\in[43,48]$ as one of its two worked examples. I confirmed it live:
`q=43` returns 529 matches and T6 is not among them, while `q=18`, `q=6` and
`q=2` — the exact rows — all reach it.

This changes the answer to a design question, not just a display one. I was
asked whether T6 should grow from $n=10$ to $n=23$, where lower bounds are
published. Every such row would be dagger-marked by construction, because the
gap between a Paley-graph lower bound and any available upper bound runs from
57-fold to eight orders of magnitude. Thirteen rows, none of which can ever
answer the question the database exists to answer. That is decisive, and it is
knowable before computing anything — but only if you know the cutoff.

What the skill says now: nothing. §5 explains that a real is stored as an
interval and that "`3.14` *is* an interval, so a table never has to say
separately how far to trust it"; §3 and §7 are about how many digits to keep
and how well they are known. The number at which the site stops indexing a
value is not in the skill at all, and it is the one number a contributor
planning a table of bounds needs first.

What it should say: state the cutoff — a value whose interval is wider than
$10^{-5}$ of its own size is excluded from search by number and marked with a
dagger on the page — and draw the consequence for ranges. A table of an open
problem's bounds is reached by name and by tag and not by digits, so its rows
have to earn their place by being read, which is an argument for few of them
and for keeping them current, not for extending the index as far as somebody
has published a one-sided bound.

Evidence: 2026-09-21. `curl https://numberdb.org/T6` for the dagger markup;
`curl https://numberdb.org/help` for the cutoff; four searches against
`https://numberdb.org/?q=...`. Full working in
`agents/critiques/T6-growth.md` §3.
