## Two Sage objects that named imports cannot build, and what to import first

What happened: screening a batch of matroid invariants, I tried the two
entry points a builder would reach for and both failed three frames away from
what I had called. Under `import numberdb.sage as numberdb` and named imports,

* `Matroid(groundset=..., rank=...)` raises `AttributeError: module
  'sage.modules' has no attribute 'with_basis'`, from a line inside
  `sage/matroids/constructor.py` testing whether the argument is a
  `ModuleMorphism`. `import sage.modules.with_basis.morphism` fixes it, but
  only if `numberdb.sage` has been imported first: on its own that import
  fails with `ImportError: cannot import name Category`.
* `M.chow_ring(QQ)` raises `ImportError: cannot import name
  PolynomialSequence_generic`, which is a cycle: `multi_polynomial_sequence`
  imports `plural`, which imports `multi_polynomial_libsingular`, which
  imports `sage.libs.singular.function`, which imports the first one back.
  Importing `sage.rings.polynomial.multi_polynomial_sequence` directly hits
  the same cycle. Importing
  `sage.rings.polynomial.multi_polynomial_libsingular` **first**, then
  `multi_polynomial_sequence`, breaks it, and `M.chow_ring(QQ,
  augmented=True).defining_ideal().hilbert_series()` then answers in
  milliseconds.

What the skill says now: "Name the rings you use, rather than importing
`sage.all`", and lists what named imports do not bring: root finding,
`RealBall.str`, `QQ('1.25')`.

What it should say: add that the failure is not always a missing name. Two
shapes recur, and the traceback points at neither call site: a lazily imported
attribute of a package that nothing has touched yet (`sage.modules.with_basis`),
and a genuine import cycle in the libsingular stack. The fix in both cases is
an explicit import in the right order, before the object is constructed, and
the order is not the one the error names. The general rule: when the error is
`cannot import name X` and `X` is a Sage class rather than something you wrote,
import the module that *defines* `X`'s neighbours before the one that raised.

Evidence: 2026-09-23. `/tmp/probe_matroid.py` (both tracebacks, in that order),
`/tmp/probe_chow.py` (`PolynomialSequence_generic` from `chow_ring`),
`/tmp/probe_chow3.py` (libsingular first: `B3 aug=False t^2 + 4*t + 1` in
4.3 s, everything after it under a second).

## `elliptic_k` and `elliptic_e` are on `ComplexBall`, not `RealBall`, and they take the parameter

What happened: bisecting $K(k)-2E(k)$ to locate the elliptic modulus in
Magnus's formula for the one-ninth constant, `RealBallField(400)(x).elliptic_k()`
raised `AttributeError: 'sage.rings.real_arb.RealBall' object has no attribute
'elliptic_k'`. `ComplexBallField` has `elliptic_k`, `elliptic_e`,
`elliptic_f`, `elliptic_pi`, the incomplete forms and the Weierstrass family;
`RealBallField` has none of them. Take `.real()` of the answer for a real
argument, as the skill's own `elliptic_k` example already does.

Second half, and the one that would have made the answer wrong rather than
absent: arb's `elliptic_k` takes the **parameter** $m$, not the modulus $k$,
with $m=k^2$ — the same trap the skill records for `elliptic_k(m)` in Sage.
Bisecting $K(m)=2E(m)$ gives $m=0.82611476598497036$, and it is its square root
$0.90890855754854138$ that is the published constant (OEIS A086199). A build
that reported the root of its own bisection would have published a number that
is off in the first decimal and looks perfectly plausible.

What the skill says now: "`elliptic_k` takes the parameter *m*, not the
modulus *k*; the two differ from the second entry onwards", and lists
`elliptic_k` among what arb implements — in a `ComplexBallField` example,
without saying that the real ball field lacks it.

What it should say: say it outright, beside the `is_finite` note that already
distinguishes the two ball types: the elliptic functions live on
`ComplexBall`. And when a source defines a constant by an equation in $K$ and
$E$, decide whether its unknown is $k$ or $m$ *before* solving, and check the
answer against the published digits of the unknown rather than of the constant.

Evidence: 2026-09-23. `/tmp/probe_approx2.py` (the `AttributeError`),
`/tmp/probe_ell.py` (both roots, and A086199 beside them).

## A defining series is not a way to compute a constant: check its rate before you use it

What happened: the Favard constants are defined by
$K_r=\frac4\pi\sum_{k\ge0}[(-1)^k/(2k+1)]^{r+1}$, and I summed it as written to
see the first few values. For even $r$ the series alternates and converges
usefully. For odd $r$ it does not alternate at all — it is
$\sum(2k+1)^{-(r+1)}$ — and **4000 terms gave $K_1=1.5707167$ against
$\pi/2=1.5707963$: four correct digits.** A table wanting 100 of them would
need about $10^{50}$ terms. Written through the Dirichlet lambda and beta
functions instead, $K_r=\frac4\pi\lambda(r+1)$ for odd $r$ and
$\frac4\pi\beta(r+1)$ for even $r$, with $\lambda(s)=(1-2^{-s})\zeta(s)$ and
$\beta(s)=4^{-s}(\zeta(s,\frac14)-\zeta(s,\frac34))$, the same values came out
to within $1.6\times10^{-120}$ of $\pi/2$, $\pi^2/8$ and $5\pi^4/384$ at 400
bits, in no measurable time.

The sign that something was wrong was not the digits, which looked fine: it was
that the check failed by $8\times10^{-5}$ while the ball claimed far better.
A ball built by adding 4000 terms carries only the rounding error of the
addition; the truncation error is mine to bound, and a series summed to a fixed
term count with no tail bound is a fixed-precision result wearing a ball.

What the skill says now: "Prefer balls for anything transcendental: arb carries
the error through every step, so the digits written are the digits the result
supports."

What it should say: arb carries the error of the steps you take, and a
truncated series has an error you did not take a step for. Either add the tail
as an explicit `add_error`, or — better — rewrite the definition in terms of
functions arb evaluates with its own error bound (`zeta`, Hurwitz `zeta(s,a)`,
`gamma`, the elliptic integrals). A defining formula is a definition, not an
algorithm, and how fast it converges is a fact to measure before quoting a
digit count.

Evidence: 2026-09-23. `/tmp/probe_approx.py` (raw series: `r=1
K=1.5707167493237651`), `/tmp/probe_approx2.py` (through $\zeta$ and $\beta$:
`check K_1 - pi/2 = -1.5490368e-120`).

## The OEIS b-file is the check; the sequence page is not

What happened: checking four constants against OEIS, I wanted the digits and
reached for the sequence pages. The page and the search are HTML meant for a
reader; the thing that actually holds the digits, one per line and nothing
else, is the b-file at `https://oeis.org/AXXXXXX/bXXXXXX.txt`. For a
decimal-expansion sequence it is exactly a digit check, it needs no parsing
beyond splitting two columns, and OEIS synthesises one even where the entry
has no uploaded b-file (its first line says so). A072558 and A073007 came back
as 60 digits each, and multiplying them gave 1 to every digit printed, which
checked two published constants against each other before either was written
down.

What the skill says now: OEIS is named as a link target, and one lesson
already says to search OEIS for a polynomial family's coefficient triangle.
Nothing says how to read the numbers out.

What it should say: when the check you want is "do these digits agree", fetch
the b-file, not the page. It is plain text, it is stable, and it is the only
form in which OEIS hands over more digits than the entry's `%S` line shows.

Evidence: 2026-09-23, four fetches: A073001 (Bernstein's constant,
`28016949902386913303643649123067200004248213981236`), A072558, A073007,
A086199.
