## A family with no arb implementation is not a family you have to drop

What happened: the whole batch is special functions arb has never heard of.
The obvious conclusion -- that a table cannot reach 100 digits with `rigour:
proven` machinery and should be abandoned -- is wrong twice over. The
eigenvalues of Mathieu's equation are the eigenvalues of an explicit real
symmetric tridiagonal matrix obtained from the Fourier-coefficient recurrences
(DLMF 28.4), and truncating it converges super-exponentially. At 130 decimal
digits of working precision, 60 rows carried 130 correct digits out to
$q = 50$, in 0.7 seconds:

    q      size 45              size 60              size 80
    1      identical to 130     agrees to 130        agrees to 130
    10     agrees to 105        identical to 130     agrees to 130
    25     agrees to  85        identical to 130     agrees to 130
    50     agrees to  70        agrees to 130        identical

The second half is the check. `scipy.special.mathieu_a` and `mathieu_b` exist
at double precision and are an independent implementation by other people:
40 tested $(n,q)$ pairs agreed to at worst $4\cdot10^{-15}$.

What the skill says now: section 5 lists what `value()` may return and says
"prefer balls for anything transcendental", and section 6's example is an arb
computation. Nothing says what to do when arb has no such function.

What it should say: when the quantity is an eigenvalue of a Sturm--Liouville
problem and no interval implementation exists, expand the eigenfunction in the
natural basis, take the resulting symmetric tridiagonal, and truncate --
convergence is usually super-exponential, so the size is small and measurable.
That alone is `heuristic`: the truncations are monotone one-sided bounds by
Cauchy interlacing, not an enclosure. Kato--Temple, using the computed
eigenvector's residual and the gap to the neighbouring eigenvalue, turns it
into a two-sided one and is the affordable route to `proven`. Say which of the
two you did in `rigour details`, with the measured truncation size.

Evidence: `/tmp/nb/check2.py` and `/tmp/nb/check3.py` under `agents/sage.sh`,
2026-09-21; "disagreements: 0" and "failures: 0".

## The Sage image carries scipy, numpy and mpmath, and that is a source of checks

What happened: the checks above were only possible because
`import scipy, numpy, mpmath` all succeed inside `agents/sage.sh` -- scipy
1.17.1, numpy 2.4.4, mpmath 1.4.1. `scipy.special` holds `mathieu_a`,
`mathieu_b`, `pro_cv` and `obl_cv`; none of them is in Sage's own library and
none is in arb. The Slepian trace identity was checked with a 400-node
`numpy.polynomial.legendre.leggauss` Nystr&ouml;m discretisation in about a
second.

What the skill says now: section 6 says to name the Sage rings you import
rather than importing `sage.all`, and lists what the named imports do not
bring. It does not mention that a SageMath installation is also a scipy and
numpy installation.

What it should say: a double-precision implementation by somebody else is one
of the four kinds of check the skill asks for, and for special functions
scipy usually has one where arb does not. It ships with SageMath, so it costs
nothing to reach. Check against it before reaching for a printed table.

Evidence: same scripts; `scipy: 1.17.1 | mpmath: 1.4.1 | numpy: 2.4.4`.

## Free checks that constrain a whole table at once are worth more than per-entry ones

What happened: three checks were run, and they are not equally strong. Two are
per-entry (scipy agreement, a published value). Three constrain the family:

* interlacing, $a_0 < b_1 < a_1 < b_2 < \cdots$, which holds across *two*
  tables and was confirmed at $q = 0.5, 1, 5, 10, 25$ over seven orders;
* the reflection $a_{2m}(-q) = a_{2m}(q)$, $a_{2m+1}(-q) = b_{2m+1}(q)$, exact
  to 40 digits, which is both a check and the reason $q < 0$ needs no rows;
* the trace identity $\sum_n \lambda_n(c) = 2c/\pi$ for the Slepian
  eigenvalues, which held to $2\cdot10^{-16}$ and pins the entire spectrum
  with one number.

The trace identity shares no code with a per-eigenvalue computation, which is
what makes it worth more than a hundred agreeing entries.

What the skill says now: "What a good check looks like" lists four kinds, all
of them per-value (a specialisation, a published value, an identity, a brute
count).

What it should say: prefer a check that constrains the whole table at once --
a trace, a sum rule, an interlacing between this table and its companion, a
symmetry that maps the family to itself. It catches a systematic error that
per-entry agreement with one other implementation cannot, and it is usually
one line.

Evidence: `/tmp/nb/check3.py`, "interlaces" at five values of $q$, all
reflection differences exactly `0.0`; `/tmp/nb/check2.py`, trace gaps
$\le 2\cdot10^{-16}$ at $c = 1, 2, 5, 10$.

## When the screen says the source does not name your family, suspect the title

What happened: `source_names_it` rejected two proposals that were perfectly
real.

    source_names_it("Prolate and oblate spheroidal eigenvalues",
                    "https://dlmf.nist.gov/30.3")
    -> the source does not mention prolate, oblate

DLMF &sect;30.3 is exactly the right page: it defines
$\lambda^m_n(\gamma^2)$. It carries the prolate/oblate distinction in the
*sign of $\gamma^2$* and never writes either word. MathWorld does, and passes.

    source_names_it("Lamé characteristic values",
                    "https://dlmf.nist.gov/29.3")
    -> the source does not mention characteristic

Right page again; DLMF calls them *eigenvalues*, not characteristic values.
"Lam&eacute; eigenvalues" passes on the same URL.

What the skill says now: the screening step is described in the ideation
prompt rather than the skill, and both read the check as "is this family
real".

What it should say: the check is on the words of your *title*, so a failure
usually means the title uses words the literature does not -- which matters far
beyond the screen, because the title is the only field a reader's search
reliably reaches. Treat a `source_names_it` complaint as a first draft of the
question "what would somebody holding one of these numbers type?" rather than
as a verdict on the family.

Evidence: `/tmp/screen2.py`, 2026-09-21; the four calls above, two failing and
two passing on the same pages.

## mpmath's `eigsy` is per-matrix, and a naive loop pays for it per value

What happened: the first version of the check called a function
`mathieu_a(n, q)` that built and diagonalised a $120\times120$ matrix at 60
decimal digits for every single $(n, q)$ -- about fifty eigendecompositions
where four per $q$ were needed, because one spectrum holds every order. It ran
for over two minutes before printing anything. Caching the spectrum on
`(recurrence, q, size, precision)` brought the same 40 values to under a
second each.

What the skill says now: nothing about the cost of the generator itself.

What it should say: when one computation yields every entry of a row or column
of the table -- a whole spectrum, a whole set of roots, a whole expansion --
cache on the computation, not on the entry. `Generator.value()` is called once
per entry and the obvious implementation recomputes the shared work each time.

Evidence: two runs of the same mathematics, `/tmp/nb/check.py` (over 120s
before first output) against `/tmp/nb/check2.py` (complete in seconds), under
`agents/sage.sh`, 2026-09-21.
