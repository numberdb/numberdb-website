# Lessons proposed by the T369 critique, 2026-09-20

Two things the skill did not warn about, both reachable by somebody with a
laptop and Sage writing a companion to a table that already exists.

## A `Similar tables` relation is a claim about the *other* table's range

What happened: T369 (zeros of the Krawtchouk polynomials) says of its
companion, which holds the polynomials themselves, "stores the polynomials
whose zeros are listed here, on the same $p$, $N$ and $n$ grid". The two
grids agree in $p$ and $N$ and disagree in $n$: the companion holds
$0\leq n\leq N$, T369 holds $n\leq\min(N,4)$, so for $N=16$ one has seventeen
degrees and the other four. T369's own `complete-note` says so, two sections
away from the sentence that denies it. A reader who arrives at the polynomial
table at $N=16$, $n=9$ and clicks through for its zeros is told by the
relation that they are here.

Nothing catches this. `audit_table` resolves the slug and stops; the relation
is prose, and the fact it asserts lives in a different table's `Data
properties`. It also decays without anybody touching the sentence: either
table can be extended, and the claim silently stops being true.

What the skill says now: `Similar tables` "states the *relation*, in words",
and "that sentence is the valuable part". Nothing about the sentence being
checkable, or about what it is checkable against.

What it should say: a relation that mentions range — "the same grid", "the
same discriminants", "these to the same degree" — is a claim about the other
table's completeness note, and is the one part of a cross-reference that goes
stale on its own. Read that note before writing the relation, and prefer a
relation that names what the two tables have in common and where this one
stops: "at the same probabilities and support endpoints, and to every degree
$0\leq n\leq N$" says both, and stays true when either table grows.

Evidence: 2026-09-20, T369 critique.
`GET /api/table?id=Krawtchouk_polynomials_in_the_Askey_normalisation` gives
`complete-note: ... every support endpoint $1\leq N\leq16$ and every degree
$0\leq n\leq N$`; `GET /api/table?id=T369` gives
`$1\leq n\leq\min(N,4)$` and the relation quoted above. The audit answers
`{"findings": [], "clean": true}` on both.

## A table of zeros holds exact rows wherever a symmetry forces one, and `type: R` hides them

What happened: T369 stores 700 zeros as 100-digit decimals, of which 124 are
exactly rational and 73 are the plain integers 1 to 12 and 15. They are not
digit coincidences. Two sub-families are forced by the construction:
$K_1(x;p,N)=1-x/(pN)$ has its one zero at $x=pN$, which is 80 of the rows;
and the reflection $K_n(N-x;\tfrac12,N)=(-1)^nK_n(x;\tfrac12,N)$ makes $N/2$ a
zero for every odd $n$, so the $p=1/2$, $n=3$ column is
$1.5, 2, 2.5, \ldots, 8$ written to a hundred places each. The reflection is
printed in the table's own `Formulas` section; nobody read it as a statement
about which rows are exact.

The mechanism is that `type` is decided once, for the table, and the rows
inherit it. A generator that isolates roots in an interval field returns
intervals for every row, including the rows whose value is $3$, and nothing
in the workflow asks again. `RealIntervalField` will happily hand back a
zero-width interval at $3$ and the client writes it out to the table's
`digits`.

What the skill says now: "Decide exactness from the definition, never from
the digits... Where there is such an argument, write the integer or the
rational — a decimal means plus or minus one unit in its last place however
long it is." That is written about a table's quantity as a whole (the
logistic map's $r=3$), not about a minority of rows inside an approximate
table, and the reader takes it as advice about choosing `type`.

What it should say: an `R` table may hold exact rows, and a table of zeros
usually does. Before publishing, ask which rows are forced: the degree-one
member of any family, a polynomial odd about the centre of its support, any
quadratic whose discriminant is square. The symmetries are the ones already
written in `Formulas`. Write those values as `3` or `5/2` rather than as a
hundred places of them — the parser reads an exact rational before it tries
the decimal forms, and the column prints it as written — and say in a formula
or a comment why they are exact, since that is the sentence the reader who
holds one came for.

Evidence: 2026-09-20, T369 critique. Of the 700 stored values, 124 have a
terminating decimal expansion and 73 are integers; all 80 $n=1$ rows agree
with $pN$ to the last stored place, and $K_2(x;\tfrac14,5)=(4x^2-12x+5)/5$
has zeros $1/2$ and $5/2$, both stored as `0.5000...` and `2.5000...` to a
hundred places. Rendering `3/4`, `1/2`, `5/2` and `3` through
`/preview?table=` in that table's shape prints them unchanged in the $x_k$
column; `parse_real_interval` (`utils/utils.py:79`) tries
`parse_rational_number` first.
