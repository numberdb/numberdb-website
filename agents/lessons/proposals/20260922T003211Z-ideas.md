## An integrality theorem is a truncation detector, and reads like a discovery instead

What happened: the genus-zero Gopakumar-Vafa invariants of a Calabi-Yau
threefold are integers, and computing them means composing about six power
series operations -- exp, series division, reversion, a cube, a divisor-sum
recursion. Truncating at $N$ coefficients, the run reported:

    quintic in P^4    integral: FAILS at [13]
    sextic            integral: FAILS at [13]
    octic             integral: FAILS at [13]
    ... all eight families, at exactly index 13, with N = 14

Every earlier value was an integer. The natural reading of "the eighth family
also fails at 13" is that the mathematics runs out; the actual cause is that
one step consumes an order -- forming $q\,z'(q)/z$ from a series known to
order $N-1$ leaves it known only to $N-2$ -- so the last value is arithmetic
on a coefficient that was never there. Running at $N = 14$ and $N = 20$ and
comparing, the values agree for $d \le 12$ and differ at exactly $d = 13$,
which is $N-1$ for the shorter run.

The general shape: when a family has an integrality (or positivity, or
alternating-sign) theorem, the *first* index at which it fails is almost never
a mathematical fact about that index. It is the order at which the
computation's precision ran out, and it will be the same index for every
member of the family, because the loss is in the code and not in the object.
Two independent truncations agreeing on a value is the cheap test, and it is
the skill's own `heuristic (agreement-checked)` discipline -- "computed at two
or more precisions, keeping the digits they agree on" -- applied to the order
of a series rather than to a number of digits. The difference is that here the
agreed values are then *exact*, so the table is `exact` and not heuristic;
what the two runs bought was the range, not the rigour.

What the skill says now: section 6 is about exactness in a generator
(`QQ(a)/QQ(b)`, `factorial(n)/k` being float division) and section 7 about
ball arithmetic. Neither covers a truncated power series, where the
coefficients are exact rationals and the *series* is the approximation.

What it should say: a value computed from a truncated power series is exact
only up to some order below the truncation, and how far below depends on the
operations -- a shift or a division by the series' own variable costs one.
Compute at two truncation orders and keep only the coefficients that agree.
Where the family has an integrality theorem, use it: a run of integers ending
in a rational tells you exactly where your series ran out.

Evidence: 2026-09-22, plain Python 3 with `fractions.Fraction` on the runner,
`N = 14` against `N = 20`, eight Calabi-Yau families; agreement for $d \le 12$,
disagreement at $d = 13$ in every family.

## OEIS through `urllib` works where `curl` gets a 403, and `A.../list` is the cheapest form

What happened: checking a computed integer family against OEIS is the best
check the prompt asks for, and the obvious command fails:

    curl -s "https://oeis.org/search?q=2875,609250,317206375&fmt=json"
    HTTP/2 403   <title>Just a moment...</title>

Cloudflare, not a rate limit; it does not clear by waiting. The same URL
through `urllib.request` with an ordinary `User-Agent` returns the data. Two
forms are useful and neither needs a key:

    https://oeis.org/search?q=1,1,12,620,87304&fmt=text    -> %I / %N lines
    https://oeis.org/A013587/list                          -> a python list

`.../list` is the one to parse when you already know the A-number: it is a
bracketed list of terms, which a regex turns into `[int]` in one line, and it
carries more terms than the `%S %T %U` lines of the text format.

What the skill says now: OEIS is named as a place to link and as a place to
check a specialisation, with no note on how to reach it from a program.

What it should say: reach OEIS with `urllib` rather than `curl`, and use
`https://oeis.org/<Anumber>/list` for terms. A search returning nothing is
evidence, so it is worth the two lines -- three of the eight families in this
batch turned out to be in no OEIS sequence at all, which was an argument for
the table.

Evidence: 2026-09-22, the runner, comparing eight computed families against
A060041, A076912, A013587, A090005, A090006, A090007, A090008.

## A published sequence can be wrong, and the growth ratios say which side is

What happened: the computed invariants of $X_{2,2,2,2}(1^8)$ agreed with OEIS
A090007 on nine of its ten terms and disagreed at $d = 6$:

    mine     170535923200
    A090007 1705359223200

One digit too many, with the neighbours at $d = 5, 7, 8$ agreeing exactly.
The way to decide without a third source is the growth: $n_{d+1}/n_d$ runs
19, 43, 62, 76, 87, 96, 102 with the computed value, and 19, 43, 62, 76,
**871, 9.6**, 102 with the published one. A smooth sequence with one term two
orders out and its successor correspondingly down is a transcription slip, not
mathematics.

What the skill says now: "Check new values against something independent",
and treats the independent source as the arbiter.

What it should say: when a computation and a published table disagree at one
entry and agree everywhere around it, the published table is a candidate for
being wrong, and the disagreement is worth recording in the table's own entry
comment rather than being silently resolved either way. A smoothness or
growth-ratio argument decides it cheaply; two independent sources decide it
properly. This is also the case that makes a second database useful, so it is
worth saying out loud rather than treating a mismatch as the contributor's
error by default.

Evidence: 2026-09-22, A090007 term 6 against the Picard-Fuchs computation of
`agents/table-ideas/BATCH-2026-09-22T0032.md`.

## `source_names_it` matches whole words, and a throttled page fails on all of them

What happened: two of the screen's complaints in this run looked like the
family being misnamed and were not.

    "Welschinger invariants of the real projective plane"
    https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0303378
        the source does not mention invariants

The paper is *Welschinger invariant and enumeration of real rational curves*:
singular. Dropping the plural passes. The screen splits on word boundaries and
does not stem, so `invariants` does not match `invariant`, and a family whose
canonical paper title is singular fails against its own source.

    "local Gromov-Witten invariants"
    https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0607121
        the source does not mention local, gromov, witten, invariants

Every word missing, including ones any mathematics page contains. That is the
tell for a page that came back as a block or a holding page rather than as the
abstract -- arXiv does this under load -- and it is a different failure from
"one or two words missing", which really does mean the name is wrong. The two
read identically in a proposal that quotes only the verdict.

What the skill says now: nothing; `source_names_it` is described in the
stage-one prompt rather than the skill, but the distinction is about reading
any automated source check.

What it should say: when a source check reports that *every* distinguishing
word is missing, suspect the fetch, not the name -- refetch or cite a
different page. When it reports one word missing, try the singular and the
hyphen-free spelling before concluding the family is misnamed.

Evidence: 2026-09-22, `agents/table-ideas/screen.py`, against
`arxiv.org/abs/math/0303378`, `math/0607121`, `math/0610901`, `1301.5695`;
the three that failed on every word all failed together, and Wikipedia and
OEIS pages fetched in the same minutes were fine.

## A search result carries the T-number, so surveying a subject costs a dozen requests

What happened: surveying what the corpus already holds by fetching
`numberdb.table('T1')` through `'T390'` downloads each table *whole* -- a
hundred kilobytes and up, because `Numbers` is every entry -- and stops with

    RateLimitError: too many requests; retry in 749s

after about 320 of them, with a key configured. (The free route round that
limit, `/bundle/Tn`, is already proposed as a lesson in
`agents/lessons/proposals/20260921T201824Z-critique.md`; this is the other
half.) The walk is tempting because the skill says there is no call that lists
the corpus, and a proposer wants exactly that list.

But the survey a proposer actually needs is "what does the corpus hold near
*this subject*", and `search_text` answers it in one request per word --
`Gromov`, `instanton`, `Calabi`, `quintic`, `mirror`, `Welschinger`,
`enumerative`, `threefold` cost eight between them here and answered better
than the walk, because they reach tables above the T-number where it stopped.
The result carries the T-number, which is not obvious: `Table` has
`__slots__ = ('tid', 'title', 'url', 'number_count')`, so

    [(t.tid, t.title) for t in numberdb.search_text('Chern classes').tables]
    [('T335', 'Chern character polynomials ...'), ('T332', 'Chern classes of
      smooth complete intersections ...'), ('T338', 'Segre classes ...'), ...]

gives the neighbours and their addresses together, and `t.url` gives the slug
that `HREF{}` wants -- which the skill says to read off a search result rather
than guess, without saying that `tid` is there beside it.

What the skill says now: "There is no call that lists the corpus -- to see
everything you walk the T-numbers", and separately that the slug comes from
`search_text(...).tables[0].url`.

What it should say: the walk downloads every entry of every table and will
meet the rate limit, so it is for reading a few neighbours, not for surveying;
survey a subject with `search_text`, whose results carry `tid`, `title` and
`url` together.

Evidence: 2026-09-22, `clients/python`, a key configured, T1-T318 fetched
before the limit; the message names the wait in seconds and it is real.
